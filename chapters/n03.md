# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter III of X · Sermons 7-9 · Nanananda, with Analayo's comparative scholarship

## How does consciousness construct the world I experience?

**The answer\:** Consciousness does not simply reveal a world to an independent observer. In conjunction with the senses, perception, attention, intention, feeling and craving, it participates in the arising of the world *as experienced*\.

But here's the trap.

The mind then treats the results of this conditioned process as independently existing things, treats the apparent observer as their owner, and starts organising its existence around acquiring or escaping them.

**It mistakes what is dependently established for what exists in its own right.**

Nanananda's extraordinary proposal is that liberation does not require destroying the world. It requires seeing through the ignorance that makes this whole arrangement compelling.

And this chapter confronts a question that could overturn everything we've established.

If consciousness participates in constructing experience, then what the heck is consciousness when the construction ceases?

Does it vanish?

Does it survive as pure awareness?

Does it become luminous and infinite?

Or are we trying to smuggle an eternal self into Buddhism through the back door, wearing a very expensive nondual robe?

Welcome to Sermon 7.


## Part I - Sermon 7: The consciousness that doesn't show anything

### 1\. The monk who tried to find the edge of reality

We're going to begin with a Buddhist story so absurd that it almost sounds written to mock metaphysicians.

A monk develops extraordinary psychic abilities.

He wants to know where the four fundamental material elements - earth, water, fire and air - cease without remainder.

A reasonable philosophical question.

So he goes looking for the answer.

Not by investigating dependent arising, mind you.

He goes to heaven.

There are gods there.

Surely gods understand reality.

He asks them.

They don't know.

They send him to more exalted gods.

Those gods don't know either.

Up the celestial management hierarchy he goes.

Department after department.

Cosmic administrators.

Divine executives.

Increasingly magnificent beings surrounded by increasingly impressive quantities of light.

And not one of these sods can answer the question.

Eventually he reaches Great Brahma.

Now this should surely settle matters.

The grand cosmic being. The enormous metaphysical cheese. The fellow one might expect to possess an executive overview of existence.

The monk asks his question.

And Brahma essentially begins announcing his credentials.

He is powerful.

He is glorious.

He is the Great Brahma.

Wonderful.

**But where do the four elements cease?**

More declarations of divine majesty.

The cosmic equivalent of asking a CEO how the company makes money and receiving a fifteen-minute speech about his leadership qualities.

Eventually Brahma privately admits he doesn't know and tells the monk to return to the Buddha.

And that's exactly what happens.

The monk returns.

The Buddha doesn't give him another celestial destination.

He corrects the question.

This is the crucial movement in the *Kevaddha Sutta*, DN 11, which Nanananda takes up at the beginning of Sermon 7.

### 2\. Why the question was already stuffed

Consider what the monk is assuming.

He asks where the material elements cease.

Implicitly, he's imagining that the elements possess some kind of cosmic range or territory.

Perhaps if he travels far enough, he can discover a location where they no longer exist.

A realm beyond matter.

A purely immaterial dimension.

A transcendent destination.

And what happens if he finds it?

Presumably he will have discovered the place where material existence ends.

But notice what he has done.

He has turned cessation into a geographical problem.

He assumes that something which arises dependently must cease at some special location.

**He's searching for a place where the conditions of experience stop applying, while using those very conditions to conduct his search.**

And the Buddha's correction is devastating.

The important question concerns where the elements, and the distinctions dependent upon them, fail to find a footing.

Nanananda interprets this as redirecting attention away from a mysterious cosmic location and towards the conditions under which our ordinary world becomes established.

So now we have a completely different question.

Not\:

"Where is the universe's secret exit?"

But\:

"How does experience become established as a world in the first place?"

Because if you understand its establishment, you might understand its cessation.

And that would be rather more useful than spending eternity asking increasingly luminous gods for directions.

### 3\. The most dangerous phrase in the entire series

The Buddha's answer contains an expression conventionally rendered as *non-manifestative consciousness*\.

It is also described, depending on the textual reading and translation, as boundless and luminous, or as having an unrestricted range.

Nanananda argues that this describes the released consciousness of an arahant: consciousness that no longer establishes name-and-form as an independently real object of appropriation.

Now this is precisely the point at which spiritual philosophy tends to lose its blimming mind.

Because somebody hears\:

"Consciousness. Infinite. Luminous."

And immediately announces\:

**"AT LAST\! THE ETERNAL COSMIC AWARENESS\! WE'VE FOUND IT\!"**

Out comes the metaphysical champagne.

Consciousness must be a boundless substance.

The universe must appear inside it.

Enlightenment must be recognising that you are this immortal luminous field.

And suddenly the Buddhist discourse has become an advertisement for an eternal witness.

But Nanananda is making a subtler argument.

He's asking what happens to the *manifestation of a world as an independently substantial reality* when ignorance and appropriation have ceased.

And that is not the same question as whether an eternal awareness-substance exists.

Let's examine why.

### 4\. The mirror that invents an owner

Remember the dog from the previous chapter.

It looks into water and sees another dog.

It growls.

The other dog growls.

It becomes furious.

Its entire experience is organised around responding to a threatening animal.

Yet the threatening animal is its reflection.

Nanananda now returns to this example and asks us to examine the significance of reflection itself.

Reflection can perpetuate delusion.

But reflection, properly directed, can also disclose delusion.

The difference is whether one understands the reflection as a reflection.

Now apply this to ordinary consciousness.

You experience a body.

You experience feelings.

Thoughts arise.

Memories appear.

Anxiety appears.

Desires arise.

And when you reflect upon this changing collection, you say\:

"Ah, yes. That's me."

Then something even stranger happens.

You begin treating that image as the owner of the very processes from which the image was constructed.

**You've mistaken the reflection for a separate being who possesses the mirror.**

Think about what this means.

A person has been anxious for several days.

They notice the anxiety.

Then announce\:

"I am an anxious person."

A changing process has now become a personal identity.

The identity enters memory.

Memory influences attention.

Attention selectively notices threatening situations.

Threatening situations reinforce the identity.

And the mind begins interpreting subsequent experience through the apparent existence of an anxious owner.

What began as a conditioned process has become a story about what somebody fundamentally *is*\.

Nanananda's concern is that consciousness becomes established upon name-and-form and subsequently treats the apparent structure as a self.

What the arahant no longer does is establish that delusive relation of appropriation.

The relevant teaching is not\:

"An awakened person has no memories, perceptions or ability to recognise objects."

Rather\:

**The awakened person no longer mistakes conditioned experience for an independently existing self and its possessions.**

That is the decisive distinction.

### 5\. But doesn't consciousness need an object?

Now we're approaching the real difficulty.

In the early discourses, ordinary consciousness arises dependent upon conditions.

Visual consciousness depends upon the visual sense and visible objects.

Auditory consciousness depends upon hearing and sounds.

And so forth.

So how can Nanananda speak of consciousness that no longer establishes name-and-form?

Wouldn't this contradict the very principle of dependent arising?

This is where we must distinguish two ideas.

First, consciousness can occur without being appropriated through craving.

An arahant who is alive can still see, hear and act.

Second, the full cessation associated with Nibbana must not be hastily equated with an everlasting consciousness existing independently of every condition.

Nanananda is especially interested in **unestablished consciousness**\: the ending of the grasping through which consciousness finds a footing in name-and-form.

His interpretation is ambitious and controversial.

The question is whether the difficult canonical passage describes a distinctive mode of liberated awareness, the cessation of conditioned consciousness, or something that cannot adequately be captured by either familiar alternative.

Those are competing interpretations, not three interchangeable ways of saying the same thing.

And we have a particularly useful piece of scholarship here.

### 6\. Analayo pulls the luminous rug out from under everybody

Analayo's comparative materials for Sermon 7 examine different versions of the passage.

And here is the discovery that makes the philosophical problem even more interesting.

**The famous description of consciousness as luminous may depend upon a textual reading that was not original.**

Analayo draws attention to Sanskrit, Tibetan and Chinese parallels, alongside the work of the Pali scholar K. R. Norman.

Some witnesses support readings associated with expansiveness or accessibility rather than luminosity.

Moreover, certain parallels place considerable emphasis on the **cessation of consciousness** itself.

His conclusion is that the luminous interpretation probably reflects a later textual development, although the evidence is not perfectly uniform.

Consider the consequences.

You were about to establish an elaborate metaphysics of eternal luminous consciousness.

You'd bought the incense.

You'd prepared the Sanskrit terminology.

You were mentally composing an essay titled *The Radiant Ground of All Being*\.

And Analayo comes along with several manuscript witnesses and says\:

"Actually, the reading may not have said that."

flipping magnificent.

This is why philological research matters, even though we're not doing language lessons in this audiobook.

A single disputed expression can affect an entire metaphysical interpretation.

To be precise, this does not prove that every doctrine of luminous awareness is false. Early Buddhist texts contain other passages concerning luminosity of mind.

It does mean that **this particular verse cannot safely serve as straightforward proof of an eternal luminous absolute**\.

And Nanananda himself need not be read as proposing one.

His distinctive concern is the ending of the deceptive establishment of consciousness upon name-and-form.

So let's preserve the philosophical insight while refusing to smuggle in conclusions the evidence cannot support.


### 7\. Why the liberated mind cannot be tracked

Nanananda develops another extraordinary consequence.

The Buddha is described in the *Dhammapada* as trackless, like a bird crossing the sky.

Think about the ordinary meaning of tracking something.

You follow its footprints.

Its movements leave traces.

You establish where it came from and where it is going.

But Nanananda isn't claiming that awakened monks become physically invisible.

He's asking what sort of *track* craving can follow.

Normally, consciousness becomes entangled with objects.

You desire something.

You keep returning to it.

Your attention settles upon it.

You elaborate its significance.

Your habits form around it.

The mind has left a recognisable trail.

An object of craving has become a place where consciousness repeatedly establishes itself.

But what happens when there is no craving establishing that relationship?

**What can possibly be followed when nothing is being compulsively taken up?**

Nanananda interprets the Buddha's immeasurability in this direction. The liberated one's freedom is not the ability to occupy an infinite amount of space or contemplate infinitely many things simultaneously. It is freedom from the limiting process through which craving becomes attached to objects.

He contrasts this interpretation with the commentarial tendency to explain the Buddha's infinite range through omniscience.

That's a fascinating reversal.

The ordinary mind imagines infinite consciousness as **consciousness possessing absolutely everything**\.

Nanananda is pointing towards consciousness that is no longer possessed by anything.

One interpretation is maximal acquisition.

The other is complete release.

Those are not the same achievement.

And the difference becomes wonderfully obvious when we return to meditation.

Suppose a profound state occurs.

Bliss.

Clarity.

Stillness.

An extraordinarily expansive sense of awareness.

The mind immediately announces\:

"THERE IT IS."

"I FOUND IT."

"THIS MUST BE THE INFINITE."

And the very next thought is\:

"How do I keep it?"

There is your track.

Craving has arrived, unpacked its suitcase, and started arranging the furniture.

The question is no longer whether the state was impressive.

It is whether your relationship to the state is free.

**An experience can be extraordinary without being liberating.**

And an experience can be entirely ordinary without requiring grasping.

### 8\. The cinema's final trick

Nanananda concludes the central argument of Sermon 7 by returning to the cinema.

Imagine the film is approaching its climax.

Every spectator is transfixed.

The projector supplies a narrow beam of light.

The room is dark.

And the entire fictional universe depends upon that arrangement.

Now somebody opens the doors.

Daylight floods the cinema.

The images become pale and indistinct.

The spectators can see the room.

They recognise the apparatus.

The drama loses its compelling hold.

And here is the subtle part.

**The film doesn't need to stop running for the illusion to lose its power.**

Nanananda uses this image to illustrate the way insight can overcome the conditioned consciousness that sustains delusive appearances.

He compares the restricted illumination of the screen with the wider illumination of wisdom.

The relevance of this simile does not depend entirely on accepting his interpretation of the disputed word *luminous*\.

Even if Analayo's preferred textual reconstruction is correct, the contemplative distinction remains valuable.

An appearance can continue without being mistaken for an independently existing reality that must be grasped.

And this allows us to formulate a careful answer to the original question.

Does the liberated person still experience anything?

While alive, yes.

But the experience is no longer organised through the same delusive appropriation.

What Nanananda describes as the cessation of *specifically constructed consciousness* must therefore be distinguished from the claim that a living arahant has permanently lost all sensory consciousness.

And this is also why we must resist identifying his teaching directly with Trika.

In Pratyabhijna, consciousness is ultimately self-revealing, independent and universally constitutive.

In Nanananda's Buddhist analysis, the central problem is the dependent arising of consciousness, name-and-form and the grasping through which they sustain becoming.

Both traditions examine the apparent subject-object division.

But a shared problem does not establish a shared metaphysical answer.

One can investigate the construction of the observer without concluding that an absolute Observer exists behind it.

And Nanananda is about to become even more ruthless about that distinction.


## Part II - Sermon 8: The universe is real enough to hurt you, but not what you think it is

### 9\. God himself has fallen for the trick

Sermon 8 opens with the Buddha's confrontation with Baka Brahma, a magnificent heavenly being who thinks his exalted existence is permanent.

Now consider the situation.

Here is an extraordinary being.

Its lifespan dwarfs an ordinary human life.

Its powers are immense.

Its realm is incomparably refined.

And it concludes\:

"Obviously, this must be eternal."

This is an extraordinarily human mistake.

You experience something that seems stable.

You cannot imagine its ending.

Therefore you assume it possesses a permanent essence.

Baka Brahma makes this mistake at a spectacularly sophisticated level.

He doesn't merely believe his house will last forever.

He believes his heavenly status does.

**He has mistaken an unusually long-lasting condition for an unconditioned reality.**

The Buddha's response, as Nanananda reads the *Brahmanimantanika Sutta*, is that the awakened mind does not become entangled with the supposed inherent natures of earth, water, fire, beings, gods, Brahma, or even the totality conceived as *everything*\.

The liberated person understands these designations without making them into an independent basis of identification.

This is a subtle point.

Nanananda isn't saying the material world vanishes because an arahant stops believing in it.

He's saying the worldling invests concepts with an inherent significance that exceeds their dependent character.

And then identifies with the resulting constructions.

Imagine becoming the most important person in your entire country.

Your name is known everywhere.

People bow when you enter.

Your opinions determine the behaviour of millions.

Surely you've finally become something substantial.

Then your position disappears.

Your influence diminishes.

Your body ages.

Your name begins to fade.

And the magnificent identity turns out to have depended on arrangements that were never yours to preserve indefinitely.

Baka Brahma is that mistake carried to cosmic proportions.

The fellow has become the CEO of an impermanent universe and believes the employment contract says *eternal*\.

### 10\. How a chair becomes a chair - and then becomes your chair

Nanananda introduces a wonderful example from theatre.

A piece of furniture appears on stage.

In one scene, it serves as a stool.

In another, the same object becomes a table.

In another, it might be used as a platform or a footrest.

The audience understands the changing significance without difficulty.

The object has been assigned a role within a particular arrangement.

But the ordinary mind tends to forget that concepts operate through conditions and relationships.

It treats the name of something as though the name disclosed an independent essence.

A chair becomes *a chair*\.

Then it becomes *my chair*\.

Then it becomes *the chair I deserve*\.

Then somebody else sits in it.

And now, apparently, we have a flipping constitutional crisis.

What happened?

The physical object remains.

But you've progressively invested it with identity, ownership and expectation.

Its significance has become part of the story of who you are.

Nanananda argues that craving, conceit and views contribute to this process of objectification.

And there is a crucial distinction here.

A concept can be **conventionally valid** without describing an independently existing essence.

Calling something a chair is useful.

You don't have to undertake a transcendental investigation before sitting down.

But the validity of the designation does not imply that the object possesses an immutable metaphysical chair-nature.

Still less does it establish an independent owner whose well-being is fundamentally secured by possessing it.

That's where ordinary conceptual recognition becomes bondage.

The mind doesn't merely identify the object.

It appropriates its significance.

And this is what Nanananda means when he insists that we must understand how things become *things* for us.

### 11\. The stone that becomes a treasure

He gives a particularly funny example.

A man is walking through the forest.

He kicks a stone.

It hurts.

He curses.

A little farther on, he kicks another stone.

But this time, he recognises that it's a precious gem.

Suddenly everything changes.

He carefully retrieves it.

Cleans it.

Wraps it.

Carries it away.

The first stone was an inconvenience.

The second was a miraculous blessing.

But notice what has changed in the man's experience.

The contact is similar.

The physical pain may be similar.

The second object's material properties and potential exchange value may genuinely differ.

Yet the emotional world that forms around each event is radically different.

One event becomes misfortune.

The other becomes extraordinary luck.

The man has discovered something valuable, and immediately his future begins reorganising around it.

Now imagine the next stage.

He starts worrying about thieves.

Perhaps he should hide the gem.

Perhaps somebody knows he has it.

Perhaps he should sell it.

Perhaps the price will rise.

Perhaps he should wait.

Perhaps another person deserves it less.

The object has become an entire psychological universe.

Its practical value may be real.

But the compulsive mental structure arising around it is something additional.

Nanananda wants us to understand precisely that addition.

**The world becomes a world of personal gains and losses through the meanings and attachments we construct around experience.**

And now the problem becomes wonderfully uncomfortable.

You cannot simply decide to stop perceiving things.

Nor would that be desirable.

A human being must recognise food, danger, people, shelter and a thousand other useful distinctions.

So how can consciousness continue recognising objects without turning those objects into fuel for becoming?


### 12\. The sewing machine that still moves but stops stitching

This might be Nanananda's most important practical image in Sermon 8.

Imagine a sewing machine.

The needle moves.

The cloth passes underneath.

The mechanism continues operating.

Everything looks normal.

Except the shuttle has run out of thread.

And so the stitches don't hold.

The fabric passes through the machine without being fastened into the expected structure.

**The movement continues. The binding does not.**

Nanananda uses this as a comparison for the liberated mind.

Sensory and mental processes can continue to occur.

But craving, conceit and views no longer supply the binding through which the experiential process produces renewed grasping and becoming.

Now this is beautiful.

Because ordinarily you think liberation requires switching off the machine.

Stop thinking.

Stop feeling.

Stop perceiving.

Stop having an identity.

Stop experiencing anything at all.

And Nanananda says, essentially\:

No. You're misunderstanding what the thread was doing.

The issue isn't simply the occurrence of conditioned phenomena.

It's the binding.

The stitching.

The construction of the entire possessive drama around their occurrence.

Imagine somebody insults you.

The sound reaches your ears.

You recognise the words.

You understand the insult.

The body may react.

But there is no compulsive identification requiring you to preserve an injured self-image through resentment.

The experience has been known.

The usual psychological stitching need not follow.

Or suppose somebody praises you.

You hear the praise.

You understand the compliment.

You may respond courteously.

But the mind doesn't immediately need to construct a magnificent personal identity that must now be maintained.

The praise doesn't become a new burden.

And this reveals an astonishing possibility.

**Freedom need not mean the abolition of experience. It can mean freedom from the grasping that transforms experience into bondage.**

But be careful.

An arahant's liberation is not merely a technique for remaining emotionally unreactive.

The Buddhist claim is stronger: the underlying defilements have been uprooted.

The thread is not being temporarily withheld.

The machinery that continually replenishes it has ceased functioning in the relevant way.

And this leads to an even more radical question.

If the stitching has stopped, what happens to the *person* who was supposedly being stitched together?

### 13\. The arahant captured by robbers

Nanananda brings forward the story of the monk Adhimutta.

Bandits capture him.

They intend to use him as a human sacrifice.

A fairly substantial interruption to one's meditation schedule.

The bandits expect terror.

Resistance.

Desperate bargaining.

But Adhimutta remains remarkably composed.

The bandit leader is baffled.

Why isn't this man afraid?

Adhimutta explains that the underlying identification and expectation which ordinarily generate mental terror have ended.

He doesn't regard the past and future through the same possessive story of a permanent *me* continuing through them.

He understands the passing of conditioned processes.

Nanananda uses the encounter to illustrate the freedom from fear associated with arahantship. In the traditional story, the robber is persuaded and Adhimutta is released; he is not actually sacrificed.

Now don't trivialise this.

This is not a claim that anyone who practises mindfulness should become incapable of experiencing ordinary fear.

Nor does a trauma response demonstrate spiritual failure.

The story describes an arahant, someone understood within the tradition to have completed the path.

The philosophical point is about the exhaustion of the assumptions through which death becomes the destruction of an imagined permanent owner.

Imagine the ordinary reaction\:

"My existence must continue."

"My experiences must continue."

"My identity must continue."

"I must not lose everything that makes me me."

And now imagine realising that what you were trying to preserve was never the independently existing entity you assumed it to be.

The body is vulnerable.

Conditioned processes arise and pass.

But the permanent metaphysical owner you feared losing was never established in the first place.

**What has ceased is not a real eternal self, but the delusion through which one was being imagined.**

This is why Nanananda resists interpreting Nibbana as the annihilation of an independently existing being.

The alleged entity was already a misconstruction.

And conversely, he resists the attempt to preserve that entity in some eternal spiritual form.

Both approaches begin by granting the self precisely the kind of substantial existence the teaching questions.

### 14\. The rope you are strangling yourself with

Now Nanananda offers another superb analogy.

Imagine two people twisting strands of a cord.

A third person grips the strands tightly in the middle.

Because of the arrangement, the twisting on either side produces tension in the middle.

The third person is convinced that the cord possesses an extraordinary solidity.

He feels the resistance.

He feels the strain.

He holds on harder.

But when he releases his grip, the opposing tensions no longer sustain the apparent structure in the same way.

Nanananda compares this with the way grasping sustains becoming amid continuous arising and ceasing.

Now here's the exquisite irony.

The man is afraid to release the rope because he thinks the rope is supporting him.

But his grasp is precisely what sustains the tension.

He has spent his entire life declaring\:

"I MUST NOT LET GO."

"IF I LET GO, EVERYTHING WILL FALL APART."

And the Buddha is pointing out that the compulsive holding is itself part of what makes the whole arrangement painful.

This doesn't mean that simply dropping all commitments produces enlightenment.

It means that **grasping contributes to the very structure of bondage from which grasping then seeks escape**\.

That's the self-perpetuating mechanism.

And there's something more.

When you understand the rope, you don't have to destroy the universe in order to escape it.

You must stop sustaining the relevant relationship.

This is the answer to the fear that Nibbana means annihilation.

You're imagining the destruction of a real metaphysical owner.

Nanananda is describing the ending of a conditioned bondage.

And the difference is enormous.


### 15\. Why even death is not the ultimate liberation

Nanananda insists that Nibbana is not merely something that happens after death.

The liberated person can realise the cessation of craving and conceit while alive.

That is indispensable.

Because if liberation only meant the ending of a physical organism, the teaching would fail to distinguish awakening from ordinary death in the required way.

And the Buddha's path would become a rather elaborate preparation for an event everybody undergoes anyway.

Nanananda is concerned with the cessation of grasping and becoming through knowledge and liberation, not a simple physical event.

The arahant can continue living while the relevant causes of bondage have ceased.

There is nevertheless a distinction between the liberation realised during life and the final passing away of an arahant, when the aggregates no longer continue.

We must not collapse them into one experience.

But Nanananda's central point is that genuine liberation is not postponed until the body's death.

**It is a transformation in the conditions sustaining suffering, here and now.**

And this brings us to the closing movement of Sermon 8.

### 16\. The mime who is riding an imaginary bicycle

Imagine somebody on a stage.

They are riding a bicycle.

Except there is no bicycle.

They turn the handlebars.

They pedal furiously.

They lean around corners.

They ring the imaginary bell.

The audience watches and immediately understands the action.

The actor dismounts.

Opens an imaginary door.

Sits on an imaginary chair.

Writes an imaginary letter.

It's a complete drama.

Yet the objects which make the drama intelligible are supplied through the relationship between the actor's movements and the spectator's understanding.

Nanananda applies this image to mindfulness of bodily posture.

Walking.

Standing.

Sitting.

Lying down.

Ordinarily, these are understood as the actions of a person who possesses a body.

But careful contemplation can reveal the dependent relationship between the bodily processes and the mental activities through which they are recognised and interpreted.

The body is not an independent metaphysical owner.

Nor is the naming activity an independently substantial self.

Nanananda calls attention to the way name and form mutually depend upon one another.

An important qualification: a real meditator does not have to hallucinate that chairs, bicycles or other people have disappeared. Nanananda's mime is an analogy for recognising the absence of independent essence, not a recommendation to deny ordinary physical reality.

But the philosophical point is devastating.

When you observe a body moving, where exactly is the independent controller?

Is it the intention?

The sensation?

The recognition?

The movement?

The consciousness of movement?

And if you choose one of these as the controller, what establishes *its* independence?

You keep looking for a permanent actor behind a conditioned performance.

And every candidate you discover turns out to participate in the performance.

The stage is full of activity.

But the independently existing performer remains strangely unavailable.

So now we reach Sermon 9.

And Nanananda takes this entire problem and makes it directly practical.

Instead of merely telling you that experience is constructed, he explains how ordinary things acquire significance and how that significance can be transformed through wisdom.


## Part III - Sermon 9: The mind turns rubbish into treasure, then becomes enslaved to the treasure

### 17\. What is the world actually made of?

At the beginning of Sermon 9, Nanananda returns to the practical investigation of bodily activity.

When there are hands, picking up and putting down become possible.

When there are feet, walking becomes possible.

When there are bodily joints, bending becomes possible.

The Buddha's point, as Nanananda interprets it, is that these recognisable activities depend upon conditions.

There isn't an independent metaphysical entity called *walking* floating around somewhere.

Walking is intelligible through the relevant bodily conditions and their operation.

Likewise, when you describe something as *my action*, you've introduced a relationship of identification in addition to the dependent process itself.

Nanananda takes this as an illustration of the reciprocal relationship between name-and-form. Analayo's notes also compare a Chinese parallel and Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation of the passage.

Now consider what normally happens.

You observe your body moving.

You don't merely recognise movement.

You recognise *yourself* moving.

The movement enters a story.

"I'm going somewhere."

"I'm becoming somebody."

"I'm accomplishing something."

"I'm getting closer to what I need."

All conventionally useful.

But the problem begins when these designations are taken as evidence of an independently existing owner.

Because the owner is never actually located outside the conditioned processes.

And that leads to a question much more useful than endlessly asking whether consciousness creates physical matter.

**How does an event become something I consider worth possessing?**

Now Nanananda introduces the most powerful example of this entire sermon.

### 18\. The gem in the rubbish heap

Imagine a man working through a heap of rubbish.

Rotten vegetables.

Broken containers.

Discarded household objects.

General filth.

The man is collecting it for disposal.

His attention is directed towards an undifferentiated mass of unwanted material.

Then something catches his eye.

A glimmer.

He looks closer.

It's a precious gem.

Suddenly everything changes.

The rubbish heap has produced a treasure.

Except, of course, the gem hasn't just materialised from nothing.

It was there before he noticed it.

What changed was **its place in his experienced world**\.

The object has become significant.

The man's intentions immediately reorganise around it.

He focuses upon it.

He imagines possessing it.

Pleasure arises.

A whole network of possibilities appears.

And Nanananda now maps this simple event onto a remarkable teaching from the *Kimmulaka Sutta*, AN 8.83.

The discourse describes the conditions through which mental phenomena arise and how they can culminate in liberation.

Watch the sequence.

The man sees the gem.

**Interest or desire\:** Something stands out as worth having.

**Attention\:** He singles it out from everything around it.

**Contact\:** The sensory encounter provides the immediate occasion.

**Feeling\:** Pleasure and excitement arise.

**Concentration\:** His mind becomes absorbed in the gem.

**Mindfulness or sustained recollection\:** He keeps the object in mind.

And then something crucial can happen.

**Wisdom\:** He recognises the gem's conditioned, impermanent and ultimately unsatisfactory character as an object of clinging.

**Liberation\:** The compulsive possession of the object can cease.

Nanananda's interpretation is that even the object which initially excited craving can become the occasion for insight.

And this is the extraordinary thing.

The mind didn't have to find another object.

It didn't need to throw the gem away and go searching for a mystical blue lotus.

It needed to understand the gem.

The very object that induced attachment can become a means of investigating attachment.

This is where Buddhism gets much sharper than the simplistic advice to avoid everything attractive.

Because avoidance can itself become another expression of bondage.

The problem isn't necessarily the object's presence.

It's your ignorance of the relationship through which the object becomes compelling.

### 19\. The most valuable thing about the gem is that you can become free of it

Now here's the insight that I think belongs at the absolute centre of this chapter.

Ordinarily, the man sees the gem and asks\:

"How can I have this?"

But wisdom asks\:

"Why has this thing acquired so much power over my mind?"

And when he understands its impermanence and the craving arising around it, the relationship can change.

He can still recognise the gem.

He can still understand its conventional market value.

He need not pretend it's actually a piece of ordinary rubbish.

But the gem no longer has to function as the basis of his imagined completion.

This is what Nanananda is getting at when he interprets liberation as the true culmination of the investigation.

**The deepest value of a thing, from the standpoint of liberation, is not that you can acquire it. It's that you can understand it so completely that you needn't be bound by it.**

This makes your earlier insight about setting the mind on Nibbana even more profound.

Suppose you desire wealth.

Ordinarily, your intention aims at possession.

You want the wealth because you believe it will provide something.

Security.

Status.

Power.

Freedom.

But now suppose you investigate the desire itself.

You begin to notice how much of your psychological life is organised around the assumption that acquiring wealth will finally settle the question of your well-being.

And you discover that the desire is producing its own anxieties.

You may still need money for entirely legitimate practical reasons.

But you no longer need to believe money possesses the power to make conditioned existence unconditionally secure.

That distinction changes everything.

Now substitute almost anything.

Romantic love.

Spiritual experiences.

Academic achievement.

Being recognised as intelligent.

Becoming a great scholar.

Finding the perfect teacher.

Discovering the ultimate nature of consciousness.

All of these can become objects around which the mind constructs bondage.

And all can also become occasions for understanding that bondage.

The crucial issue is whether the object becomes fuel for further appropriation or an occasion for wisdom.

**The same object can lead towards entanglement or release, depending on how it is understood.**

And this brings us to a seemingly devastating objection.

If desire can lead towards the ending of desire, isn't Buddhism contradicting itself?

### 20\. Can you desire the ending of desire?

Nanananda does not dodge this problem.

He makes it one of the central arguments of Sermon 9.

A brahmin comes to Ananda and asks about the purpose of the Buddhist holy life.

Ananda explains that it aims at the abandonment of desire.

Fine.

Then the brahmin asks how this is accomplished.

Ananda describes a path involving the cultivation of intention, energy, concentration and investigation.

The brahmin spots what he thinks is a contradiction.

Hang on.

You're using desire to abandon desire?

That's impossible.

Aha\!

Buddhism defeated by its own blimming grammar.

But Ananda gives an exceptionally simple answer.

Suppose you want to travel to a park.

That intention motivates your journey.

You travel.

You arrive.

And the specific desire to get there subsides.

The desire accomplished its function.

It doesn't need to remain active forever.

Nanananda explicitly uses this example to explain how even a desire for liberation can be employed in the ending of desire. He also discusses a discourse in which a provisional form of craving for attainment is used to overcome craving.

This is profoundly important.

The path is not an attempt to attain liberation while refusing to cultivate any intention whatsoever.

The Buddha teaches right effort.

The practitioner develops wholesome motivations.

Meditation requires sustained attention.

Wisdom must be cultivated.

But the whole enterprise is directed towards a cessation that does not require the perpetual maintenance of those motivations as objects of clinging.

Think about learning to swim.

You want to cross the river.

That desire motivates you to learn.

You practise.

You become capable.

You cross.

And now you don't have to continue craving arrival on the opposite bank.

You're there.

The original intention has fulfilled its purpose.

The distinction is between a motivation serving a liberating function and a craving that perpetually demands further becoming.

Not every desire or intention functions identically.

Nor does cultivating skilful intention imply that all forms of craving are harmless.

Nanananda's point is more subtle.

**An activity can be used to bring about the conditions for its own eventual cessation.**

This answers one of the great practical puzzles in Buddhism.

Why meditate if you're supposed to stop striving?

Because skillful effort is not the same as compulsive acquisition.

Why contemplate Nibbana if desire is the problem?

Because the contemplation can orient the mind towards understanding and ending the very processes that sustain bondage.

And when that understanding is complete, there is no need to keep producing the ambition to become liberated.

The ambition has accomplished its task.

This is why Nanananda insists that liberation cannot be understood merely as another object which the mind successfully possesses.

It is the consummation of a process that eventually releases its own supports.


### 21\. You can attain insight from something completely unexpected

Nanananda takes the argument even further.

He recalls the story of a monk who encounters an attractive dancer.

Now you might expect the traditional Buddhist response to be\:

"Terrible\! An attractive woman\! Spiritual danger\!"

But Nanananda's example is more interesting.

The monk encounters the spectacle.

He becomes attentive.

And rather than becoming enslaved by attraction, he recognises the dangers of craving and the impermanence of the experience.

The encounter becomes the occasion for insight and release.

Nanananda uses this alongside the image of a tiny dewdrop revealing the spectrum of sunlight.

You don't have to possess an entire rainbow.

The relevant principle can become visible through a single small phenomenon.

And this reveals a remarkable feature of insight.

An ordinary event can disclose something universal about conditioned experience.

Not because the event contains the entire universe in some mystical physical sense.

But because the same principles of conditionality, arising, ceasing and appropriation can be investigated in particular experiences.

Take a single moment of jealousy.

You recognise somebody receiving attention you wanted.

A painful feeling arises.

Your mind constructs a comparison.

You imagine what their recognition means about your own worth.

And then the entire identity becomes involved.

You could spend the next three hours analysing the other person's character.

Or you could investigate the mechanism.

What was contacted?

What was felt?

What was desired?

What was appropriated?

What assumption made another person's good fortune seem like a threat to your existence?

And what happens when that assumption is seen through?

**The ordinary experience of jealousy can become an investigation of dependent arising.**

This doesn't mean every instance of jealousy will produce enlightenment.

Nor does it justify deliberately provoking distress or creating harmful situations to obtain insight.

It means that the relevant principle is not restricted to a special class of exotic spiritual experiences.

The mechanisms of bondage are operating within ordinary life.

And so ordinary life provides opportunities to understand them.

### 22\. The difference between looking at something and seeing through it

Now Nanananda makes an important distinction.

A person may possess extraordinary sensory or meditative powers.

They may remember previous lives.

They may experience subtle states.

They may see things unavailable to ordinary perception.

And yet none of this necessarily means they possess liberating wisdom.

Why?

Because **the content of experience is not the same as insight into the conditions of experience**\.

Imagine somebody acquires the ability to inspect every historical moment in their own past.

They see themselves as a child.

They remember experiences long forgotten.

They observe apparent previous lives.

And then they begin constructing grand conclusions about who they ultimately are.

"I was this person."

"I was that person."

"My true nature must be ancient and eternal."

They've acquired extraordinary information.

But have they understood its conditioned character?

Or have they simply expanded the story of the self to include more episodes?

Nanananda reads the *Brahmajala Sutta* in exactly this context.

The Buddha examines numerous speculative views, including views supported by profound meditative experiences and recollections.

The problem is not necessarily that every reported experience is fabricated.

The problem is that its interpretation can exceed what the experience justifies.

And that is where the real epistemological danger lies.

You have seen something.

Excellent.

But **what does seeing it actually establish?**

A remembered life does not automatically establish an eternal soul.

An experience of boundless awareness does not automatically establish an unconditioned universal consciousness.

A state in which subject-object distinctions become attenuated does not automatically prove that all reality consists of one metaphysical substance.

These claims require arguments.

And Buddhist insight must examine whether the arguments are themselves conditioned by craving, identification and speculative attachment.

That's a rather higher standard than experiencing something impressive and announcing that you've cracked the universe.

### 23\. Sixty-two philosophical systems, all caught in the same net

Nanananda's final movement in Sermon 9 is particularly devastating.

The *Brahmajala Sutta* examines sixty-two types of speculative views.

Some assert that the self and world are eternal.

Others assert partial eternality.

Others propose cosmic origins.

Others speculate about the destiny of a liberated being after death.

Astonishingly elaborate philosophical systems.

And the Buddha's analysis repeatedly returns to a common principle\:

**These views arise dependent upon contact and the ensuing conditioned processes.**

Nanananda emphasises that even the most exalted speculative metaphysics must be examined through the conditions that gave rise to it.

The relevant distinction is not simply between believing a doctrine and disbelieving it.

It is between becoming attached to a constructed standpoint and understanding the conditions of its construction.

This is where the teaching becomes dangerous to intellectual vanity.

Because it doesn't merely say\:

"Other people's philosophies are conditioned."

It says\:

**Your philosophy is conditioned too.**

Your interpretation of Buddhism.

Your interpretation of consciousness.

Your preferred account of the universe.

Your theory of enlightenment.

Your conviction that you have finally understood how everything works.

All of them can become objects of craving, conceit and identification.

Which means you can construct the most sophisticated possible account of non-attachment while being completely attached to the account.

You may even begin treating your extraordinary understanding of emptiness as evidence that you are spiritually superior.

The ego has become so subtle that it now presents itself as the absence of ego.

Congratulations.

You've invented the world's first **self-congratulatory doctrine of selflessness**\.

And Nanananda's response is uncompromising.

The purpose of understanding dependent arising isn't to provide one more ultimate position from which you can defeat competing philosophers.

It's to understand the processes that sustain clinging.

The philosophy is valuable insofar as it contributes to that understanding.

If it becomes another possession, the teaching has been turned against itself.


## 24\. So does consciousness actually create the world?

Now we can finally answer the question with which the chapter began.

**Not in the simplistic sense that your private mind manufactures an external physical universe out of nothing.**

That would be an enormous metaphysical claim, and Nanananda's discussion of conditioned experience does not establish it.

His argument concerns the arising and establishment of **the world as experienced, recognised, interpreted and appropriated**\.

The senses operate through conditions.

Consciousness arises in dependence upon conditions.

Perception discriminates.

Attention selects.

Feeling gives experience an affective character.

Intention contributes to further activity.

Craving makes certain experiences compelling.

Conceit establishes relationships of personal identity.

Views stabilise interpretive structures.

And the mind begins treating this dynamically conditioned organisation as though it were composed of independently existing things belonging to an independently existing person.

That is the construction we're investigating.

But Nanananda's account is not merely a modern psychological theory.

He connects these processes with the full Buddhist teaching of dependent arising, including becoming, birth, death and the cessation of rebirth.

We shouldn't reduce that traditional soteriological claim to nothing more than a convenient metaphor for everyday emotional reactions.

At the same time, we need not accept every speculative extrapolation he makes as established by the earliest sources.

The distinction between his interpretation and the full range of surviving textual evidence remains important.

Analayo's comparative notes are valuable here. The Chinese parallel to the *Kimmulaka Sutta*, for instance, doesn't reproduce the Pali formulation in precisely the same way, although it retains the broad progression towards wisdom and liberation. That evidence encourages precision about the extent of Nanananda's argument rather than treating one recension as the only possible formulation.

And now comes the real philosophical payoff.

### The observer-observed divide

Ordinarily, experience seems to present two fundamental realities.

There is **me**, the observer.

And there is **the world**, observed.

The world affects me.

I respond to the world.

I try to arrange the world in accordance with my needs.

And the entire struggle seems to presuppose a stable subject standing opposite a stable collection of objects.

But Nanananda asks whether the independence attributed to these poles is justified.

The observer is known through conditioned processes.

The observed world, as experienced, is organised through conditioned processes.

And the sense of opposition between them becomes a basis for craving and grasping.

This is not a proof that subject and object are secretly one eternal substance.

Nor is it the assertion that conscious experience simply does not occur.

It is the claim that **the subject and object, conceived as independently existing entities, are not established in the way ignorance assumes**\.

The duality has been appropriated.

The mind takes up one side as *me*\.

The other as *what I experience*\.

Then it spends its existence trying to obtain unconditional security through arranging the relationship.

But what if that entire project rests upon a misunderstanding?

What if the supposed independent owner cannot be found?

What if the objects upon which the owner depends are themselves impermanent and conditionally established?

Then the whole project of securing that owner becomes unintelligible.

And the activity of grasping can be understood as fundamentally misdirected.

**You are trying to protect an independent self through the acquisition of independent things, while neither kind of independence can be established.**

That's the cut.


## 25\. The practical experiment: Watch an object becoming important

Let's turn this into something you can investigate directly.

Not as a mystical exercise.

Not as a performance of detachment.

And certainly not as an attempt to make ordinary objects visually disappear.

Simply examine a moment when something becomes important to you.

Suppose you receive a message.

You see the notification.

Immediately, something changes.

Perhaps it's from somebody you admire.

Pleasure appears.

Anticipation develops.

You wonder what they want.

You imagine the possibilities.

Your attention becomes organised around the message.

Now observe.

The message has a particular meaning.

But the importance it has acquired for you involves more than its literal content.

Your desires are participating.

Your expectations are participating.

Your memories are participating.

Your sense of identity may be participating.

And you may already be constructing a story about what the message means for your future.

Now ask a series of very precise questions.

What was actually encountered?

What feeling arose?

What interpretation followed?

What did I begin wanting?

What personal identity became involved?

And what would remain of this psychological drama if the demand to possess a particular outcome ceased?

Don't force an answer.

Investigate.

The point is not to eliminate pleasure.

It's to recognise the additional movements through which pleasure becomes something you must secure.

And if you discover anxiety or desire, don't immediately make that discovery another personal failure.

That would merely be another instance of the same process.

Observe the arising.

Observe the conditions.

Observe the movement towards appropriation.

And recognise whatever cessation genuinely occurs.

This is a very modest experiment compared with full liberation.

But it points in the direction of Nanananda's argument.

**An experience can be understood in such a way that the compulsive need to appropriate it is weakened.**

And that is why setting the mind towards Nibbana has practical significance.

The contemplation isn't primarily a search for a strange object hidden behind consciousness.

It is the cultivation of an understanding that exposes the activity of grasping.

The more deeply the process is understood, the less compelling its demands need become.

Not because a superior self has finally conquered the inferior self.

But because the dependent nature of the whole process is being recognised.


## The final cut - What has actually been discovered?

Three sermons.

One enormous philosophical problem.

And a remarkably consistent answer.

**Sermon 7** challenges the assumption that the cessation of conditioned experience can be found by locating some transcendent place or metaphysical consciousness-substance.

Nanananda examines non-manifestative consciousness in terms of release from establishment upon name-and-form. Analayo's textual comparisons prevent us from treating the famous luminosity passage as unequivocal proof of an eternal luminous awareness.

**Sermon 8** explains how ordinary concepts become the materials of a personal world.

The stage props become objects.

The objects become possessions.

The possessions become supports for identity.

And craving, conceit and views supply the thread that stitches these processes into renewed becoming.

When the thread no longer binds, the physical and mental activities of a living arahant can continue without the same bondage.

**Sermon 9** shows how desire, attention, contact and feeling participate in the formation of our experienced world - and how wisdom can transform that very process.

The gem becomes an object of desire.

But the gem can also become an object of insight.

The mind doesn't necessarily need to replace it with something more magnificent.

It needs to understand what has made the gem compelling.

And that brings us back to the original question\:

*How does consciousness construct the world I experience?*

It participates in a dependent process through which appearances become recognised objects, objects become appropriated, and appropriation sustains the apparent division between an owner and a world of things that owner must manage.

But here's the decisive insight\:

**Understanding this construction need not produce another construction to replace it. It can contribute to the ending of the grasping that sustains the whole arrangement.**

That is why Nibbana is not merely another object towards which the mind moves.

It is the ending towards which wisdom is directed.

And suddenly your earlier intuition becomes much more exact.

You set the mind towards Nibbana because understanding the possibility of release changes the purpose of attention itself.

Ordinarily, attention helps you acquire things.

Now attention is being trained to understand the process of acquisition.

Ordinarily, knowledge helps you secure an identity.

Now knowledge reveals the conditioned construction of identity.

Ordinarily, consciousness becomes entangled with things.

Now wisdom investigates the conditions of that entanglement.

The goal is no longer simply to possess a better experience.

It is to end the compulsion through which experience becomes bondage.

And we have now arrived at the next unavoidable question.

**If the mind can understand all of this, why does it keep doing it anyway?**

Why does recognition of the machinery not automatically stop the machinery?

Why can you understand craving perfectly well in one moment and be entirely consumed by it in the next?

Why does the mind keep proliferating experiences into stories, interpretations, identities, grievances, fantasies and endless opportunities for self-concern?

What keeps the whole blimming operation running?

That's Chapter IV.

And it takes us into one of Nanananda's most important subjects: **conceptual proliferation - the process through which an apparently simple encounter becomes an entire world of psychological entanglement.**
