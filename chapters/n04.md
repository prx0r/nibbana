# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter IV of X · Sermons 10-12 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, examined alongside Bhikkhu Analayo

## What keeps this whole blimming machinery running?

**The answer: You keep mistaking what happens for what you are. And once that mistake occurs, the mind begins constructing an entire world around defending, improving, preserving or escaping the person it imagines itself to be.**

Not merely through conscious thoughts.

Through perception itself.

Through the significance assigned to objects.

Through the assumption that experience must belong to somebody.

And through an extraordinary process in which the mind manufactures interpretations, becomes emotionally invested in them, and is then overwhelmed by its own productions.

Nanananda calls the last process *papanca*: conceptual proliferation.

But don't reduce it to overthinking.

Overthinking is only its most embarrassing public performance.

At its deepest, proliferation is implicated in the construction of a world divided into *me*, *mine*, *what I want*, *what threatens me*, *what I have been* and *what I must become*.

And the truly vicious feature is this:

**The mind mistakes the consequences of its own construction for independent realities which justify further construction.**

It's a self-confirming system.

A flipping philosophical Ponzi scheme.

You invent an identity.

The identity develops needs.

You pursue objects to satisfy those needs.

The pursuit reinforces the identity.

The identity becomes more important.

And the cycle continues.

But the precise question is: where does this whole process acquire its apparent solidity?

Nanananda begins Sermon 10 with an astonishingly simple answer.

A reflection.


# Sermon 10 - The person chasing his own reflection

## 1. The man who emptied an entire pond to find a jewel

Imagine somebody walking beside a pond.

He looks into the water.

There, beneath the surface, is a beautiful jewel.

An absolutely magnificent gem.

Naturally, he decides to retrieve it.

He reaches down.

Nothing.

He tries again.

Nothing.

Perhaps it's deeper than it appears.

He begins removing the water.

Still nothing.

Eventually, he empties the blimming pond.

Still no jewel.

The man is now standing in the mud, having devoted an enormous amount of effort to solving a problem which never existed in the form he assumed.

The jewel was a reflection.

And the man's crucial mistake wasn't that he saw something.

He really did see the appearance of a jewel.

His mistake was assuming that the appearance established the existence of an object *at the place where it appeared*.

He treated a dependent image as an independently recoverable possession.

Nanananda resumes the jewel-in-the-pond story from the *Ummagga Jataka* at the beginning of Sermon 10 to explain the predicament of the ordinary person.

Now imagine substituting the self for the jewel.

You experience a body.

You have memories.

You recognise your characteristics.

You notice thoughts, preferences and intentions.

And within this changing collection, you discern somebody called *me*.

Perfectly intelligible at an everyday level.

But then you make the additional assumption that there must be an independently existing owner behind the entire collection.

You begin searching for it.

Perhaps it's the body.

But the body changes.

Perhaps it's thought.

But thoughts appear and disappear.

Perhaps it's memory.

But memory is incomplete and constantly reconstructed.

Perhaps it's consciousness.

But conditioned consciousness arises dependent upon circumstances.

So you invent a deeper consciousness.

And if that too becomes questionable, perhaps an ultimate witness behind consciousness.

Or the witness of the witness.

Or the absolutely unsurpassable witness who witnesses all the other witnesses having a flipping meeting.

Meanwhile, you're still standing in the pond.

The mud is up to your knees.

And the jewel hasn't appeared.

**The mistake may be the assumption that an independent possessor must exist behind the processes that give rise to the appearance of possession.**

That is the philosophical problem Nanananda wants to expose.

But he immediately insists that the alternative cannot simply be to declare that nothing exists.

Because that is another way of misunderstanding the reflection.

## 2. The two lunatics arguing over the reflection

Picture two men standing beside the pond.

The first says:

"There's definitely a jewel in the water. It must exist somewhere beneath the surface."

The second says:

"No. The jewel is absolutely nonexistent. There is nothing to investigate."

Both positions miss something.

An appearance genuinely occurs.

But the appearance is not what the first man imagines.

And the conditions that produce it aren't abolished merely because the second man denies its existence.

Nanananda uses this contrast to explain the two extremes of eternalism and annihilationism.

The eternalist takes the apparent self as an independently existing entity that must somehow continue forever.

The annihilationist takes that same supposed entity and imagines its absolute destruction.

Both begin by granting the very self whose independent existence Buddhism questions.

The argument resembles two people fighting over whether a fictional character survives the end of a film.

One insists the character must live eternally.

The other insists the character has been murdered by the projectionist.

Meanwhile, neither has investigated the conditions under which the character appeared.

And Nanananda's point is that **Nibbana is neither the permanent preservation nor the annihilation of an independently existing self**.

### The Buddha's third answer

Nanananda now takes up a discourse from the *Itivuttaka*.

The Buddha identifies two mistaken orientations.

One group delights in existence and recoils from its cessation.

Another is so troubled by existence that it delights in the prospect of non-existence.

The first is attached to being.

The second is attached to the destruction of being.

The second may appear more radical, but it is still organised around a view of what must happen to *me*.

**One wants to preserve the prisoner forever. The other wants to destroy the prisoner. Neither has examined whether the prisoner exists as imagined.**

The Buddha presents a third possibility: understand what has arisen as something dependently arisen.

Not as an eternal essence.

Not as an absolutely nonexistent nothing.

As something that has come about through conditions.

And because it has arisen through conditions, its cessation is intelligible.

This is the middle path Nanananda is developing. \[buddhistinquiry.org; Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammàsambuddhassa\]

Notice how much sharper this is than merely saying:

"Everything is impermanent."

That's true enough, but it doesn't yet expose the underlying mistake.

The deeper problem is that the mind takes conditioned processes, imagines an independent being within them, and then becomes obsessed with the destiny of that imagined being.

One person wants eternal existence.

Another wants non-existence.

The awakened perspective questions the assumption shared by both.

And this reveals the central mechanism.

**The mind is not merely frightened by impermanence. It is frightened because it has made the impermanent into its identity.**

### 3. The donkey chasing his own carrot

Nanananda now introduces an absolutely brilliant image.

A donkey pulls a cart.

Somebody suspends a carrot in front of it.

The carrot is attached to the cart.

The donkey moves towards the carrot.

The cart moves forward.

The carrot moves forward.

The donkey moves towards the carrot.

The cart moves forward.

And the poor sod goes on chasing a goal which the very act of chasing keeps out of reach.

This is Nanananda's comparison for craving and becoming.

Think about your own projects.

"I will be happy once I've achieved this."

You work.

You achieve it.

And then the mind identifies another condition that must be fulfilled.

Now you need recognition.

You obtain recognition.

Now you need security.

You obtain some security.

Now you fear losing it.

Now you need greater security.

The objective changes.

But the fundamental movement continues.

*Something I do not yet possess must make me complete.*

And the startling possibility is that **the psychological demand for completion itself helps perpetuate the feeling of incompleteness**.

The carrot keeps moving because the donkey keeps moving the cart.

Now don't misunderstand this.

Having practical goals isn't inherently delusive.

You need food.

You may want an education.

You may wish to help other people.

You can pursue meaningful projects.

The problem arises when attainment becomes the supposed means of securing an independently complete self.

The mind isn't merely undertaking a useful activity.

It's trying to resolve an existential lack through acquisition.

And every acquisition gives that lack another opportunity to reappear.

So why does the entire mechanism keep running?

Because the mind doesn't merely want something.

It wants something **for someone**.

Someone must benefit.

Someone must become complete.

Someone must be protected.

Someone must eventually arrive.

And that brings Nanananda to an apparently innocent phrase:

*I am.*

## 4. The most expensive two words in human history

Nanananda argues that the conceit of *I am* acts like a central reference point around which the experienced world becomes organised.

He develops this through an unusual reflection on ordinary grammar.

We speak of things being here.

There.

Somewhere else.

Mine.

Yours.

Belonging to somebody.

But these relationships are understood relative to a standpoint.

*Here* is where I am.

*There* is away from me.

*Mine* is something related to me.

And once that standpoint has become reified into an independently existing subject, the world appears as a collection of objects standing in relation to that subject.

This is Nanananda's philosophical interpretation of the conceit *I am*; it is not an established linguistic proof that every use of the verb *to be* arises from ego-attachment.

Now consider what this means for ordinary life.

Somebody receives praise.

The mind doesn't merely register approval.

**I am admired.**

Somebody rejects a proposal.

**I am rejected.**

Somebody becomes more successful.

**I am falling behind.**

Somebody expresses a different opinion.

**I am being challenged.**

And what began as a changing event has become a verdict upon the supposedly stable person at the centre of experience.

Now the person must respond.

Defend itself.

Improve itself.

Explain itself.

Vindicate itself.

Become something greater.

And the process renews itself.

### The mirror manufactures a second person

Nanananda reinforces this through a discourse in which the monk Punna explains that the sense of *I am* occurs dependent upon the five aggregates.

The illustration is a young person looking into a mirror.

The person sees their reflection.

They recognise the image.

And the image gives rise to self-consciousness.

"That's me."

Now there's nothing intrinsically problematic about recognising a reflection.

The difficulty begins when the image becomes a basis for grasping.

Perhaps you like the image.

You become proud.

Perhaps you dislike it.

You become ashamed.

Either way, the image acquires an enormous power over your psychological life.

And suddenly you're no longer merely seeing a face.

You're evaluating an identity.

Nanananda draws a parallel with the five aggregates: bodily form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness provide the dependent basis upon which the sense of *I am* arises.

The remarkable point is that **the sense of self is dependent, not independently self-established**.

And if it depends upon conditions, then the conceit can cease when its supporting ignorance is removed.

But look how easily we get trapped.

You examine the body.

You decide the body isn't the self.

You examine thoughts.

You decide thoughts aren't the self.

You examine feelings.

You decide feelings aren't the self.

Then you discover awareness.

And announce:

"AH! HERE'S THE REAL ME."

The self has simply changed departments.

It's now working in consciousness.

You've dismissed the entire ordinary management team, only to discover that the chief executive has rebranded himself as Pure Awareness.

Nanananda's analysis does not allow us to make that move without argument.

Consciousness itself belongs among the aggregates that can become supports for identification.

And the question therefore remains:

**What conditions allow consciousness and its objects to appear as a world inhabited by a separate owner?**

Now the sermon gets particularly subtle.

## 5. Contact is not simply two independent things bumping together

Ordinarily, we imagine experience like this.

There's a body.

There's an external object.

They meet.

A sensation occurs.

Simple.

But Nanananda examines the *Mahanidana Sutta*, DN 15, which describes contact as dependent upon name-and-form.

He highlights two related dimensions.

One concerns the recognisable characteristics and designations through which objects become intelligible.

The other concerns resistance, impingement and the way embodied experience is affected.

The important point is their interdependence.

An object cannot become a meaningful feature of experience without the conditions through which it is perceived and identified.

Nor is experience reducible to unconstrained mental naming with no bodily or sensory conditions.

**The mind does not simply invent everything. Neither does it encounter the world as an interpretation-free spectator.**

Nanananda's interpretation joins the naming and bodily dimensions of experience in an account of conditioned contact.

### The carpenter who says two planks are touching

Imagine a carpenter working with wood.

He places two planks together.

He says:

"These two pieces are touching."

Perfectly useful language.

But what is he actually describing?

The carpenter recognises a physical relationship.

His attention distinguishes the planks.

He interprets their arrangement.

He applies a concept.

And he communicates the relationship through language.

The planks don't need to possess a tiny subjective faculty of feeling touch.

Yet the metaphor of contact is perfectly intelligible.

Nanananda uses examples of this sort to demonstrate the role of attention, recognition and convention in the designation of relations.

Now here's the point for meditation.

When something strikes your senses, you don't experience a raw physical universe with every quality labelled objectively from outside the process.

You experience through a particular body, particular capacities, particular attention and particular conditions.

Which is why the same event can possess radically different significance for different people.

A loud sound may be exciting.

Or frightening.

Or irrelevant.

Or pleasant.

And what happens next depends partly upon the relationship established through experience.

But be careful: this is not a claim that attention creates external objects or that material interactions cease when nobody notices them.

The Buddhist analysis concerns the conditions under which objects become part of conscious experience and a basis for attachment.

This distinction is especially important because Nanananda sometimes writes in language that can sound radically idealistic.

His investigation does not independently establish that the physical universe is entirely produced by an individual's consciousness.

### Analayo's useful corrective

Analayo's Sermon 10 materials place Nanananda's rendering of the *Mahanidana Sutta* beside Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation.

They also compare the definition of bodily form with a Chinese *Samyukta-agama* parallel.

That parallel explicitly emphasises resistance and susceptibility to damage, including injury through physical contact and environmental conditions.

This is important because Nanananda's account must not be reduced to a theory in which naming creates all physical reality.

There really is a body vulnerable to cold, heat, injury and death.

The Buddha's investigation is concerned with the embodied conditions of suffering.

And Nanananda now uses a rather brutal image.

### 6. Your body is an enormous wound

Imagine somebody with an exposed wound.

Even a small contact can produce pain.

Heat irritates it.

Cold irritates it.

Pressure irritates it.

The wound is vulnerable because of its very constitution.

Nanananda draws upon the *Khajjaniya Sutta*, SN 22.79, where bodily form is characterised through susceptibility to being affected.

The body is affected by hunger, thirst, heat, cold and other conditions.

Nanananda develops the memorable image of the embodied organism as an extensive wound, vulnerable to contact.

Now understand the philosophical force.

The body doesn't become vulnerable because you're insufficiently spiritual.

Vulnerability is inherent in its conditioned nature.

And this is precisely why identifying with the body as an independent source of unconditional security is so problematic.

You are attempting to construct invulnerability around something whose defining characteristic includes susceptibility to change.

Again, not an argument against caring for the body.

Quite the opposite.

Care for it intelligently because it is vulnerable.

But don't demand that it become the permanent foundation of your existence.

That demand is impossible.

And now another question emerges.

Even if you understand the body is conditioned, why does it still appear so extraordinarily solid?

Why do objects look like compact, independent things?

Why does the world continue to present itself as if everything possessed an essence?

And why does that appearance give rise to such elaborate judgments about what exists and what doesn't?

That's Sermon 11.


# Sermon 11 - The mind gets mugged by its own perceptions

## 7. Why does the world look so blimming solid?

Nanananda begins Sermon 11 with a simple observation.

When something presses against the body, it feels real.

Cold affects you.

Heat affects you.

A mosquito bites.

A sharp object hurts.

The mind takes these encounters as confirmation that it is dealing with substantial things.

And in an ordinary practical sense, it is.

But Nanananda asks whether this establishes everything we subsequently conclude about the independent existence of objects.

It doesn't.

Consider the difference between a sensation and a metaphysical assertion.

A surface feels hard.

That is an experience.

But the conclusion that *hardness belongs to an absolutely self-existing thing* is an interpretation.

Similarly, something appears.

Then disappears.

And we conclude that a completely independent entity has passed from absolute existence into absolute non-existence.

Nanananda argues that our ordinary impression of solidity encourages these extremes. He calls this the *perception of compactness*: the tendency to apprehend conditioned phenomena as unified, substantial things.

### A fist that isn't a fist

Imagine somebody holding up a closed fist.

There it is.

A recognisable object.

Now they open their hand.

Where did the fist go?

You can ask the question grammatically.

But the fist was not an independent substance hidden inside the hand.

It was a configuration.

The arrangement made the designation appropriate.

When the arrangement changed, the designation ceased to apply.

Now, obviously, a living organism is immeasurably more complicated than a fist.

And Nanananda isn't claiming human consciousness can be exhaustively explained by finger positions.

But the analogy exposes a crucial logical mistake.

**The usefulness of a designation does not establish the independent existence of what the designation names.**

This is why the problem isn't solved by merely opening up the body and examining its components.

You discover tissues.

Cells.

Molecules.

Atoms.

You construct more sophisticated descriptions.

All potentially useful.

But the question of how you become *identified with* these processes hasn't been answered.

You can study material reality with extraordinary precision and still become psychologically enslaved to the thought:

**"This is my body, therefore my existence is threatened whenever it changes."**

More information about the object doesn't automatically remove the grasping.

And Nanananda is challenging the assumption that a sufficiently detailed description of matter will, by itself, solve the problem of suffering.

That is a soteriological criticism, not a scientific demonstration that modern physics is mistaken.

### 8. Why you can't escape a ghost by running into another room

Now Nanananda brings back one of his excellent images.

Imagine walking through a dark house.

You see something moving.

A ghost!

You immediately run away.

You hide in another room.

Still terrified.

Perhaps you lock the door.

Perhaps you move to another house.

Perhaps you spend the next twenty years avoiding the building.

But what if the ghost was never there?

What if the appearance was a mistaken interpretation?

Running away may temporarily remove the frightening sight.

It doesn't necessarily correct the misunderstanding.

The delusion survives the change of location.

Nanananda applies this to the attempt to escape material existence simply by attaining an immaterial meditative state.

A practitioner may enter exceptionally refined absorption.

The perception of ordinary bodily form may disappear from awareness.

The state may be extraordinarily peaceful.

But if the underlying ignorance remains, the attainment is not yet Nibbana.

**Removing an object from experience isn't the same as removing the delusion associated with that object.**

The ghost has followed you into the immaterial realm.

Not because a supernatural entity is actually pursuing you.

Because the misunderstanding remains uncorrected.

Nanananda uses this analogy while interpreting the *Kalahavivada Sutta*, a discourse concerned with conflict, perception, contact and the ending of the conditions that sustain them.

And this makes the entire project of meditation much more exact.

The question isn't:

"How do I make the troublesome objects disappear?"

It's:

"What am I failing to understand about the conditions through which these objects become compelling?"

You can temporarily remove sensory stimulation.

You can enter deep concentration.

You can withdraw into solitude.

These may all be useful.

But **if the capacity for craving remains, the absence of an object is not proof of liberation from the object**.

### The strange answer about perception

Nanananda now examines one of the most difficult passages in the early discourses.

A questioner asks what kind of condition allows material form and the experiences of pleasure and pain to cease.

The answer describes someone who is neither perceiving in the ordinary way, nor simply unconscious, nor occupying one of several other familiar classifications.

What the heck is that supposed to mean?

At first glance, it sounds like the Buddha is describing an exotic altered state.

Perhaps a special concentration attainment.

Perhaps a level between ordinary perception and complete unconsciousness.

And the traditional *Mahaniddesa* interpretation moves in that direction, associating the passage with progress towards immaterial attainments.

Nanananda disagrees.

He believes the context points towards a much deeper liberation: the ending of the delusive dependence upon perception which sustains conceptual proliferation.

Analayo's notes preserve this interpretive disagreement and place different translations, including Bhikkhu Bodhi's and a Chinese parallel, alongside the passage. The parallels are relevant evidence, but they do not make Nanananda's specific reading an uncontested conclusion. \[buddhistinquiry.org; buddhistinquiry.org\]

His philosophical proposal is worth taking seriously.

**Liberation doesn't mean being trapped in ordinary perception, nor does it mean merely eliminating perception through unconsciousness or absorption.**

It involves a wisdom that no longer becomes deluded through what is perceived.

Think again about the cinema.

The audience is completely absorbed in the story.

A knowledgeable spectator recognises the projection as a projection.

The same images may remain visible.

But the relation to them changes.

The spectator is no longer deceived in the same way.

Nanananda considers this a useful analogy for why liberation cannot be understood simply as a choice between ordinary perception and its total absence.

But the analogy has limits.

An arahant isn't merely someone who has learned to recognise visual illusions.

And the canonical teaching concerns the full cessation of the defilements, not simply a change of interpretive attitude.

Still, it exposes the error in thinking that Nibbana must be the most sophisticated conceivable form of sensory deprivation.

### 9. The questioner tries to resurrect the soul

Here's the particularly funny part.

The discourse has spent considerable effort examining perception and the cessation of its delusive consequences.

The questioner then wants to know whether this represents the supreme purification of a soul.

Or whether something even more exalted remains.

Mate.

We've just spent the entire afternoon questioning the independent self.

And you're asking whether its soul has now acquired a premium subscription to purity?

This is exactly the tendency Nanananda criticises.

A teaching designed to undermine substantial selfhood is immediately reinterpreted as a description of what happens to a substantial self.

If the self survives, the eternalist celebrates.

If the self disappears, the annihilationist celebrates.

Either way, the original assumption remains intact.

The discourse's conclusion instead points towards freedom from dependence upon speculative views.

**The liberated person does not need to settle the destiny of an imagined independent self, because that imagination is no longer being sustained.**

This is also why the Buddha's refusal to answer certain metaphysical questions cannot simply be treated as a coy endorsement of whichever answer we already prefer.

Sometimes the problem is not that the answer is too mystical to express.

Sometimes the question itself presupposes what must be investigated.

But now we're left with something troubling.

If the ordinary mind is constantly using perception to construct and defend its world, how does that mechanism actually work?

Nanananda has spent ten sermons preparing the ground.

And now he arrives at one of the greatest psychological analyses in the early Buddhist canon.


## 10. The Honeyball Sutta: a perfect explanation of how you drive yourself insane

Imagine somebody approaching the Buddha with a simple question:

"So, what exactly do you teach?"

The person asking is Dandapani, a Sakyan who carries a staff.

He's expecting a doctrine.

Something he can understand, classify and perhaps dispute.

Instead, the Buddha gives a strange answer about a teaching that does not lead to quarrelling with anyone, and about the ending of the perceptions that underlie obsession.

Dandapani is baffled.

He shakes his head.

Raises his eyebrows.

Makes an expression of profound philosophical disgust.

And leaves.

The Buddha has apparently failed the ancient Indian equivalent of a podcast interview.

Later, the monks ask him to explain.

He gives another, even more compressed statement.

Then he goes into his dwelling.

The monks are now completely lost.

So they approach Mahakaccana, renowned for explaining brief teachings in detail.

And Mahakaccana delivers the analysis.

This is the structure of the *Madhupindika Sutta*, MN 18.

Nanananda introduces it towards the conclusion of Sermon 11.

### The mechanism

Here is the essential sequence, in plain English.

From an ordinary encounter to an entire psychological universe

A sense meets an object

Consciousness arises

Contact

Feeling

Perception

Thinking about what is perceived

Conceptual proliferation

**The person is overwhelmed by proliferating perceptions and interpretations concerning past, present and future.**

Now that final reversal is enormously important.

The person begins by experiencing something.

Then thinks about it.

Then proliferates it into further interpretations.

And finally becomes overwhelmed by the consequences of that proliferation.

**The activity that was supposed to help you understand experience begins dominating the person who initiated it.**

That's the mechanism.

### One message. An entire catastrophe.

Imagine a friend fails to reply to you.

That's the event.

No reply.

Now the sequence begins.

You notice the absence.

Perhaps disappointment arises.

You recognise that the friend normally responds quickly.

You begin interpreting.

"That's unusual."

Then:

"Maybe they're annoyed."

Then:

"Perhaps I said something wrong."

Then:

"They've probably been annoyed with me for months."

Then:

"Everyone eventually gets tired of me."

Then:

"I always ruin relationships."

Now you have moved from one presently observable event to an entire identity extending across your personal history and imagined future.

And the emotional consequences are real.

Your chest tightens.

Your stomach contracts.

You become anxious.

You begin preparing defensive messages.

The mind is now reacting to possibilities which it has treated as though they possessed the certainty of observed facts.

**You have converted incomplete evidence into a fully operational emotional reality.**

And now here's the vicious reversal.

You send an accusatory message.

Your friend becomes confused.

Perhaps irritated.

They respond curtly.

And you announce:

"SEE! I KNEW SOMETHING WAS WRONG."

The proliferation has begun shaping the very circumstances it was supposedly interpreting.

You've manufactured additional evidence for the story.

The original experience was simple.

But the mind has created an entire personal universe.

And then become enslaved to it.

### 11. The three magicians who resurrected a tiger

Nanananda provides a marvellous story.

Three magicians encounter a pile of tiger bones.

The first reconstructs the skeleton.

The second restores flesh and blood.

The third brings the tiger to life.

Amazing.

A magnificent display of occult skill.

Unfortunately, the revived tiger immediately eats all three magicians.

A slight oversight in the project-management department.

Nanananda uses this to illustrate the Honeyball Sutta's sequence: the mind progressively constructs something that eventually overwhelms its own maker.

And this is much better than saying proliferation merely means excessive thinking.

Because the frightening feature isn't the number of thoughts.

It's the **loss of freedom**.

At first, you're directing attention.

Then you're evaluating.

Then you're elaborating.

Then the elaborated world begins dictating what you must feel and do.

The tiger is alive.

And now it's eating you.

### Why insight doesn't automatically stop it

Here is the direct answer to one of our central questions.

You can understand the mechanism intellectually while its underlying conditions remain active.

You can know that a message doesn't establish rejection.

And still feel rejected.

You can know that your social status is impermanent.

And still experience the possibility of humiliation as existentially threatening.

You can know that bodily sensations are conditioned.

And still construct elaborate fear around them.

Why?

Because the intellectual proposition isn't identical to the uprooting of the tendencies that generate appropriation.

The mind has repeatedly organised experience through craving, conceit and views.

Those tendencies can become active when the appropriate conditions appear.

And their operation need not wait for your conscious philosophical permission.

**Understanding a mechanism in theory is not the same as ceasing to participate in it.**

That's why meditation matters.

It creates opportunities to observe the mechanism as it occurs, rather than merely remembering that the mechanism exists.

But we have to be careful.

Not every episode of persistent thought is delusive proliferation.

Planning a journey requires thought.

Studying philosophy requires thought.

Remembering a danger may be protective.

Reconsidering an argument may be intellectually responsible.

Nanananda's target is the kind of proliferation which is governed by ignorance and attachment, and which culminates in being overwhelmed by one's own conceptual constructions.

The problem is not the bare capacity to think.

It's the bondage generated through how thinking is used.

And that is precisely where Sermon 12 begins.


# Sermon 12 - How the mind gets lost on the way to liberation

## 12. The monk who was supposed to go to Colombo

Nanananda wants to distinguish two different failures.

Imagine somebody has an urgent errand in Colombo.

They must get there.

The destination is clear.

Now suppose they become distracted.

They stop at a tavern.

They get drunk.

They fall asleep.

The errand is forgotten.

That's ordinary heedlessness.

But there's another possibility.

The person leaves intending to reach Colombo.

They begin travelling.

They investigate roads.

They choose alternatives.

They take increasingly elaborate diversions.

And after an enormous amount of activity, they arrive in Kandy.

Wrong city.

They haven't simply been idle.

They've been extraordinarily busy.

They've travelled.

Made decisions.

Exerted effort.

Perhaps developed sophisticated theories about navigation.

But they have missed the destination.

Nanananda uses this contrast to distinguish ordinary heedlessness from conceptual proliferation.

And it is a particularly devastating analogy for spiritual practice.

Because spiritual people can become phenomenally industrious.

You meditate.

Study.

Debate.

Develop elaborate systems.

Investigate consciousness.

Construct detailed metaphysical theories.

Compare traditions.

Attend retreats.

And spend so much time refining your account of liberation that you never examine the actual activity of grasping.

You've produced an extraordinarily sophisticated map.

Unfortunately, you're in flipping Kandy.

### The spiritual version

Suppose you begin with the Buddha's practical question:

"How does suffering cease?"

Then somebody mentions consciousness.

You investigate consciousness.

Reasonable.

Then you investigate whether consciousness is fundamental.

Interesting.

Then you develop a theory about consciousness being the ontological ground of reality.

Now we're getting ambitious.

Then you begin debating which traditions have correctly identified this ground.

Then you become personally attached to one position.

Then somebody criticises your theory.

Then you spend a week defending it.

Then you feel humiliated because somebody has misunderstood your argument.

And suddenly the original project of ending attachment has become a magnificent exercise in attachment.

You've transformed the teaching into an identity.

The problem isn't that metaphysical investigation is forbidden.

It's that the investigation has lost its liberating function.

The donkey is chasing a scholarly carrot.

The tiger is eating its magicians.

And the fellow with the map is proudly announcing that his arrival in Kandy represents an advanced form of reaching Colombo.

### 13. Why craving, conceit and views belong together

Nanananda explains that traditional commentaries frequently associate conceptual proliferation with three tendencies:

Craving.

Conceit.

Views.

But he makes an important distinction.

These are not merely three unrelated symptoms.

They are characteristic ways in which proliferation develops.

Consider a desirable object.

**Craving:** I want it.

**Conceit:** Possessing it would make me someone.

**Views:** My interpretation of its significance must be correct.

And the three can reinforce one another.

Imagine purchasing an expensive watch.

Initially you admire it.

Then you want it.

You acquire it.

Now the watch becomes part of your identity.

You begin thinking of yourself as somebody with refined taste.

Then you develop opinions about which watches are genuinely superior.

Someone criticises your brand.

You become defensive.

You spend hours researching why they're wrong.

And what began as an attractive object has become an entire philosophical dispute concerning your worth.

The watch isn't the fundamental problem.

The problem is how craving, conceit and views have turned a useful object into an existential possession.

Nanananda argues that proliferation is the expanding, complicating activity within which these tendencies operate. It is not simply a synonym for any one of them.

This also explains why abandoning one form of attachment doesn't necessarily end the entire problem.

You may stop craving luxury.

But become proud of your simplicity.

You may abandon material possessions.

But become attached to being a renunciant.

You may understand that your opinions are provisional.

But become extraordinarily proud of your intellectual openness.

The original object changes.

The pattern continues.

**You can turn the absence of possessions into a possession.**

And this is why liberation must reach deeper than behavioural simplification.

It must undo the conditions through which identity and appropriation keep reconstituting themselves.

### 14. The secret weapon of perception: the sign that pretends to remain

Nanananda now investigates an extraordinarily subtle feature of perception.

To recognise something, the mind must be able to identify a characteristic.

A mark.

A pattern.

Something through which the object is recognisable again.

That is enormously useful.

Without recognition, ordinary life would become impossible.

You couldn't recognise your friends.

You couldn't navigate your house.

You couldn't distinguish edible food from poison.

But recognition has an interesting implication.

It enables the mind to apprehend changing phenomena through relatively stable signs.

You see a particular person today.

You recognise them tomorrow.

You call them by the same name.

And the continuity of the designation can encourage the assumption that an independently identical entity persists unchanged beneath every experience.

Nanananda argues that this tendency contributes to the perception of substantiality.

Now take the most intimate example.

You have a memory of yourself at ten years old.

You have another of yourself at twenty.

You recognise them as belonging to the same person.

The recognition is conventionally valid.

But now you add something.

"There must be an independently existing self that remained absolutely the same throughout."

That is the additional metaphysical claim.

And the continuity of a name does not establish it.

### The strange immortality of the label

Suppose a river changes continuously.

Water enters.

Water leaves.

Its banks shift.

Its depth fluctuates.

Its course may alter.

But you keep calling it by the same name.

The name is useful.

It allows communication.

It identifies a conventionally continuous feature of the landscape.

But the name can also conceal the dynamism of what it describes.

Now suppose somebody begins arguing:

"Because the river has the same name, there must be an absolutely unchanging river-substance underneath its flow."

That doesn't follow.

The designation tracks a pattern.

It need not name an independently immutable essence.

And the ordinary mind makes a similar mistake with itself.

"I was that child."

"I am this adult."

"I will be this future person."

The conventional continuity is understandable.

But the mind treats it as evidence that an eternal owner must persist through the entire series.

**A useful designation becomes an existential assertion.**

And Nanananda thinks this mistaken use of conceptual continuity contributes to the proliferation of identity.

But there's another feature of the process.

It doesn't merely make changing things appear permanent.

It allows the past and future to invade the present.

### 15. The mind invents a time machine, then gets trapped inside it

Imagine remembering something humiliating from years ago.

The event is over.

The people involved may have forgotten it.

The circumstances no longer exist.

Yet you remember.

You interpret.

You rehearse.

Your body reacts.

And suddenly you're furious about something that happened in an entirely different situation.

Now imagine the future.

You anticipate an important conversation.

You imagine failing.

You construct the embarrassment.

You envision the consequences.

And your body begins responding as though the imagined situation is already occurring.

The mind has made an extraordinary move.

It is treating absent events as present objects of emotional investment.

Nanananda draws attention to the *Niruttipatha Sutta*, which distinguishes the appropriate designations of past, present and future phenomena.

His argument is that proliferating thought can blur these distinctions, bringing what has passed or has not yet occurred into an apparently immediate reality.

This is not the simplistic assertion that memories and anticipations are unreal.

They are present mental events.

The important distinction concerns what they represent.

A present memory is not the past event itself happening again.

A present expectation is not the future event already occurring.

And the mind can lose sight of this.

### The monk who smacked his teacher with a fan

Nanananda gives an absolutely magnificent story from the Dhammapada commentary.

A young monk is fanning his teacher.

A simple task.

Nothing particularly dramatic.

But the young monk begins daydreaming.

Perhaps he will leave the monastic life.

Perhaps he will marry.

Perhaps he will have a child.

Perhaps he will return one day in a splendid carriage to visit his former teacher.

An entire future unfolds.

The imaginary family travels.

The child falls.

The young monk becomes furious with his imaginary wife.

He lashes out.

And discovers, rather abruptly, that he has struck his actual teacher with the fan.

The poor sod has become so absorbed in a fictional future that his present body is acting out the fantasy.

Nanananda uses this story to illustrate the mind's confusion of temporal constructions with present reality.

Now the comedy is obvious.

But the mechanism is extraordinarily important.

The daydream isn't merely a harmless sequence of images.

The monk begins emotionally participating.

The imagined future acquires present force.

And finally the imagined action becomes a real physical movement.

The fantasy has started controlling the body.

Exactly as the tiger devoured the magicians.

And now consider how much ordinary suffering operates through this mechanism.

You remember something painful.

You reconstruct it.

You identify with the person who suffered.

You imagine what should have happened instead.

You become angry that reality failed to conform to the imagined alternative.

Then you fear that something similar will happen again.

Now the past is generating a future.

And the imagined future is changing your present.

The mind is juggling three periods of time while insisting it is simply responding to reality.

**Much of what you're fighting is not occurring in the way the mind currently represents it.**

That doesn't make the original events harmless.

Nor does it mean trauma, grief or anxiety can simply be switched off by noticing their temporal structure.

But it exposes an important component of proliferative suffering.

The mind treats constructed temporal narratives as possessions and threats.

And then becomes overwhelmed by them.

### 16. The Buddha's milk-and-butter trick

Nanananda now introduces a surprisingly profound analogy from the *Potthapada Sutta*, DN 9.

Milk becomes curd.

Curd becomes butter.

Butter becomes ghee.

At each stage, a different designation is appropriate.

When the substance is milk, calling it milk makes sense.

When it becomes curd, calling it curd makes sense.

And so forth.

These are designations for stages in a conditioned process.

But imagine becoming philosophically obsessed with the relationship between the stages.

You ask whether butter already exists secretly inside milk.

Or whether milk is absolutely annihilated when curd appears.

Or whether there's an eternal dairy-substance underneath the entire transformation.

The metaphysical madness begins.

Nanananda uses the simile to examine the way ordinary linguistic categories can be mistaken for independent substances, and how this confusion influences theories about personal identity and its persistence.

Now notice the practical distinction.

A conventional description can be entirely adequate without solving every metaphysical question.

You can cook with butter.

Drink milk.

Make cheese.

None of these activities requires treating the different products as eternal substances.

Likewise, you can talk about a child becoming an adult.

A person learning a skill.

A meditator developing wisdom.

These are intelligible descriptions of conditioned changes.

But if you assume every description refers to a metaphysically independent owner who persists unchanged through all the transformations, you've added something the description itself doesn't establish.

And the Buddha's position, as Nanananda interprets it, is wonderfully practical.

Use the conventions.

Understand their conditions.

Don't become enslaved to them.

### The crucial difference between language and bondage

This is where the entire argument becomes especially sharp.

**Language is not the enemy.**

The Buddha uses words.

He teaches concepts.

He distinguishes suffering from its cessation.

He describes the path.

He explains conditioned arising.

He speaks to people using ordinary conventions.

The issue is not that words must disappear.

The issue is that words need not be grasped as though their referents possessed independently established essences.

Nanananda points out that the Buddha uses conventional designations without clinging to them.

And this is important for anyone who enjoys philosophy.

You don't have to stop thinking.

You don't have to abandon scholarship.

You don't have to become suspicious of every concept.

You have to understand what a concept is doing.

Is it helping you investigate experience?

Or are you using it to establish and defend an identity?

Are you employing a distinction because it's useful?

Or because you've become emotionally invested in its absolute truth?

Is the teaching functioning as an instrument of liberation?

Or has the instrument become another possession?

And perhaps most importantly:

**Can you use a concept without requiring the concept to define what you ultimately are?**

Because that is the distinction between an intelligible teaching and a new form of bondage.


## 17. Why the Buddha can argue without becoming entangled

Nanananda now takes up an apparent paradox.

The Buddha engages in discussions.

He challenges mistaken views.

He presents arguments.

He sometimes gives exceptionally forceful replies.

And yet the discourses describe him as someone who does not engage in quarrelling in the ordinary possessive sense.

How is that possible?

Surely arguing means you're attached to a position.

Not necessarily.

Suppose someone asserts that a poisonous substance is harmless.

You explain that it is dangerous.

You provide evidence.

You correct their misunderstanding.

None of this necessarily requires making the debate into a contest over your personal worth.

You can be committed to the truth of an explanation without being emotionally enslaved to the identity of the person who explains it.

Nanananda invokes the Buddha's debate with Saccaka as an example of maintaining composure even in forceful disagreement.

Now think about the ordinary alternative.

Somebody challenges your interpretation.

Immediately:

"They don't understand me."

"They think I'm stupid."

"I have to prove I'm right."

"If I lose this argument, what does that say about me?"

A philosophical disagreement has become a threat to identity.

And the moment that happens, the content of the argument may become secondary to the need to defend the person arguing.

You're no longer merely investigating whether a proposition is correct.

You're protecting *your proposition*, because it's become part of *you*.

This is how conceptual proliferation produces conflict.

The disagreement becomes personal.

The person becomes defensive.

The defence generates further interpretations.

Those interpretations produce further feelings.

And suddenly two people who began discussing a simple issue are fighting for their psychological survival.

The mind has constructed a tiger.

And is now demanding that everybody acknowledge its claws.

### What freedom from proliferation actually means

It doesn't mean never expressing an opinion.

It doesn't mean every possible interpretation is equally valid.

It doesn't mean abandoning reason or evidence.

It means the end of the compulsive appropriation through which interpretations become the supports of identity, craving and conceit.

And this explains something that can otherwise sound impossible.

The Buddha can speak.

Think.

Teach.

Respond.

Disagree.

And yet not generate the same psychological entanglement.

Nanananda describes this as the liberated person's ability to employ conventional designations without being bound by them.

Now here's the extremely important consequence.

**The cessation of conceptual proliferation is not necessarily the cessation of conceptual functioning.**

A liberated person can understand language.

Recognise a tree.

Follow an argument.

Remember a conversation.

But those activities need not produce delusive identification.

The distinction is between functioning and bondage.

And that brings us to the final extraordinary move of Sermon 12.


## 18. The Buddha begins his entire philosophical teaching by attacking your relationship to things

Nanananda now turns to the very first discourse of the *Majjhima Nikaya*.

The *Mulapariyaya Sutta*, MN 1.

It is a notoriously difficult discourse.

And Nanananda thinks it contains an indispensable key to Buddhist philosophy.

The discourse takes a range of concepts: material elements, beings, gods, exalted meditative states, things seen and known, unity, diversity, the totality of experience, even Nibbana itself.

Then it examines how different kinds of people relate to these concepts.

The first is the ordinary person.

The second is someone undergoing higher training.

The third is the fully liberated arahant.

The Buddha is also described separately, with the perfected relation explored in the continuation of Nanananda's discussion in Sermon 13.

The crucial question is not simply **what objects does a person experience?**

It is **what does the person do with them?**

### The ordinary person's fatal addition

Take an object.

Any object.

You perceive it.

Fine.

But then imagination and identification begin.

You conceive yourself in relation to it.

You conceive it in relation to yourself.

You appropriate it.

You delight in it.

And in doing so, the object becomes part of an expanding personal world.

Now apply this to something more interesting than a physical object.

Knowledge.

You discover an important philosophical truth.

At first, you simply understand the argument.

Then you begin identifying with it.

"I understand something most people don't."

You imagine how this distinguishes you.

Perhaps you begin using the insight to establish a superior spiritual identity.

You become proud.

You develop expectations of recognition.

And when somebody challenges your understanding, you become defensive.

A useful insight has become an object of possession.

The original truth may be entirely valid.

But your relationship to it has become entangled.

**A correct philosophical proposition can still become the occasion for psychological bondage.**

Now extend the argument to Nibbana.

This is where it becomes especially devastating.

You hear about Nibbana.

You develop an idea of what liberation must be.

Perhaps an extraordinary peace.

Perhaps limitless awareness.

Perhaps the complete absence of identity.

Perhaps an unconditioned reality.

You become fascinated.

Then you begin imagining yourself attaining it.

You speculate about what it would mean for your future.

You compare your present experience with your imagined enlightened state.

You become dissatisfied.

You start chasing the image.

And now the very idea of Nibbana has become another carrot.

The donkey has entered a monastery.

### 19. Can you turn Nibbana itself into an object of clinging?

Absolutely.

And the fact that MN 1 includes Nibbana among the concepts examined makes this point extraordinarily sharp.

**You can appropriate even the concept of non-appropriation.**

You can become attached to the idea of freedom from attachment.

You can imagine the liberated person you will become.

You can construct an identity around having seen through identity.

And then defend that identity with immense determination.

The spiritual project has become self-defeating.

But Nanananda does not conclude that the path should be abandoned.

Instead, he distinguishes between the ordinary person's way of relating to concepts and the attitude of someone who is actively training in wisdom.

The trainee learns to recognise the object without indulging in the additional constructions of identification and ownership.

The task is not merely to obtain a more sophisticated concept.

It is to change the relationship through which concepts become grounds for grasping.

And Nanananda places special importance on the instruction not to indulge in that conceptual appropriation.

### Analayo's comparative clarification

Analayo's Sermon 12 materials place Nanananda's reading alongside Nanamoli's translation of MN 1.

Both renderings support the practical distinction between the ordinary person's conceiving and the trainee's instruction to refrain from it.

The materials also present a useful difference concerning a verse in the *Sabhiya Sutta*: Nanananda reads name-and-form as a product of proliferation, whereas Bhikkhu Bodhi's rendering can be read as treating proliferation and name-and-form as related objects of understanding, without necessarily asserting that one produces the other.

That matters. The strongest causal formulation is part of Nanananda's interpretation; it should not be presented as the only possible reading of the verse. \[The Nibbana Sermons 12 to 22 by Bhikkhu K Nanananda; The Nibbana Sermons 12 to 22 by Bhikkhu K Nanananda\]

This doesn't make Nanananda's interpretation useless.

It makes the question more exact.

How far can we establish that proliferation constructs the *experienced world*?

How far can we establish that it constructs the *sense of self*?

And what is the relationship between the two?

Those are the questions that now become unavoidable.


## 20. The practical experiment: One insult, three different worlds

Let's bring the entire chapter into one immediately recognisable event.

Suppose someone says:

"Your work isn't very good."

That's the encounter.

A sound.

A recognised sentence.

Now imagine three possible responses.

### The first: ordinary proliferation

You hear the criticism.

Unpleasant feeling arises.

Then identification.

"They think I'm incompetent."

Then memory.

"This always happens."

Then imagination.

"Other people probably agree."

Then fear.

"Maybe I'll never succeed."

Then resentment.

"Who the heck are they to judge me?"

Then the construction of an entire personal world.

You imagine arguments.

You rehearse replies.

You construct a narrative in which you have been misunderstood.

Perhaps you become determined to prove your superiority.

And now something extraordinary has happened.

A single present encounter has become an elaborate story encompassing past, future, identity, status, threat and vindication.

**The mind has promoted an event into an existential crisis.**

That's proliferation in action.

### The second: suppression disguised as wisdom

Now suppose you understand Buddhism.

You know you're not supposed to become attached.

So the criticism arrives.

And you immediately think:

"I must not feel hurt."

"I must not identify."

"I must remain equanimous."

"I am above this."

But now you've created another identity.

The person who must be spiritually superior to criticism.

And when hurt appears anyway, you become angry with yourself.

"I should have overcome this by now."

You've taken the original insult and added an entire second layer of suffering.

This is not liberation.

It's proliferation wearing meditation robes.

The mind has discovered a new means of defending itself.

It now defends the identity of somebody who is not defensive.

A magnificent achievement in self-contradiction.

### The third: investigating the process

The criticism arrives.

Unpleasant feeling arises.

You recognise it.

You notice the desire to defend yourself.

You notice thoughts about identity.

You notice the story beginning to expand.

And instead of treating every element of that process as an unquestionable command, you examine the conditions.

Was the criticism accurate?

Perhaps.

Was it inaccurate?

Perhaps.

Is some practical response required?

Possibly.

But does any of that establish that your fundamental worth as a person has been destroyed?

No.

Does it require an entire identity to be constructed around the encounter?

No.

Must the experience become a permanent possession?

No.

And suddenly, the available possibilities expand.

You might learn from the criticism.

You might reject it on reasonable grounds.

You might ask for clarification.

You might protect yourself against abusive behaviour.

Or you might simply allow the matter to end.

The point is not that your emotional reactions must immediately disappear.

It is that **the compulsive construction of an existential drama is no longer the only possible response**.

And as wisdom deepens, the conditions supporting that compulsion can be progressively weakened.

This is a modest illustration of the path, not a demonstration that a moment of emotional composure equals arahantship.

But it reveals the direction of practice.

You're learning to recognise the machinery without automatically feeding it.


## 21. So what actually keeps the machinery running?

We can now answer the chapter's question precisely.

It isn't merely thought.

Thought can be useful.

It isn't merely perception.

Perception is necessary for ordinary functioning.

It isn't merely memory.

Memory can support intelligence and wisdom.

It isn't even the bare recognition that a conventional person exists.

The Buddha himself speaks meaningfully about persons.

**The machinery is sustained by ignorance and the craving, conceit and clinging through which conditioned experience becomes an apparently independent world organised around an apparently independent owner.**

And conceptual proliferation is one of the principal ways this process expands and reinforces itself.

The mind encounters something.

Recognises it.

Evaluates it.

Builds a story.

Identifies with the story.

Projects the story across time.

Then becomes overwhelmed by the products of its own activity.

The whole process can operate without a deliberate decision to make it happen.

Which is why understanding the theory doesn't automatically end it.

The relevant tendencies must be understood and uprooted.

And that requires more than intellectual agreement.

It requires cultivation, ethical restraint, concentration and liberating wisdom.

Nanananda's radical contribution is to show how these familiar Buddhist teachings come together as an investigation of the very conditions through which experience becomes bondage.

But the final point is the most important.

**The goal is not to build a better story about yourself. It's to end the necessity of being imprisoned by the story.**

And we must distinguish that from psychological blankness.

An arahant can still communicate.

Remember.

Recognise.

Act.

Use concepts.

Respond appropriately.

But the delusive appropriation through which these activities sustain renewed becoming has ended.

That's what makes this a doctrine of liberation rather than merely a technique for reducing mental chatter.


## The final cut - The machine doesn't need an enemy. It needs fuel.

Imagine a factory producing elaborate machines.

One machine builds another.

The second repairs the first.

Both produce components for a third.

The third constructs a mechanism that generates increasingly elaborate demands upon the entire factory.

And eventually the factory becomes convinced that its purpose is to satisfy those demands forever.

It hires more workers.

Expands its operations.

Builds new departments.

Develops sophisticated theories about productivity.

And every increase in production generates new requirements.

The workers become exhausted.

The management becomes anxious.

The factory starts treating its own expansion as an existential necessity.

Then somebody asks a very simple question:

**What are we actually trying to accomplish?**

And the factory has no answer.

Only more production.

This is the predicament Nanananda has been describing.

The mind constructs an identity.

The identity generates demands.

The demands produce craving.

Craving leads to further becoming.

Becoming sustains the identity.

And the whole arrangement continues because its products are mistaken for independent realities that must be defended.

Then the teaching of Nibbana enters.

And it doesn't say:

"Build a bigger factory."

"Find the ultimate machine."

"Become the chief executive of consciousness."

It says something radically different.

Investigate the production.

Understand its conditions.

Recognise what is being manufactured.

And understand how the causes of bondage can cease.

This is why setting the mind towards Nibbana can be so powerful.

**You're directing attention towards the possibility that the activity of grasping does not have to continue.**

Not because you've discovered a magical object.

Not because you've eliminated every thought.

Not because you've constructed an extraordinary state of awareness.

But because the very processes through which bondage is maintained are becoming intelligible.

And what is conditioned can cease when the relevant conditions are removed.

That is the promise of the teaching.

But now an even more uncomfortable question arises.

We've discovered that the apparent world of possessions and threats is organised around an imagined independent owner.

We've seen how perception and thinking become proliferative.

We've seen how the mind converts events into stories and stories into identities.

We've seen how the identity then becomes the basis of further craving.

**But exactly how does the sense of self get constructed out of these processes?**

Where does the first-person perspective acquire its extraordinary authority?

Why does experience so insistently appear to belong to *me*?

And how does the Buddhist account explain identification without simply inventing a hidden subject behind it?

Most importantly, if the self is dependently constructed, what happens when that construction is fully understood?

That is Chapter V.

And Nanananda is about to take us into the Buddha's analysis of the most deeply ingrained conceit of all:

**The conviction that there must be somebody at the centre of experience who owns the whole blimming thing.**


**End of Chapter IV - Sermons 10-12 of 33.**
