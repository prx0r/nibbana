# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter X of X · The Finale · Sermons 29-33 of 33 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, with Analayo's comparative scholarship

## If you know the prison is constructed, how do you stop building it?

**The answer: You stop treating the processes that create bondage as things you must possess, defend or become. But the way to accomplish that is not another act of blind renunciation. It is a disciplined, intelligent, progressively refined training that finally makes its own compulsive continuation unnecessary.**

And this is where the whole thing gets absolutely magnificent.

For twenty-eight sermons, Nanananda has been explaining the trick.

The mind constructs an apparently substantial world through conditioned consciousness, perception and contact.

It takes sides.

Develops craving.

Builds identities.

Mistakes conditioned processes for independent possessions.

Then spends its existence attempting to make those possessions guarantee permanent security.

We've met the dog growling at its reflection.

The donkey chasing the carrot attached to its own cart.

The magician whose audience believes every illusion.

The whirlpool mistaking its swirling movement for an independent entity.

The chess players who nearly turned a friendly game into a matter of life and death.

And the children who eventually stopped caring about their sandcastles.

Now the question becomes unavoidable.

**What do you actually do when you see yourself participating in the machinery?**

Because understanding how a prison operates isn't identical to leaving it.

You can become the world's greatest expert on imprisonment while remaining in your cell.

You can publish a twelve-volume commentary on the architecture.

Develop a revolutionary new theory of prison consciousness.

Win an academic prize for explaining the relation between the prisoner and the bars.

And then become absolutely furious when somebody criticises your interpretation.

Congratulations.

You've built a second prison out of your knowledge of the first.

The Buddha's teaching is not intended to produce an exceptionally well-informed inmate.

It's intended to end the bondage.

And in Sermons 29-33, Nanananda finally turns the entire philosophical argument into a practical path.

But here's the first surprise.

**You cannot begin by pretending you have already finished.**

You need effort.

Attention.

Concentration.

Ethics.

Determination.

Confidence.

Discernment.

And a very particular kind of desire.

You must learn to use conditioned processes to bring about the ending of the conditions that sustain suffering.

Which seems paradoxical.

How can thinking help end compulsive thinking?

How can desire help end craving?

How can concentration lead towards something that isn't merely another concentrated experience?

How can a path made of conditioned activities lead to the unconditioned?

And, most dangerously, how can you practise relinquishment without becoming attached to being the magnificent person who relinquishes things?

Nanananda's answer is a masterpiece of practical Buddhist intelligence:

**Use what is necessary. Understand what it does. Do not mistake the means for the destination. And relinquish the means when their work is complete.**

But he doesn't begin with the Noble Eightfold Path.

He begins with a carpenter.

A razor.

And a battle against Mara's army.


# Sermon 29 - The carpenter who started shaving the ego off his own furniture

## 1. Even the planet isn't permanent. What the heck are you planning to preserve?

Sermon 29 resumes precisely where Chapter IX ended.

Nanananda has contrasted the gradual emergence of a world described in the *Agganna Sutta* with the destruction of a world described in the *Seven Suns Sutta*.

The entire cosmic stage is conditioned.

Mountains erode.

Oceans alter.

Continents change.

Even the vast structures that appear unimaginably stable are not beyond impermanence.

And now he recalls Sariputta's teaching in the *Greater Simile of the Elephant's Footprint*, MN 28.

If even the enormous earth element is subject to change, what grounds are there for treating our short-lived body as an unchanging, independently owned self?

Analayo's materials provide the corresponding Chinese *Madhyama-agama* passage, MA 30, which likewise contrasts the impermanence of the great earth with the vulnerability of the human body. \[buddhistinquiry.org; Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammàsambuddhassa\]

Now the argument is devastating.

You're worried that your body is changing.

Well, yes.

It's a body.

You'd prefer permanent youth.

Excellent.

Unfortunately, even mountains haven't negotiated that arrangement.

You want permanent status.

Permanent health.

Permanent recognition.

A permanent collection of experiences which reliably confirms that your existence is worthwhile.

And you're attempting to establish this upon a body whose continued functioning depends upon food, water, oxygen, sleep, temperature and an absurd number of other conditions.

**You've asked a vulnerable organism to provide an unconditional guarantee of existence.**

The organism has not agreed.

It wasn't even consulted.

And the Buddha's response is not to make you hate the body.

It's to stop demanding from it what its nature cannot provide.

Care for it.

Feed it.

Rest it.

Treat illness.

But understand that its vulnerability is not a personal insult.

And that understanding creates the foundation for meditation.

Now comes Nanananda's first precise instruction.

## 2. The carpenter has to become the critic of his own work

Imagine a carpenter.

He's shaping a plank.

He planes the wood.

Runs his hand across the surface.

Then lifts it up.

Closes one eye.

Looks along its edge.

And inspects the imperfections.

Why?

Because from that particular angle, irregularities become visible.

A surface which looked reasonably flat suddenly reveals little protrusions.

Uneven patches.

Ridges.

Places that need further work.

Now suppose the carpenter becomes emotionally attached to the plank.

"This is MY plank."

"I MADE this plank."

"This plank represents the quality of my craftsmanship."

"Therefore any suggestion of an imperfection is an attack upon my existence."

He would be a terrible carpenter.

The fellow has become so identified with the wood that he can no longer correct it.

Nanananda uses precisely this analogy for the special kind of investigation involved in insight meditation.

The practitioner must learn to examine their own experience without automatically defending it as *me* or *mine*.

That's an extraordinary distinction.

Ordinarily, introspection can become self-justification.

You feel angry.

You think about why you're angry.

And immediately begin constructing reasons your anger is entirely reasonable.

Perhaps some are valid.

But your investigation may already be compromised by the need to defend yourself.

You feel jealous.

You examine the jealousy.

And begin compiling evidence that the other person is threatening your happiness.

Again, you may be investigating the story rather than the mechanism.

You feel proud.

You investigate your pride.

And become proud of how perceptive you are about your pride.

The ego has become its own quality-control department.

A truly wonderful corporate arrangement.

Nanananda's carpenter does something different.

He changes the standpoint.

He examines the processes without automatically taking them as possessions.

**The relevant question isn't whether the experience makes you look good. It's what the experience reveals about its conditions.**

### Looking at the body without immediately becoming the body

Suppose discomfort arises in meditation.

The knee hurts.

Now ordinary identification begins.

"My knee."

"My pain."

"My body is resisting."

"I am terrible at meditation."

"I'll never be able to sit properly."

The sensation has become an entire personal narrative.

But suppose attention investigates the phenomenon.

There is pressure.

Perhaps warmth.

Perhaps muscular tension.

Unpleasant feeling.

An urge to change position.

A judgment about what the sensation means.

A fear of future consequences.

Some of these may convey useful practical information.

For example, sharp joint pain can indicate that you should change position rather than risk injury.

Buddhist insight doesn't require ignoring bodily safety.

But the investigation can distinguish the sensation from the additional activity through which it becomes an existential verdict.

**Painful feeling is one thing. The fabrication of a wounded permanent self around it is another.**

And that is precisely what Nanananda wants the meditator to see.

Not merely:

"The body is impermanent."

But:

"How is identification being imposed upon these changing processes right now?"

This is the carpenter examining the plank.

And what does he discover?

Three particularly persistent defects.

Craving.

Conceit.

Views.

The old trio.

Apparently, after twenty-eight sermons, the sods still haven't resigned.

So the carpenter reaches for a sharper tool.

## 3. The razor of not taking things personally

Nanananda describes the repeated Buddhist contemplation which refuses to treat the aggregates as possessions, identities or an eternal self.

But he makes an important point.

The negation isn't supposed to become another dogmatic proposition.

You're not meant to sit there endlessly declaring that nothing exists.

Nor develop a metaphysical identity around being the person who knows there is no self.

The contemplation has a practical function.

It cuts against the habits through which identification is established.

Nanananda compares this with planing down the three protrusions of craving, conceit and views until the mind no longer produces them as expressions of grasping.

Now imagine the carpenter at work.

He discovers a ridge.

Planes it down.

Another appears.

Planes that down.

The surface gradually becomes smoother.

But the crucial thing is that he doesn't confuse the tool with the finished product.

The plane is a means.

The purpose is to remove the irregularities.

Likewise, a contemplation of not-self is useful insofar as it removes identification.

Not insofar as it creates an increasingly elaborate intellectual object called *The Ultimate Doctrine of Not-Self*.

And here's the subtle point.

**Even the teaching against grasping must not become something you grasp.**

A practitioner may need to cultivate a particular perception in order to undermine an unskilful perception.

But that cultivated perception isn't necessarily the final state.

It's a tool.

And it has to be used intelligently.

That is why Nanananda keeps returning to the razor.

You cannot sharpen it properly while gripping it with desperate rigidity.

You need control.

But not strangulation.

Effort.

But not compulsive tension.

A direction.

But not an identity organised around succeeding.

And now comes the first great question of the finale.

**If you use thoughts to understand thoughts, how do you avoid producing more of the very activity you're trying to overcome?**

Nanananda's answer is that the path must be gradual.


## 4. The path isn't an instant deletion button. It's a relay of increasingly useful tools.

Imagine someone announces:

"I have understood that the mind constructs its own suffering."

"Therefore I shall now stop every unskilful thought."

Fine.

They sit down.

A thought appears.

They try to destroy it.

Another appears.

They become frustrated.

Now there's a thought about frustration.

Then a thought about how bad they are at stopping thoughts.

Then a thought about whether they have developed a spiritual obstacle.

Then a theory concerning the metaphysical origins of that obstacle.

Then a plan to write an essay about it.

Within ten minutes, they've developed an entire new psychological empire.

The anti-thinking campaign has become the largest thought-production facility in the country.

Nanananda's alternative is much more intelligent.

**Use the skill that is presently available to cultivate a better condition, and use that condition to approach the next stage.**

He calls the path both *pragmatic* and *relative*.

Pragmatic because its practices serve a purpose.

Relative because a stage is valuable in relation to what it enables, not as an absolute possession in itself.

He invokes the relay of chariots, which will become central to Sermon 30.

This changes how we understand spiritual progress.

A technique can be genuinely useful without being ultimate.

A meditative state can be profound without being Nibbana.

A conceptual insight can be correct without having uprooted all defilements.

The purpose of each stage is to enable a deeper understanding.

Not to provide another identity.

### The danger of demanding immediate perfection

Suppose you're angry.

You know anger is unskilful.

You try to stop it.

But it's already present.

Now you become angry about being angry.

The old mechanism has acquired another layer.

Instead, the path asks what can be understood and cultivated.

Can you recognise the anger?

Can you refrain from harmful speech or action?

Can you investigate the feeling beneath it?

Can you notice the interpretation that makes the anger compelling?

Can you cultivate a less hostile response?

Can you eventually understand the underlying conceit through which the whole experience becomes an attack upon *me*?

These are different tasks.

They need not all be accomplished in a single miraculous moment.

And they need not be confused with the final eradication of anger.

**The path is gradual because the conditions of bondage are established deeply enough that they require systematic cultivation.**

But now a particularly dangerous question arises.

If you are cultivating states, aren't you just constructing another psychological prison?

Why should replacing anger with peace be any better than replacing one pleasant object with another?

Nanananda answers through the Buddha's conversation with Sakka, king of the gods.

And it is extraordinarily precise.

## 5. Even unhappiness can sometimes be worth cultivating

Sakka asks the Buddha how desire arises.

The Buddha connects it with thinking.

Thinking, in turn, depends upon the concepts and distinctions produced through proliferation.

Sakka then asks what kind of practice leads towards the cessation of that proliferation.

And the Buddha gives a surprising answer.

Pleasant feeling can be skilful or unskilful.

Unpleasant feeling can be skilful or unskilful.

Even equanimity can be skilful or unskilful.

The criterion is whether cultivating a particular state causes wholesome qualities to increase and unwholesome qualities to diminish.

Nanananda draws on the *Sakkapanha Sutta*, DN 21, and interprets it as a description of graded training rather than a command to eliminate every feeling indiscriminately.

Now consider how radical this is.

Suppose you're studying a difficult philosophical argument.

You discover you've made a serious error.

You feel disappointed.

Perhaps embarrassed.

That unpleasant feeling might lead to greater honesty.

You examine the mistake.

Correct it.

Become more careful.

The experience is unpleasant.

But it can contribute to wisdom.

Alternatively, suppose someone praises your argument.

You feel wonderful.

But the pleasure reinforces arrogance.

You become less open to correction.

More attached to being right.

The experience is pleasant.

But it can contribute to bondage.

So the path isn't simply an optimisation algorithm for feeling good.

**The question is not merely whether a state feels pleasant. It is what the state is helping the mind become free from.**

That's an extraordinary shift.

And Nanananda adds that subtler forms of happiness, unhappiness and equanimity can lead beyond their grosser counterparts.

The path progressively cultivates states which make the next stage possible.

But even these must eventually be relinquished.

### The army of Mara turns out to be much closer than expected

Nanananda now returns to Mara's army.

The Buddha's early verses describe battalions associated with sensual desire, discontent, hunger, thirst, craving and other obstacles.

Nanananda stresses that the meditator's immediate struggle often concerns the *thinking and proliferation* surrounding these conditions.

A desirable object may remain visible.

But the thought that it must be possessed is something additional.

Hunger may be a real bodily condition.

But the endless mental drama surrounding food can become a further source of disturbance.

A practical concern can become an identity.

And that identity can generate further craving.

Nanananda's point is that insight investigates the conditions making these thoughts compelling.

Now he takes up the phrase *noble silence* and interprets a special thought-free concentration as the fruition attainment of arahantship.

He contrasts the temporary stilling of thought through tranquillity with the deeper uprooting of the defilements through wisdom.

That is a strong interpretive claim, not a universally agreed classification of meditative states.

**Analayo's comparative notes supply an important qualification:** a Chinese parallel, SA 501, identifies noble silence with the second absorption, where applied and sustained thought have subsided. That supports a more conventional meditative interpretation rather than Nanananda's exclusive emphasis on arahant fruition.

So we must distinguish two things.

The ending of unskilful mental tendencies through liberating wisdom.

And a temporary meditative state in which certain kinds of thought do not arise.

They are not identical.

A person can temporarily stop discursive thinking without becoming an arahant.

And a living arahant can still think and communicate without being bound by the old defilements.

The important point is the ending of bondage, not an absolute ban on thought.

### The first great practical conclusion

Nanananda's Sermon 29 is therefore not recommending a frontal assault on every thought.

It is showing how intelligent contemplation can progressively remove the conditions that make thought into proliferation.

The carpenter becomes capable of examining the wood without defending his craftsmanship.

The meditator learns to examine experience without making it into an identity.

Skilful states are cultivated to weaken unskilful states.

And the path advances through stages whose value is practical rather than absolute.

**You don't escape the prison by punching every brick. You investigate what keeps producing the walls.**

But now comes an enormous problem.

If the path employs increasingly subtle states, how do you know which states to cultivate?

And how do you avoid becoming attached to the very tranquillity you're using to escape attachment?

That's Sermon 30.

And it begins with one of the most brilliant distinctions in the entire Buddhist contemplative system.


# Sermon 30 - The Buddha's extraordinary instruction: Use one attachment to dismantle another, then put both down.

## 6. There are thirty-six ways to make yourself miserable-or begin escaping.

Nanananda starts Sermon 30 with the *Analysis of the Six Sense Fields*, MN 137.

This is where the practical genius of the teaching becomes unmistakable.

The Buddha distinguishes thirty-six ways in which experience can lead the mind.

Why thirty-six?

Because the six classes of sensory objects can each become the occasion for three kinds of feeling-joy, grief or equanimity-under two broad orientations.

One oriented towards ordinary acquisition.

The other towards renunciation and wisdom.

That's six sensory domains, three affective responses and two orientations.

Thirty-six possible patterns of mental life.

The Chinese parallel MA 163 preserves the basic distinction, though its descriptions and emphases differ in places.

Now the significant thing isn't the arithmetic.

It's that **the same sensory encounter can lead the mind in profoundly different directions**.

Imagine seeing a beautiful painting.

You might feel pleasure because you want to possess it.

You imagine buying it.

Displaying it.

Being admired for owning it.

Perhaps you become anxious about the price.

Or jealous of somebody who already possesses it.

That is a pleasure organised around acquisition.

But imagine seeing the same painting while understanding its impermanence.

You appreciate its beauty.

You recognise the changing conditions through which its appearance and your enjoyment occur.

You don't require the painting to become the guarantee of your happiness.

And the understanding itself produces a different kind of joy.

The object hasn't necessarily changed.

**What has changed is the significance you impose upon the object.**

And here's where it becomes really interesting.

The Buddha doesn't merely distinguish worldly joy from joy connected with renunciation.

He also distinguishes two kinds of grief.

### The misery that can be spiritually useful

Imagine somebody has developed insight into impermanence.

They understand how conditioned experiences continually arise and pass.

They begin longing for the freedom realised by the noble ones.

But they haven't attained it.

The longing produces sadness.

An uncomfortable sense of incompletion.

Now here's the surprising point.

The Buddha recognises this as grief connected with renunciation.

It's still something that eventually has to be transcended.

But it is not identical to the grief of somebody lamenting the loss of a desirable possession.

Its direction is different.

One form of grief reinforces craving for the world.

The other arises through the aspiration to transcend craving.

That distinction is explicit in MN 137, and Analayo's materials confirm the corresponding theme in MA 163.

Now this is exceptionally relevant to anyone practising seriously.

You may experience a period in which ordinary entertainments become less satisfying.

You begin recognising how much time you've spent pursuing things that cannot provide lasting security.

And the realisation is uncomfortable.

You don't yet possess the freedom towards which your understanding points.

But the old pursuits have begun losing their authority.

You may feel disillusioned.

Restless.

Uncertain.

And the mind immediately declares:

**"SOMETHING HAS GONE WRONG. I'M SUPPOSED TO BE BECOMING HAPPIER."**

Not necessarily.

The relevant question is whether the experience contributes to wisdom, ethical conduct and the weakening of craving.

Not merely whether the present moment feels pleasant.

But this is not an invitation to cultivate depression, punish yourself or regard persistent psychological distress as evidence of spiritual progress.

The Buddha is distinguishing the motivational and contemplative significance of different mental states.

He is not prescribing misery.

The grief connected with renunciation is itself eventually relinquished.

And that brings us to something even subtler.

### Two kinds of equanimity: one is wisdom, the other is ignorance wearing a very convincing disguise

Imagine someone observing suffering.

They remain unmoved.

Calm.

Emotionally neutral.

And you might conclude:

"Ah! Equanimity."

But why are they unmoved?

Perhaps they understand the conditioned nature of experience and remain free from compulsive reaction.

Or perhaps they simply don't care.

Perhaps they're indifferent.

Perhaps they're avoiding something emotionally difficult.

Perhaps they have learned to suppress their reactions.

These are not equivalent.

Nanananda stresses the Buddha's distinction between equanimity associated with ignorance and equanimity grounded in understanding.

The former doesn't transcend its object.

The latter understands the object's impermanence and therefore doesn't need to cling to it.

This is crucial.

A person might experience tremendous calm while remaining profoundly deluded.

They may be unaware of their own underlying tendencies.

Or withdrawn from difficult situations.

Or simply unmoved because nothing currently threatens what they value.

The calm itself doesn't establish insight.

**Peace without understanding can be another comfortable room inside the prison.**

And the Buddha doesn't stop even with intelligent equanimity.

He distinguishes equanimity related to diverse sensory objects from the more unified equanimity of refined meditative states.

The unified form can help transcend the grosser form.

But now the practitioner faces the final danger.

They may become identified with the extraordinary unified state.

They may announce:

"I have attained the highest equanimity."

"This is my true nature."

"I am finally beyond the ordinary world."

And the self has returned.

An exceptionally calm self.

But a self nonetheless.

So how do you escape that?

Nanananda's answer is the central word of his entire interpretation.

**Non-identification.**

The Buddha's instruction is to use non-identification to transcend even the refined equanimity associated with unity.

You cannot simply collapse the sensory world into an absolute One and declare victory.

Nanananda explicitly warns that replacing the opposition of two with a metaphysical unity is not the solution to clinging.

This is where the distinction from Trika is particularly important.

A nondual experience doesn't, by itself, establish that consciousness is the ultimate ontological substance of the cosmos.

The Buddhist investigation asks whether the experience has become another basis for conceit.

And the final criterion is freedom from that appropriation.

### The ladder that removes its own rungs

Imagine climbing a ladder.

You need the first rung.

Then the second.

Then the third.

Each is useful.

But if you're attempting to reach the roof, you cannot spend eternity defending your right to remain on rung number three.

At some point you move on.

And when you reach the destination, you don't need to carry the entire ladder around as a personal achievement.

Nanananda describes the Buddha's gradual method in precisely this spirit.

Use a subtler and more liberating mental state to transcend a grosser state.

Then use a still more refined understanding to transcend attachment to that one.

And finally relinquish the very activity of identification through which the states were being possessed.

This is not the rejection of practice.

It's intelligent practice.

**The means have value because of what they enable-not because they must remain forever.**

And now Nanananda asks the question which makes the entire middle path so extraordinarily difficult.

If you don't strive, you'll never cross the flood.

But if you struggle desperately, the flood sweeps you away.

So how the heck are you supposed to cross?


## 7. The Buddha who crossed the flood without stopping or struggling

A deity asks the Buddha how he crossed the flood.

The Buddha gives a seemingly impossible answer.

He crossed without remaining stationary and without struggling forward.

The deity is confused.

Understandably.

This sounds like the world's most infuriating swimming lesson.

"How do I cross?"

"Don't stay still."

"Fine. So I should swim?"

"Don't struggle forward."

"WHAT?"

"Excellent question. You'll figure it out."

But the Buddha explains.

When he remained still, he sank.

When he struggled, he was carried away.

By avoiding both extremes, he crossed.

This is the *Oghatarana Sutta*, SN 1.1, which Nanananda makes central to his account of the middle path.

Now the philosophical problem is wonderfully clear.

Suppose you are caught in a powerful current.

You decide to do absolutely nothing.

You sink.

So you decide to fight the river with every ounce of strength.

You exhaust yourself.

The current overwhelms you.

Neither extreme produces freedom.

The skilled swimmer works intelligently with the conditions.

Effort is required.

But not frantic, self-defeating struggle.

And Nanananda draws an important distinction.

**The path requires striving, not the compulsive struggling that reinforces bondage.**

That's almost the entire Buddhist method in a sentence.

### The meditator who tried to defeat himself

Imagine sitting down.

You notice craving.

You decide to destroy it.

Immediately.

No compromise.

Every desire must disappear.

Every thought must cease.

Every feeling must become acceptable.

And whenever the mind fails to obey, you become furious.

You've constructed a war against craving that is itself powered by craving.

You desperately want the absence of wanting.

You hate your hatred.

You become anxious about your anxiety.

And the entire practice has become an extraordinary exercise in adding suffering to suffering.

The river is carrying you away.

Now imagine the other extreme.

You announce that all effort is grasping.

Therefore there's no point in practising.

No need for ethical restraint.

No need for concentration.

No need to cultivate wisdom.

Everything is already fine.

And now you spend the rest of your life being dragged along by the very tendencies you were supposed to investigate.

You've sunk.

Nanananda's middle path is not a decorative compromise between the two.

It's a practical way of applying effort without turning effort into another expression of possessive identity.

You notice what is arising.

Understand the conditions.

Cultivate what is skilful.

Abandon what is unskilful.

And keep the goal of relinquishment in view.

Not because an eternal spiritual athlete must win.

But because the causes of bondage can be removed.

### Why the whirlpool is such a perfect analogy

Nanananda returns to the whirlpool.

The vortex is sustained by interacting currents.

Its apparent centre is not an independent object.

Now imagine attempting to defeat the vortex by adding more violent movement to it.

You may simply intensify the disturbance.

But refusing every intelligent intervention doesn't necessarily remove the supporting conditions either.

What is required is understanding the current.

The relevant conditions.

The way the movement sustains itself.

And the possibility of cessation.

**You cannot end a self-reinforcing process merely by becoming its most enthusiastic participant.**

And that is why the Buddhist path needs wisdom alongside effort.

Effort without understanding can reproduce the problem.

Understanding without cultivation may remain merely intellectual.

The two must work together.

But Nanananda now takes us to an apparently contradictory passage.

The Buddha says purity cannot be obtained through views, learning, knowledge, virtue or observances.

Yet neither can it be obtained without them.

What the actual heck?


## 8. The Buddha appears to announce that everything works and nothing works

A brahmin named Magandiya asks about the inward peace praised by the wise.

And the Buddha gives a remarkable reply.

Purity isn't achieved merely through philosophical views.

Nor learning.

Nor knowledge.

Nor ethical observances and vows.

But it also isn't achieved simply through their absence.

Magandiya is baffled.

Naturally.

The Buddha appears to have rejected both using the tools and not using the tools.

An extraordinary teaching.

Perfect for generating a religious argument lasting two thousand years.

But Nanananda thinks this is one of the clearest statements of the path's fundamental principle.

**You need the tools, but you must not grasp the tools as the goal.**

He disagrees with a commentarial explanation which takes the contrast primarily as a distinction between wrong and right kinds of views and practices.

Nanananda instead sees a deeper challenge to attachment even to correct and necessary means.

Now imagine learning to swim.

You need instruction.

Practice.

Appropriate technique.

You cannot simply jump into a raging river and declare that swimming is a conceptual construction.

That would be an extremely brief spiritual career.

But suppose you eventually become a competent swimmer.

The techniques have served their purpose.

You can cross.

You no longer need to preserve the identity of someone who must endlessly practise the first swimming lesson.

The technique was necessary.

But it wasn't the ultimate destination.

This is the distinction.

Ethics are necessary.

Concentration matters.

Knowledge matters.

But none should become an object of clinging.

And now Nanananda reveals one of the most extraordinary canonical conversations in the whole series.

The dialogue between Sariputta and Punna Mantaniputta.

It concerns seven purifications.

And the apparent impossibility of reaching Nibbana either through them or without them.


## 9. Seven chariots, one destination, and a king who doesn't keep the horses

Sariputta asks Punna a series of questions.

Is the holy life undertaken merely for purification of ethical conduct?

No.

For purification of mind?

No.

For purification of view?

No.

For overcoming doubt?

No.

For understanding what is and isn't the path?

No.

For purification of knowledge of the way?

No.

For purification of knowledge and vision?

No.

Now Sariputta is apparently running out of patience.

"Then what the heck is the holy life FOR?"

Punna answers that its goal is complete Nibbana without clinging.

Fine.

But then Sariputta asks whether any of the seven purifications **is** that Nibbana.

No.

Well, can it be attained without the seven purifications?

Also no.

Brilliant.

The Buddhist path has apparently become a metaphysical customer-support system in which every answer is no.

But Punna gives an image.

King Pasenadi needs to travel from Savatthi to Saketa.

A long journey.

Seven relay chariots are prepared.

He mounts the first.

Travels to the next station.

Disembarks.

Changes chariots.

Continues.

Eventually he reaches his destination.

Now suppose somebody asks:

"Did you travel the entire journey using the first chariot?"

No.

"Did you travel without any chariot?"

No.

"Then how?"

Through a succession of vehicles.

Each serving its purpose.

Then being left behind.

This is the *Rathavinita Sutta*, MN 24. Analayo's Sermon 30 materials compare the Pali version with MA 9, which preserves the dialogue and the underlying relay-of-chariots structure. \[Namo tassa bhagavato arahato sammàsambuddhassa; The Nibbana Sermons 23 to 33 by Bhikkhu K Nanananda\]

And now the philosophical point becomes beautifully obvious.

**A stage can be necessary without being final.**

Ethical conduct is not itself Nibbana.

But without ethical conduct, the path may lack an indispensable foundation.

Concentration isn't necessarily Nibbana.

But concentration can enable the investigation required for wisdom.

A conceptual insight isn't complete liberation.

But insight can undermine the ignorance sustaining bondage.

And the successive developments make the final release possible.

The mistake is to turn a necessary stage into a permanent possession.

### The king who became a collector of enlightened vehicles

Imagine Pasenadi arriving at Saketa.

The journey is complete.

But he's terribly proud of the chariots.

He decides to preserve them all.

The first chariot represents his early spiritual development.

The second, his advancement in concentration.

The third, his extraordinary philosophical understanding.

And so on.

He constructs an enormous museum.

Displays every chariot.

Develops an entire identity around his magnificent progress.

Then spends the rest of his life defending the superiority of his vehicle collection.

The journey has become an opportunity to establish a new ego.

He's reached the destination.

But can't stop being the man with seven chariots.

Nanananda wants us to avoid exactly this mistake.

The path is not intended to culminate in the accumulation of spiritual achievements.

It culminates in the ending of clinging.

**Each stage is to be understood and used without being made into the ultimate possession of an independent self.**

And this is why the last thing you need to do is carry the raft after crossing.

But note a useful textual complication.

The Pali *Rathavinita* describes seven purifications. Other traditions, including the Chinese *Madhyama-agama* counterpart, preserve differences in how aspects of purification are enumerated or expressed.

Analayo's materials also cite comparative research on these stages.

So Nanananda's specific progression should be read as his interpretation of a transmitted textual structure, not as proof that every early Buddhist school inherited an identical seven-step technical scheme.

Still, the core teaching is clear.

Use the vehicles.

Do not worship them.

Arrive.

And don't confuse the vehicle with the destination.

### The second half of the problem

Now here's the question that makes this entire story more than a charming metaphor.

Who decides when a vehicle has served its purpose?

What prevents someone from abandoning ethical conduct prematurely?

Or discarding concentration because they've decided they are beyond meditation?

Or claiming they no longer need wisdom because every concept is empty?

The answer cannot simply be:

"I feel like letting go."

That could be genuine insight.

Or ordinary laziness wearing robes.

So the path requires a coherent system of cultivation.

And that's precisely what Nanananda devotes Sermons 31-32 to explaining.

The thirty-seven factors of awakening.

Not thirty-seven spiritual trophies.

Not thirty-seven independent instructions randomly assembled by ancient monks.

A structured training in which different capacities develop, strengthen and eventually converge.

And it begins with something extraordinarily unglamorous.

**Actually noticing what is happening.**


# Sermon 31 - Thirty-seven ways to stop being an idiot, and not one of them is a trophy

## 10. You have been given a raft. Please stop trying to make it your personality.

Nanananda opens Sermon 31 by returning to the raft from the *Alagaddupama Sutta*, MN 22.

A man needs to cross dangerous water.

He constructs a raft.

Uses it.

Reaches safety.

Then must decide what to do with the raft.

The Buddha's instruction is that the teaching is for crossing over, not for clinging.

And Nanananda makes this the foundation of everything that follows.

You need the teaching.

You need the practices.

But their purpose is to end grasping, not to become objects of grasping themselves.

Analayo's materials preserve the corresponding Chinese passage in MA 200, which likewise emphasises using the teachings for crossing and then relinquishing attachment to them.

Now here's the danger.

You can study Buddhism.

Learn the discourses.

Develop concentration.

Cultivate mindfulness.

Become deeply knowledgeable.

And gradually construct an identity around your Buddhist progress.

Suddenly you aren't simply practising the path.

You are **a practitioner**.

An important practitioner.

A serious practitioner.

A practitioner whose insight is more refined than the insight of other practitioners.

And the whole thing has become a competition over who has the most spiritually impressive absence of ego.

Absolutely magnificent.

The self has turned renunciation into a prestige industry.

Nanananda's response is that every component of the path must be understood according to its function.

What does it cultivate?

What does it weaken?

What does it enable?

How does it contribute to the ending of craving?

If it becomes another possession, something has gone wrong.

And that is why the raft matters.

The path doesn't exist to give you a magnificent raft collection.

It exists to bring you across.

But now the question becomes immediate.

**How do you know whether the raft is actually taking you anywhere?**

Nanananda answers with an important distinction between the promise of happiness after death and the possibility of knowing something about freedom in this very life.

## 11. The monk who rejected pleasure because he wanted something he could actually know

A young monk named Samiddhi has just bathed at a hot spring.

He's drying himself in the sun.

A deity notices him.

The god is impressed by his youth.

And suggests he should enjoy ordinary human pleasures rather than wasting his life on a promise concerning a distant future.

It's a reasonable challenge.

Why give up pleasures you can enjoy now for some spiritual achievement that may or may not occur later?

Samiddhi's answer is extraordinarily sharp.

He says the situation is precisely the reverse.

Ordinary sensual pleasures are bound up with time and their consequences remain uncertain.

The Dhamma, however, is presented as something whose liberating effects can be known directly in the present.

Nanananda uses the story to explain the qualities of the teaching as immediately visible, inviting investigation and personally realisable.

Now here's why this matters.

Suppose someone tells you:

"Give up your pleasures."

"Follow these rules."

"Believe these doctrines."

"And after death you'll receive the ultimate reward."

You might reasonably ask for evidence.

What is the relationship between the practices and the claimed outcome?

What can be investigated now?

How do you distinguish genuine liberation from imagined progress?

Nanananda insists that the teaching must be examined in terms of its present transformative function.

Does anger weaken?

Does craving become more intelligible?

Does identification loosen?

Does wisdom increase?

Is the mind becoming less dependent upon the things it previously required for security?

These are not complete proofs of the traditional doctrine of rebirth.

But they are important dimensions of the path that can be investigated.

**The teaching should not require you to postpone every meaningful test of its value until you're dead.**

And that's a rather important feature of a philosophy concerned with ending suffering.

Otherwise the entire project becomes another promise of future possession.

One more extraordinary reward you must acquire.

One more invisible treasure waiting beyond death.

And you've returned to the carrot.

### The real danger of spiritual optimism

Now imagine somebody begins meditating.

They experience calm.

They conclude the practice works.

Then they have an unpleasant week.

The mind becomes restless.

Old desires return.

And they announce:

"The Dhamma has failed."

But this assumes a very crude criterion.

Every session must produce pleasant feelings.

Every week must feel better.

Every setback means something has gone wrong.

And that can become another form of craving.

The relevant question is not whether every moment feels good.

It's whether the practice is developing the capacities required to understand and abandon the causes of suffering.

This is why Nanananda now turns to the thirty-seven factors.

They provide a more comprehensive structure than simply attempting to feel peaceful.

And the first group is the foundation of the whole thing.

## 12. Four foundations of mindfulness: Stop guessing what is happening.

The first group is the four establishments of mindfulness.

Mindfulness of the body.

Of feelings.

Of the mind.

And of relevant mental phenomena.

Nanananda presents them as an organised way of beginning with relatively accessible aspects of experience and progressively investigating subtler conditions.

The practitioner doesn't simply learn to notice things.

They learn to understand **how those things arise, persist and cease**.

Now imagine you're sitting.

You notice bodily discomfort.

That's an observation.

Then you notice unpleasant feeling.

That's another.

Then you notice the mind becoming irritated.

Then the thought that your meditation should be going better.

Then the desire to escape the experience.

Then the identification with someone who is failing.

The process becomes increasingly intelligible.

And here's the important distinction.

Mindfulness isn't meant merely to provide an inventory of mental events.

It's intended to enable understanding.

The body is experienced.

Feelings arise.

Mental states change.

Hindrances appear and disappear.

Factors conducive to awakening can be cultivated.

And attention begins revealing relationships between these phenomena.

### The problem with merely watching

Nanananda makes a criticism of certain approaches to mindfulness.

He argues that some teachers overemphasise bare attention as though merely noticing whatever happens were sufficient for the entire path.

But the Buddhist training also includes right effort.

The practitioner must understand what is unskilful and what should be cultivated.

Mindfulness provides the information necessary for intelligent effort.

Now here's an example.

You're angry.

You notice the anger.

Fine.

But suppose the anger is accompanied by the desire to hurt someone.

And you announce:

"I'm simply observing."

Then you proceed to insult them.

An extraordinary form of mindfulness.

You've noticed the anger with great precision while allowing it to govern your behaviour.

Nanananda's point is that observation must be connected with ethical discernment and effort.

The practitioner recognises the unskilful state.

Understands its conditions.

And cultivates the appropriate response.

**Mindfulness without discernment can become an exceptionally well-lit prison cell.**

You can see everything happening inside.

But you haven't stopped feeding the machinery.

So what comes next?

The second group.

Four forms of right effort.

And they're much more intelligent than simply trying to become calm.

## 13. Four right efforts: The maintenance department of liberation

The four right efforts concern four tasks.

Preventing unarisen unskilful states.

Abandoning unskilful states that have arisen.

Cultivating skilful states not yet established.

And maintaining and developing skilful states already present.

Now look how practical this is.

The Buddha isn't merely saying:

"Observe your mind."

He's giving you a framework for responding to what you discover.

Nanananda argues that these four efforts are already foreshadowed within the establishment of mindfulness, especially in the investigation of hindrances and the factors of awakening.

Let's make that immediate.

### Preventing what hasn't arisen

Suppose you know that spending an hour reading hostile arguments online tends to make you angry.

You don't have to repeatedly expose yourself to that situation merely to prove your equanimity.

You can intelligently avoid conditions that are likely to produce the unskilful state.

That's prevention.

Not cowardice.

Not spiritual weakness.

Intelligent management of conditions.

### Abandoning what has arisen

Suppose the anger is already present.

Now the task changes.

You may need to stop speaking.

Refrain from harmful action.

Change the object of attention.

Investigate what is sustaining the reaction.

Develop a more appropriate attitude.

The important thing is that you are not required to obey the anger simply because it has appeared.

A mental state isn't an executive order.

You don't have to implement every suggestion issued by your nervous system.

### Cultivating what hasn't arisen

Suppose kindness is absent.

You can deliberately cultivate it.

Reflect upon the humanity of other people.

Remember that they too are vulnerable to suffering.

Develop the intention not to cause harm.

The aim isn't to manufacture a sentimental feeling.

It's to establish a more skilful response.

### Sustaining what has arisen

Suppose the mind becomes clear and steady.

Understanding deepens.

You begin responding less compulsively.

Now the task is to nurture the relevant conditions without turning the achievement into an identity.

Not:

"I AM NOW A PERSON OF SUPERIOR EQUANIMITY."

Because that would be a rather unfortunate maintenance strategy.

But:

"What conditions have supported this clarity, and how can they be developed?"

The entire process is intelligent and causal.

**You stop treating the mind as an independent sovereign and begin examining the conditions under which its states arise.**

And Nanananda now gives us the most physically demanding analogy of the finale.

A large rock.

And a meditator who isn't going to move it merely by wishing.

## 14. The enormous flipping rock that doesn't care how motivated you are

Imagine a huge rock blocking a road.

You need to remove it.

You look at the rock.

Develop a strong intention.

"I WANT THIS ROCK GONE."

Nothing.

The rock remains completely indifferent to your motivational excellence.

So you try pushing.

Perhaps it doesn't move.

You find a lever.

Adjust your position.

Apply pressure.

The rock begins shifting.

But now it threatens to roll back.

You steady yourself.

Coordinate your effort.

And finally, with a decisive movement, you topple it.

Nanananda uses this image to explain the relation between intention, energy, determination and investigation.

The important point is that **desire alone doesn't move the rock**.

But neither does the absence of desire.

You need an intention.

Energy.

Attention.

A workable method.

And the ability to adjust according to what happens.

Now apply that to suffering.

You want to become free from anger.

Fine.

But merely wanting isn't enough.

You must understand its conditions.

Cultivate restraint.

Develop appropriate attention.

Investigate the assumptions sustaining it.

Recognise what weakens it.

And continue until the relevant causes are uprooted.

The rock of suffering isn't moved by a beautifully worded declaration about the emptiness of rocks.

It requires practice.

And that is why the third group of awakening factors is so important.

## 15. Four bases for accomplishment: Desire isn't the enemy when it knows its job.

Nanananda identifies four bases supporting successful practice.

Interest or intention.

Energy.

Collectedness or determination of mind.

Investigation.

And here we return to the apparent paradox that has haunted this entire series.

How can desire help end desire?

Because not all motivations function identically.

A wholesome intention to practise is not necessarily the same as compulsive craving for possession.

The desire to cross a river can motivate you to construct a raft.

But once you've crossed, that specific intention has fulfilled its purpose.

Likewise, the wish to become free can support the development of the path.

It need not remain as an eternal psychological demand.

Nanananda connects these four bases to the capacities developed through the four right efforts and shows how they prepare the ground for the next set of spiritual faculties.

Now imagine a beginner announcing:

"All desire is bad."

"Therefore I must not want to practise."

"But I want to stop wanting."

"Which is itself wanting."

"So perhaps I should want not to want to stop wanting."

And within four minutes they've invented the world's least productive philosophical recursion.

Nanananda's answer is simply to understand the function of intention.

**You can employ a conditioned motivation for a liberating purpose without pretending the motivation itself is the goal.**

That is why the path is pragmatic.

It isn't demanding that you instantly behave like someone who has already completed it.

It provides methods appropriate to the conditions you actually possess.

And Nanananda's final point in Sermon 31 is that these methods begin producing something stronger.

Not merely temporary efforts.

Stable faculties.

Confidence.

Energy.

Mindfulness.

Concentration.

Wisdom.

These become increasingly capable of guiding the practitioner.

And that's where Sermon 32 begins.

But first, the practical consequence of the entire sermon is worth stating.

**Do not confuse the desire for liberation with liberation itself. But do not pretend you can dispense with the path simply because liberation is the ending of desire.**

The raft must be built.

The rock must be moved.

The mind must be trained.

And when the work is complete, the tool doesn't have to remain a burden.

That is the middle path.

Not a compromise between doing everything and doing nothing.

A sequence of skilful activities which eventually makes compulsive activity unnecessary.


# Sermon 32 - The thirty-seven factors assemble, and the prison finally has a functioning exit

## 16. The five faculties: Now your practice needs a balanced management team.

Nanananda resumes from the development of the four bases for accomplishment.

He now examines five spiritual faculties.

Confidence.

Energy.

Mindfulness.

Concentration.

Wisdom.

And the interesting thing is that they aren't simply five independent achievements.

They must work together.

Nanananda argues that their specific functions emerge from the earlier training, with mindfulness playing an especially important balancing role.

Now imagine a spiritual institution run by five managers.

Confidence is enthusiastic.

"THIS PATH WORKS!"

Energy is already running around preparing everything.

Concentration wants everybody to shut the heck up.

Wisdom keeps asking whether anyone has understood the problem.

And mindfulness is trying to prevent the whole operation from turning into a disaster.

This is a surprisingly useful model.

Because the faculties can become unbalanced.

### Too much confidence without wisdom

Imagine someone who has tremendous faith in a particular teacher.

They accept every claim.

Defend every interpretation.

Dismiss every objection.

And believe that doubting anything is a spiritual failure.

They possess confidence.

But the confidence isn't sufficiently governed by discernment.

It can become dogmatism.

The path doesn't require replacing ignorance with unquestioning certainty.

It requires understanding.

### Too much analysis without confidence

Now imagine someone who has read every Buddhist text.

They know every scholarly disagreement.

Every problematic recension.

Every contested translation.

They can explain why every possible interpretation is questionable.

But they've become incapable of actually practising.

Nothing is ever sufficiently certain.

Every meditation technique must first survive another six-month literature review.

Their scepticism may contain legitimate intellectual caution.

But it can also become paralysis.

The question isn't whether scholarship is valuable.

It is.

The question is whether the person's relationship to uncertainty still allows intelligent action.

### Too much energy without concentration

Now imagine somebody practising meditation with extraordinary intensity.

They never stop.

Every session is an effort to achieve something.

Every thought is an obstacle.

Every moment of distraction is treated as defeat.

They become tense.

Restless.

Exhausted.

The effort has become self-defeating.

They're trying to cross the flood by violently attacking the water.

### Too much concentration without energy

The opposite problem.

The practitioner becomes calm.

Very calm.

Extraordinarily calm.

The meditation cushion has become a magnificent place to spend the afternoon doing absolutely nothing.

But the relevant understanding isn't developing.

The person is comfortable.

Not necessarily liberated.

And this is why Nanananda thinks the balance matters.

**The practice needs enough energy to investigate, enough calm to see clearly, enough confidence to proceed, and enough wisdom to know what is being done.**

Mindfulness helps maintain the balance.

It remembers the task.

Recognises the current condition.

And enables appropriate adjustment.

But here's the subtle point.

The goal isn't to manufacture five maximally intensified mental states.

It's to cultivate a coherent capacity directed towards liberation.

A powerful engine without steering is dangerous.

Excellent steering without an engine won't move the vehicle.

And an engine and steering system operated by somebody who has no idea where they're going can produce an extraordinarily sophisticated journey to the wrong destination.

Nanananda wants the whole system aimed at Nibbana.

### Faith becomes something deeper

At the mature stage of the faculties, confidence is not merely enthusiasm.

Nanananda connects it with the qualities of the stream-enterer: confidence grounded in understanding of the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha, together with conduct appropriate to the noble path.

His discussion draws upon the *Indriya Samyutta*, the collection on the spiritual faculties.

Now here's why this matters.

An initial interest in the teaching can motivate practice.

But as understanding deepens, the confidence changes.

It's no longer merely:

"I hope this works."

It increasingly concerns what has been understood and verified through the path.

And the development is not simply psychological optimism.

Within the Buddhist framework, stream-entry represents a real transformation in the disciple's relation to the fetters that sustain bondage.

One needn't pretend that an ordinary moment of calm establishes stream-entry.

The doctrinal claim is considerably stronger.

But Nanananda's functional point is clear.

Confidence, effort, mindfulness, concentration and wisdom should reinforce one another.

And now he introduces an especially interesting image.

A log of wood.

Which sounds rather less exciting than cosmic consciousness.

But is actually much more useful.

## 17. The man carrying a heavy log discovers that effort is a question of balance

Imagine carrying a long log.

One end is thick.

The other thin.

The weight isn't distributed evenly.

You might think you can simply lift it from the middle.

But the geometric midpoint isn't necessarily the point of balance.

You have to feel the weight.

Adjust your grip.

Shift your position.

And discover the appropriate relationship between the forces involved.

Nanananda uses this image to explain why the faculties must be balanced through practical investigation rather than merely understood in theory.

Now apply it to meditation.

You're becoming restless.

Perhaps the mind needs tranquillity.

You're becoming dull.

Perhaps investigation and energy need strengthening.

You're becoming overconfident.

Perhaps more critical examination is required.

You're becoming excessively doubtful.

Perhaps you need to return to the practical evidence and cultivate confidence through experience.

The response depends on what's happening.

And mindfulness provides the awareness necessary to know.

**The middle path isn't always doing the same amount of everything. It's responding intelligently to the conditions.**

That's extraordinarily important.

Because people often imagine spiritual discipline as a fixed level of effort.

You must always practise harder.

Or always relax more.

Or always concentrate.

Or always investigate.

But those are crude instructions.

The skilled practitioner understands what is required.

The same person may need to cultivate more energy in one situation and more calm in another.

The criterion isn't ideological allegiance to effort or relaxation.

It's what helps the relevant factors develop.

And that leads Nanananda to another important distinction.

### The five faculties become five powers

As the faculties become established and resistant to their opposites, they are described as powers.

The same five qualities remain.

Confidence.

Energy.

Mindfulness.

Concentration.

Wisdom.

But now they possess greater stability.

Nanananda treats this as part of an ascending development rather than merely a repetition of terminology.

Imagine somebody learning a skill.

At first, they can perform it only under favourable circumstances.

They need constant reminders.

The technique is fragile.

But through practice, the capacity becomes more reliable.

It can operate under more demanding conditions.

It's no longer destroyed by every small disturbance.

The underlying skill has been consolidated.

That's a useful analogy for the faculty becoming a power.

But now another danger appears.

You can become very capable and still use the capability for the wrong end.

A powerful concentration can support wisdom.

Or, if accompanied by conceit, become another basis for spiritual pride.

So what directs the power?

Again, right view.

And the intention towards release.

Nanananda is absolutely insistent that the entire system must remain oriented towards seclusion, dispassion, cessation and relinquishment.

Without that orientation, spiritual capacities can become possessions.

And we know what happens when possessions become identities.

The old machinery starts running.

So the path needs another group.

Seven factors of awakening.

And these are particularly beautiful because they explain how understanding, energy, joy, calm and equanimity progressively support one another.


## 18. Seven awakening factors: The process begins doing some of the work for you.

The seven factors are mindfulness, investigation, energy, joy or rapture, tranquillity, concentration and equanimity.

Nanananda discusses them as a coherent process.

Mindfulness makes the relevant experience available.

Investigation examines it.

Investigation can arouse energy.

Energy supports the emergence of wholesome joy.

Joy can lead to tranquillity.

Tranquillity supports concentration.

And concentration culminates in a balanced equanimity.

The movement is described in the discourses, and Nanananda uses it to explain how the path becomes progressively refined.

Now here's what makes this magnificent.

You don't necessarily have to manufacture every factor independently.

Appropriate conditions can support the next factor.

And this is precisely what dependent arising means from the perspective of liberation.

We've spent most of the series examining how conditions produce suffering.

Contact can condition feeling.

Feeling can become craving.

Craving becomes clinging.

Clinging sustains becoming.

But the Buddhist path also investigates a different direction of conditionality.

The cultivation of skilful conditions can support wisdom and freedom.

**Dependent arising isn't merely a theory explaining how the prison gets built. It also helps explain how the conditions for leaving it can be cultivated.**

That's the key.

Now imagine a practitioner investigating an unpleasant feeling.

At first, the experience is confusing.

They feel irritated.

Want the discomfort to end.

And become entangled in the reaction.

But mindfulness recognises what's happening.

Investigation begins distinguishing the physical sensation from the interpretation and craving surrounding it.

Understanding deepens.

The mind becomes less confused.

And the reduction of confusion may itself produce relief.

Energy becomes easier to sustain.

The body and mind may settle.

Concentration improves.

The person becomes more capable of observing without compulsive reaction.

This isn't a mechanical sequence guaranteed to occur every time somebody notices a sensation.

Nor is the final equanimity of an arahant established by a few moments of calm.

But it illustrates the direction of the path.

**You cultivate conditions that make wisdom easier, and wisdom helps weaken the conditions that previously produced bondage.**

This is the practical genius of the teaching.

It doesn't demand that ignorance disappear before practice becomes possible.

It provides a way to work with the conditions actually present.

And it doesn't require you to treat the resulting joy as another possession.

The joy is useful.

The tranquillity is useful.

The concentration is useful.

But their function is to support the ending of craving.

Not to become the final object of craving.

### The final factor: Equanimity isn't apathy.

Nanananda places particular importance on equanimity.

When concentration is established, the practitioner doesn't need to keep violently adjusting it.

A balanced watchfulness becomes possible.

But this isn't indifference.

The mind remains alert.

Capable of understanding.

Not merely numb.

And Nanananda connects this balance with the possibility of seeing phenomena according to their actual conditions.

Now remember the carpenter.

At first, he was planing down rough surfaces.

Constant adjustment.

Careful inspection.

Effort.

But suppose the work is now smooth.

He doesn't need to keep planing the same surface forever.

At some point, additional effort would damage what has already been accomplished.

The skilful action changes according to the stage of the task.

That is why the path cannot be reduced to endless exertion.

Effort has a function.

And when the relevant work is accomplished, continuing the same effort may be inappropriate.

**The purpose of effort isn't to establish an eternal identity as somebody who makes effort. It is to accomplish the work.**

And this brings us to the final group of the thirty-seven factors.

The Noble Eightfold Path.

Not another collection of independent spiritual trophies.

The coordinated structure through which the training reaches completion.


## 19. The Noble Eightfold Path: Eight factors, one direction.

Nanananda now assembles the entire training.

Right view.

Right intention.

Right speech.

Right action.

Right livelihood.

Right effort.

Right mindfulness.

Right concentration.

But he makes an important argument.

These factors don't operate as eight isolated techniques.

They support one another.

And in the *Mahacattarisaka Sutta*, MN 117, the Buddha describes noble right concentration as supported by the other seven factors.

The corresponding Chinese *Madhyama-agama* passage, MA 189, likewise preserves the importance of these supporting factors.

Now consider what that means.

You cannot simply practise concentration while ignoring your conduct.

You cannot cultivate ethical restraint while remaining completely indifferent to the views guiding your decisions.

You cannot develop insight while continually feeding the intentions that produce bondage.

The training is integrated.

And Nanananda gives right view particular priority.

Why?

Because your understanding of the problem determines the direction of your actions.

### The wrong map makes every step more efficient in the wrong direction.

Imagine you're travelling.

You possess extraordinary stamina.

A magnificent vehicle.

Excellent equipment.

And tremendous determination.

There's just one problem.

You're going the wrong way.

Now suppose you improve your vehicle.

Travel faster.

Increase your stamina.

Develop more sophisticated navigation methods.

The result?

You become extraordinarily efficient at reaching the wrong destination.

The problem isn't insufficient effort.

It's the direction.

And this is why right view matters.

If you imagine liberation is the acquisition of an eternal consciousness-state, your meditation may become organised around producing one.

If you imagine happiness consists in maximising pleasurable experiences, your efforts may become directed towards refining sensual enjoyment.

If you imagine the self must be purified through violent self-denial, you may undertake increasingly extreme forms of mortification.

And in each case, the effort may be impressive.

But the goal may be misconceived.

Nanananda emphasises that right view guides intention, speech, action, livelihood and the meditative factors towards the actual ending of craving.

Now this is where his interpretation becomes especially powerful.

He distinguishes right view which supports wholesome conduct and the accumulation of merit from supramundane right view directed towards Nibbana.

The first is indispensable within the gradual path.

The second leads beyond continued becoming.

These are different levels of function.

And the point isn't that ordinary moral conduct becomes worthless.

It remains necessary.

But **even the accumulation of merit is not identical with the final cessation of craving**.

The path ultimately concerns a deeper freedom.

### Why the middle path isn't fifty per cent pleasure and fifty per cent self-torture

Nanananda makes another cutting distinction.

The Buddha's middle path isn't a compromise in which you take half of sensual indulgence and half of self-mortification.

That would be absurd.

"Monday: exquisite pleasures."

"Tuesday: punish the body."

"Wednesday: return to sensual delights."

"Thursday: sleep on a bed of nails."

"Friday: enlightened balance achieved."

No.

The Buddha rejects the assumptions supporting both extremes.

The path is a distinct way of understanding and cultivating freedom.

Nanananda further associates the extremes with opposing views about existence and annihilation. That particular mapping is his interpretive extension; the Buddha's first sermon itself identifies the extremes principally as sensual indulgence and self-mortification.

But the practical point is secure.

**The middle path isn't an average of two bad methods. It's a method that avoids the error underlying both.**

And its culmination is the ending of craving.

Now here's the part which makes the entire series coherent.

The Noble Eightfold Path is conditioned.

It's cultivated.

Developed.

Supported by causes.

And yet it leads towards Nibbana, which the Buddhist teaching describes as unconditioned.

How?

By using conditioned activities to remove the conditions responsible for bondage.

The path doesn't have to become an eternal entity.

It has to fulfil its purpose.

Just as the chariots aren't the destination.

And the raft isn't the farther shore.

### Eight factors become ten at completion

Nanananda draws attention to the *Mahacattarisaka Sutta* distinction between the eight factors of the trainee's path and the ten factors associated with completion.

The additional two are right knowledge and right liberation.

The trainee cultivates the path.

The arahant possesses the completed knowledge and release.

This is a canonical distinction, preserved in the Chinese parallel MA 189 as well.

And here the philosophical structure becomes extraordinarily elegant.

Right view guides the path.

The factors mutually support one another.

Wisdom penetrates the conditions of suffering.

The defilements are abandoned.

And liberation is realised.

**The path culminates not in an infinite continuation of practice, but in the completion of its liberating function.**

You don't keep climbing the ladder after reaching the roof.

You don't keep rowing after crossing the river.

You don't keep planing a surface once the task is complete.

And you don't need to keep becoming liberated once liberation has been realised.

But now Nanananda raises a final question.

What exactly makes the eightfold path so comprehensive?

Why does the Buddha describe it as the culmination of the other awakening factors?

He answers through the image of the sky.


## 20. The sky through which every useful wind can blow

Imagine the sky.

Winds move through it.

From different directions.

Some cold.

Some warm.

Some gentle.

Some powerful.

The atmosphere provides the context within which all those movements occur.

Nanananda invokes a discourse comparing the comprehensive development of the Noble Eightfold Path with the various winds moving through the sky.

The point is that when the path is cultivated in the relevant liberating manner, the other groups of awakening factors develop towards fulfilment within it.

And this is an important culmination.

The practitioner doesn't have to treat the thirty-seven factors as thirty-seven unrelated obligations.

They form a coherent system.

Mindfulness supports understanding.

Understanding guides effort.

Effort cultivates skilful capacities.

The capacities strengthen.

The factors of awakening develop.

And the Noble Eightfold Path gives the entire system a direction towards Nibbana.

But Nanananda insists that the orientation is crucial.

The factors must be cultivated in connection with seclusion, dispassion, cessation and relinquishment.

Otherwise they may remain useful conditioned achievements without fulfilling their final liberating purpose.

**The path is not a collection of spiritual abilities. It's the training of a mind towards the ending of grasping.**

Now comes the final question of Sermon 32.

If the eightfold path contains the essence of the thirty-seven factors, how exactly does the Buddha describe the moment when all these dimensions converge?

Nanananda turns to the *Greater Discourse on the Six Sense Bases*, MN 149.

And for the first time in the entire finale, the Buddha gives a beautifully direct answer.

The senses.

Contact.

Feeling.

Craving.

And the difference between experiencing with ignorance and experiencing with wisdom.

Everything we have studied for thirty-two sermons.

Right where we live.

Right where suffering arises.

Right where the prison is being built.

**And right where it can stop.**


# Sermon 33 - The final stitch: How craving sews a universe together

## 21. The Buddha finally shows you the exact place where the prison is built.

Thirty-three sermons.

And Nanananda doesn't finish by announcing an unimaginable cosmic revelation.

He returns to the most ordinary thing in the world.

**Seeing something.**

The *Greater Discourse on the Six Sense Bases*, MN 149, describes two fundamentally different ways of encountering experience.

One governed by ignorance.

The other informed by wisdom.

And the extraordinary thing is that the distinction begins before you've become involved in an elaborate personal drama.

It begins with the senses.

An eye encounters visible forms.

Visual consciousness arises.

Contact occurs.

Feeling follows.

And the mind either understands the process according to its actual conditions or begins becoming infatuated with it.

The pattern applies equally to hearing, smelling, tasting, touching and mental experience.

Nanananda concludes his discussion of the Noble Eightfold Path by taking up this precise teaching. Analayo's comparative materials include the Chinese parallel SA 305, which preserves the contrast between attachment through ignorance and the diminution of grasping through understanding.

Now here's why this is the decisive answer to the whole series.

We have spent thousands of words investigating the construction of the world.

The role of consciousness.

The apparent independent owner.

The machinery of proliferation.

The consequences of craving.

The illusion of an eternal self.

The metaphysics of Nibbana.

And the stages of practice.

But everything comes back to something you can investigate.

An experience occurs.

What happens next?

### The ordinary sequence

Imagine somebody says something unkind.

Sound is heard.

Its meaning is recognised.

Unpleasant feeling arises.

The mind becomes involved.

"That person insulted me."

"They don't respect me."

"My status is threatened."

"I must respond."

Suddenly the experience is no longer simply an encounter.

It's a possession.

A wound to an imagined independent owner.

And because the owner must be defended, the mind generates further actions.

Resentment.

Arguments.

Imagined conversations.

Plans for vindication.

The whole machinery begins operating.

Now suppose the response develops into hatred.

You act destructively.

Damage a relationship.

Regret the action.

Become ashamed.

And then begin constructing a further identity around being a person who behaves badly.

An entire sequence of suffering has arisen from a particular encounter.

This is not simply the automatic consequence of sound.

It involves conditioned processes of feeling, interpretation, craving and identification.

The Buddha's discourse says that when these are not understood, attachment grows and the conditions of renewed suffering are strengthened.

Within the traditional framework, this also contributes to the continuation of becoming across lives.

### The alternative sequence

Now imagine the same encounter.

Sound is heard.

Meaning is understood.

Unpleasant feeling arises.

But the mind recognises the experience as conditioned.

The sense faculty.

The object.

The consciousness.

The contact.

The feeling.

The interpretation.

The movement of craving.

The whole arrangement is investigated.

And instead of automatically treating the experience as an injury to an eternal owner, the practitioner recognises its changing and dependent character.

They may still respond.

Perhaps the criticism is accurate.

Perhaps it's malicious.

Perhaps they need to establish a boundary.

But the response need not be driven by the compulsive demand to defend an independent self.

And the discourse describes the resulting decrease in attachment, agitation and the conditions supporting renewed becoming.

**This is the difference between an experience becoming fuel and an experience becoming an opportunity for understanding.**

That's the entire practical revolution.

And now Nanananda makes an important point.

The Noble Eightfold Path is not separate from this investigation.

It is fulfilled through it.

## 22. The thirty-seven factors converge in the moment you stop lying to yourself about experience.

The Buddha explains that when experience is known and understood in the relevant way, right view, intention, effort, mindfulness and concentration develop together.

Ethical conduct has already been cultivated as a foundation.

And as the Noble Eightfold Path is developed, the other awakening factors also reach fulfilment.

Serenity and insight work together.

Analayo's notes show that SA 305 preserves this overall structure, although the Chinese recension differs in the ordering and wording of some elements.

Now this is beautiful.

Because it means the thirty-seven factors aren't thirty-seven arbitrary spiritual achievements you need to collect like rare trading cards.

They're a coherent system directed towards the transformation of how experience is understood.

The body is observed.

Feelings are recognised.

Unskilful states are restrained and abandoned.

Skilful states are cultivated.

Confidence and energy develop.

Mindfulness and concentration deepen.

Wisdom penetrates conditionality.

And the whole process converges upon the ending of the defilements.

But Nanananda makes the final distinction extraordinarily precise.

The Buddha identifies four tasks associated with the completed path.

**Understand what must be understood.**

**Abandon what must be abandoned.**

**Develop what must be developed.**

**Realise what must be realised.**

In MN 149 these correspond respectively to the aggregates subject to grasping; ignorance and craving; serenity and insight; and knowledge and liberation.

Nanananda takes this as a culminating explanation of the integrated Buddhist path.

Now notice how radically different these tasks are.

You don't have to destroy the aggregates.

You have to understand them.

You don't have to accumulate craving.

You have to abandon it.

You don't have to abandon serenity and insight before they've served their purpose.

You have to cultivate them.

And you don't have to fabricate liberation as another possession.

You have to realise it.

**Confusing these tasks is how spiritual practice becomes an enormous flipping mess.**

People try to destroy experience when they should understand it.

They try to cultivate the very desires they should abandon.

They abandon practices before those practices have done their work.

And they attempt to manufacture Nibbana as though it were another conditioned state.

Nanananda's final practical synthesis keeps the distinctions clear.

And that brings us to an important point about mindfulness.

### Why noticing is not enough

You can notice your anger.

But noticing doesn't necessarily mean you understand its conditions.

You can notice craving.

But noticing doesn't necessarily mean you're no longer acting upon it.

You can notice a sense of self.

But noticing doesn't necessarily mean the conceit of self has been uprooted.

And you can notice thoughts cease.

But temporary silence doesn't necessarily mean the relevant defilements have been eradicated.

The path includes investigation, ethical action, effort, concentration and liberating wisdom.

**Mindfulness supplies an indispensable basis for understanding. It is not a magical replacement for the entire path.**

And this is why Nanananda resists treating the four foundations of mindfulness as an isolated method sufficient without reference to the other factors.

His argument is that the entire training is directed towards the same end.

The extinguishing of craving.

The relinquishment of grasping.

The ending of the conditions of suffering.

But we now have a final philosophical problem.

If the senses produce the distinction between subject and object, what exactly is it that craving does to turn the distinction into bondage?

Nanananda ends all thirty-three sermons with one extraordinary image.

A seamstress.

And she's been quietly working behind the scenes the entire time.


## 23. The seamstress of existence has been stitching you together for an eternity.

Imagine a sewing machine.

The needle descends.

The shuttle moves.

The thread passes through the material.

A stitch is formed.

Then another.

Then another.

And eventually separate pieces of fabric become joined into one apparent object.

A garment.

A complete structure.

Now imagine removing the thread.

The needle can still move.

The shuttle can still operate.

But the stitches no longer hold.

The material passes through.

The binding fails.

Nanananda returns to this image in Sermon 33 and makes it the culmination of the entire series.

**Craving is the seamstress.**

It stitches together the relationships through which conditioned experience becomes an ongoing world of possession, identity and renewed becoming.

Now think about the relationship between consciousness and its objects.

Ordinarily, you imagine two independent things.

There's *me*, the observer.

And there's *the world*, observed.

The world contains desirable objects.

Threatening objects.

Possessions.

Other people.

Possible achievements.

And the observer is continually attempting to secure itself through them.

The whole system is organised around the relation between the two.

But Nanananda argues that the apparent solidity of the opposition itself depends upon conditional processes.

The senses.

Their objects.

Consciousness.

Contact.

Feeling.

Perception.

Craving.

And the subsequent activity of grasping.

And the key is that the mind habitually overlooks the conditionality of the relationship.

It treats the two poles as independent things.

Then attempts to bind them through possession.

The seamstress goes to work.

**"THIS EXPERIENCE MUST BELONG TO ME."**

Stitch.

**"THIS PLEASURE MUST CONTINUE."**

Stitch.

**"THIS INSULT THREATENS MY EXISTENCE."**

Stitch.

**"THIS MEMORY DEFINES WHO I AM."**

Stitch.

**"THIS FUTURE ACHIEVEMENT WILL FINALLY COMPLETE ME."**

Stitch.

And gradually the mind has constructed an entire garment.

An identity.

An apparently continuous personal existence.

A world of things which must be acquired, defended or escaped.

And then it becomes trapped inside its own tailoring.

The seamstress has created the costume.

And the actor has mistaken the costume for an eternal self.

But Nanananda isn't merely offering a clever metaphor.

He draws on a genuine canonical discussion.

One that makes the image considerably more profound.

## 24. Six enlightened monks argue about the middle, and the Buddha says they're all right.

The Buddha gives a compact instruction in the *Questions of Tissametteyya*, in the *Sutta Nipata*.

The teaching concerns understanding two ends without becoming attached to what lies between them.

A group of elder monks later discusses its meaning.

They offer six different interpretations.

One concerns contact, its arising and cessation.

Another concerns past, future and present.

Another concerns pleasant, unpleasant and neutral feeling.

Another places consciousness between name and form.

Another places consciousness between the internal and external sense fields.

The last concerns the aggregates subject to grasping, their arising and their cessation.

In each interpretation, craving is identified as the seamstress.

The monks approach the Buddha.

And he acknowledges the validity of their interpretations while explaining that his intended meaning particularly concerns contact, its arising, its cessation and craving.

Nanananda treats this dialogue as a synopsis of his entire philosophical argument.

Now imagine the discussion.

Six senior monks.

Six interpretations.

All sophisticated.

All apparently competing.

And somebody decides to ask the Buddha which one is right.

An excellent idea.

The world's most important philosophical referee is available.

They approach.

Explain the disagreement.

And the Buddha's response is effectively:

"All of you have said something useful."

Which is extraordinarily inconvenient for anybody hoping to establish a personal identity around having won the debate.

But the teaching isn't suggesting that every conceivable interpretation is equally valid.

The six explanations illuminate different aspects of the same underlying problem.

**How does craving bind conditioned phenomena into the experience of an independent, continuing world?**

Nanananda works through the interpretations.

And the result is the most comprehensive final account of bondage in all thirty-three sermons.

### First interpretation: Contact and its arising conceal cessation.

An experience occurs.

The mind recognises it.

Another follows.

Then another.

And the entire sequence becomes a continuous story.

But the mind is habitually fascinated by the arising of experience.

It notices the presence of things.

Their apparent continuity.

Their recurrence.

And it fails to attend sufficiently to their ceasing.

Nanananda argues that craving helps stitch successive occurrences together into an apparently continuous structure.

The present event becomes part of a story of possession and becoming.

And the cessation of the event is obscured.

Now consider a pleasant sensation.

It arises.

Changes.

Disappears.

But you begin longing for its return.

The object no longer needs to be presently available for craving to continue organising your experience around it.

**The desire has outlived the sensation.**

And this is how conditioned occurrences can become the materials of a much larger psychological structure.

But the teaching points towards understanding arising and ceasing together.

Not merely chasing what appears.

The cessation is part of the truth of the process.

And seeing that can undermine the compulsion to continue grasping.

### Second interpretation: The past and future become one enormous imaginary possession.

Think about memory.

Something happened ten years ago.

The event has ended.

But the mind remembers it.

Interprets it.

Replays it.

And constructs an identity around what happened.

Now think about the future.

Something may happen tomorrow.

The event has not occurred.

But the mind imagines it.

Fears it.

Desires it.

Prepares itself psychologically.

And begins identifying with the imagined outcome.

Past and future become joined through the activity of craving.

The mind constructs an apparently continuous owner who must carry the past and secure the future.

But the actual processes of remembering and anticipating occur in the present.

Nanananda uses the second interpretation to emphasise the importance of examining present conditioned phenomena without being carried away by the possessive elaborations of past and future. He connects it with the *Bhaddekaratta Sutta* and its attention to present experience.

Now here's the practical cut.

You're anxious about what might happen next month.

But the anxiety is present now.

You remember something painful from childhood.

But the remembering is occurring now.

The mind may be constructing a narrative across decades.

Yet the relevant processes of feeling, perception and identification can be investigated as they occur.

**The prison's story may extend across your entire lifetime. The activity of constructing it is occurring through conditions you can examine.**

And that's extraordinarily important.

Because it gives you a practical point of entry.

You don't need to solve every event in your past before recognising the activity of grasping.

Nor guarantee every possible future before cultivating freedom.

You investigate the conditions through which the mind is presently binding itself.

But now the seamstress has another trick.

Pleasure and pain.

### Third interpretation: You are trapped on a seesaw and calling it happiness.

Imagine a seesaw.

One side rises.

The other falls.

Then they reverse.

Again.

And again.

The movement continues.

And suppose someone announces:

"At last! I have discovered permanent happiness."

Then points to whichever side happens to be elevated.

The problem is obvious.

The elevated position depends upon the movement of the system.

It cannot remain permanently elevated merely because somebody likes being up.

Nanananda uses pleasant and unpleasant feeling as the two poles of another dependent relationship.

The ordinary mind pursues the pleasant.

Rejects the unpleasant.

And becomes bound by the alternation.

But the third type of feeling-neither pleasant nor unpleasant-presents another danger.

It can be overlooked.

Or misunderstood.

Or accompanied by ignorance.

The canonical discussion involving the nun Dhammadinna connects pleasant and painful feeling, neutral feeling and ignorance, knowledge, liberation and Nibbana.

Analayo's materials include the Chinese parallel MA 210, which retains the contrast between feelings and knowledge but differs in the precise arrangement of the concluding relationships.

Now this is important.

**The way beyond the seesaw is not simply to remain permanently on one side.**

Nor to manufacture the perfect neutral feeling.

It's to understand the entire relationship of feelings and craving.

Pleasure need not become something you must secure.

Pain need not become a verdict against your existence.

Neutrality need not become unconscious indifference.

And the mind can cultivate wisdom that understands all three without becoming enslaved to their alternation.

But now we reach the fourth interpretation.

And Nanananda returns to the very deepest problem of the series.

Consciousness.

Name.

Form.

And the apparent owner who seems to stand behind them.


## 25. The dog returns one final time-and still hasn't figured out the flipping reflection.

Imagine the dog on the plank.

The same unfortunate animal we've met repeatedly throughout the series.

It looks into the water.

Sees another dog.

Becomes interested.

Then suspicious.

Then angry.

And now the entire situation has become an encounter between two apparently independent beings.

But what lies between the dog and its image?

The reflecting water.

Nanananda uses the analogy for the fourth interpretation of the canonical verse.

Name and form appear as two distinguishable poles.

Consciousness is placed in the middle.

And the apparent separation becomes the basis for the delusion of an independent observer confronting an independent object.

He links this with the reciprocal dependence of consciousness and name-and-form.

Now this is one of Nanananda's most ambitious interpretations.

The early Buddhist discourses certainly analyse consciousness as dependently arisen and connected with name-and-form.

But treating consciousness as *the very discrimination* between the poles is Nanananda's philosophical reconstruction.

It is not an uncontested definition accepted across all Buddhist traditions.

And we should not confuse it with a claim that all external physical reality is merely a projection of individual consciousness.

The practical point is more precise.

**The existence of an experience does not establish the independent existence of the owner and object imagined through it.**

The reflection appears.

But the second dog isn't there in the way the first dog assumes.

The dog can understand the reflection without destroying the water.

Likewise, consciousness can be investigated without inventing an eternal consciousness-substance behind the process.

And the very movement of appropriation can cease when its dependent character is understood.

That is the insight Nanananda wants to make intelligible.

But now the fifth interpretation takes the same problem into the ordinary senses.

### The fifth interpretation: The internal and external are joined by an imaginary ownership.

The internal sense faculties constitute one side.

The corresponding external objects constitute the other.

Consciousness arises dependent upon the relevant conditions.

And craving turns the relationship into a structure of possession.

*I see that.*

*I want that.*

*That belongs to me.*

*I must not lose it.*

The mind takes a conditioned encounter and builds an existential claim around it.

Nanananda argues that the apparent gap between internal subject and external object becomes a basis for the compulsive stitching of grasping.

But once again, we must be careful.

The Buddhist analysis doesn't require denying ordinary distinctions between an organism and its environment.

Those distinctions remain useful.

It questions the additional claim of independent ownership and selfhood imposed upon the relationship.

You can recognise an object without requiring it to establish your existence.

You can use the senses without making every sensory encounter part of an endless project of becoming.

And that's precisely what the living arahant demonstrates.

The body continues.

The senses function.

But the old grasping no longer binds.

Now the final interpretation concerns the very entity we've been trying to locate throughout all ten chapters.

The person.

### The sixth interpretation: The person is a conditioned process, not an eternal proprietor.

The sixth elder interprets the two ends through the aggregates subject to grasping and their arising, with their cessation as the middle.

Nanananda connects the aggregates' arising with desire and attachment, and their cessation with the abandonment of that desire.

He interprets the apparently compact person as something established through grasping at conditioned processes.

Now remember the question from Chapter V.

How does a process become somebody called *me*?

The mind experiences the body.

Feeling.

Perception.

Intention.

Consciousness.

And then constructs an independent owner.

The owner acquires a past.

A future.

A collection of possessions.

A reputation.

A spiritual trajectory.

And an enormous anxiety concerning its eventual destruction.

The entire structure is supported through the activity of appropriation.

But the supposed independent owner cannot be established outside the processes through which it is imagined.

And so the Buddhist response isn't to discover a more magnificent owner.

Nor to annihilate an eternal owner.

It's to understand the conditionality of the processes and end the grasping that makes them into bondage.

That's the point.

And now Nanananda gives his final image.

The sewing machine.

The seamstress.

The thread.

And the possibility that the whole garment can finally unravel.


## 26. The final stitch is not made. The whole flipping operation ends.

Imagine the sewing machine again.

The needle descends.

The shuttle moves.

The cloth passes through.

And the thread binds the material.

Stitch.

Stitch.

Stitch.

The garment takes shape.

Now imagine the thread runs out.

The mechanism can continue moving.

The needle can continue descending.

But the stitches no longer hold the fabric together.

The binding has lost its support.

Nanananda compares this with the cessation of craving.

He explicitly returns to craving as the seamstress which binds conditioned existence into renewed becoming.

And here's the extraordinary significance.

The problem was never simply that movements occurred.

The needle could move.

The shuttle could move.

The fabric could pass through.

But the thread made the binding possible.

Likewise, ordinary experience can occur without the compulsive grasping through which it becomes renewed bondage.

An arahant can see.

Hear.

Think.

Eat.

Walk.

Speak.

But the greed, hatred and delusion that sustained the relevant bondage have been uprooted.

The machinery of ordinary life continues until its remaining conditions are exhausted.

But the binding is no longer being produced.

And this brings us back to the exact distinction between living liberation and final Nibbana.

The living arahant is free from the defilements.

The organism continues functioning.

At death, its conditioned aggregates cease.

And within the Buddhist account, there is no further rebirth because the conditions sustaining becoming have been eradicated.

**The absence of renewed bondage is not a new possession. It is the ending of the binding.**

Now here's the final danger.

The mind can hear this and immediately construct another identity.

"I understand."

"I have discovered the truth."

"I am the person who knows that existence is stitched together."

And now the sewing machine has started again.

The insight has become a possession.

The possession has become an identity.

And the identity has generated another reason to defend itself.

But Nanananda's entire project is directed against that movement.

Even understanding the seamstress must not become another garment.

Even the doctrine of non-identification must not become an identity.

Even Nibbana must not become a thing for an eternal owner to possess.

**The purpose of the teaching is not to construct the ultimate correct story about yourself. It is to end the bondage sustained by the need for such a story.**

And this is where Nanananda ends his thirty-three sermons.

Not with a triumphant declaration that he has personally discovered the ultimate metaphysical secret.

Not with a new theory of eternal consciousness.

But with a reminder that much of the confusion surrounding Nibbana comes from failing to understand dependent arising and the middle path.

He then dedicates whatever merit has arisen from the sermons to his elderly, ailing teacher who had invited him to deliver them, wishing for his recovery and the fulfilment of his Dhamma aspirations.

Think about that ending.

Thirty-three sermons.

An extraordinary philosophical investigation.

Hundreds of canonical references.

Difficult textual problems.

Disputes with classical commentarial interpretations.

One of the most ambitious modern reconstructions of early Buddhist liberation.

And the final movement is an act of gratitude.

The teachings return to the human relationship that occasioned them.

No grandiose assertion of spiritual superiority.

No immortal ego celebrating the complete conquest of ontology.

Just the work of the teaching, offered for the welfare of others.

And that is exactly where our own final chapter should end.

But before we finish, there is one question we have to answer.

The one you've been asking from the beginning.

**Why the heck should anyone actually want Nibbana?**


# The final word - You have been trying to escape suffering by producing more of its causes.

Consider your life.

Not as a philosophical abstraction.

Your actual life.

You wake up in the morning.

Before you've even left the bed, the mind is already working.

Things to do.

Problems to solve.

Money to earn.

People to contact.

Responsibilities.

Regrets.

Plans.

Desires.

Fears.

And somewhere beneath all of it, an assumption so deeply ingrained that you almost never notice it.

**Something must happen before I can finally be secure.**

Something must be obtained.

Something must be completed.

Some uncertainty must disappear.

Some person must love you.

Some achievement must be recognised.

Some truth must be discovered.

Some state of consciousness must be attained.

And then, perhaps, you will finally be at peace.

But look at the structure.

You imagine a future state.

You make it necessary.

The necessity creates dissatisfaction with the present.

You begin striving.

Sometimes the striving succeeds.

Sometimes it fails.

But even success introduces new possibilities for loss.

You acquire something valuable.

Now it must be protected.

You build an identity.

Now the identity must be defended.

You become knowledgeable.

Now your knowledge can be challenged.

You achieve a beautiful state of consciousness.

Now you become afraid of losing it.

You find someone you love.

And because love matters, the possibility of separation becomes painful.

Ordinary life contains real beauty, meaning and ethical responsibility. Buddhism does not require denying any of that.

But the mind has been adding something.

A demand for unconditional security through things that cannot provide it.

And then it wonders why it is anxious.

**You're trying to extract permanence from impermanence through an activity that continually makes you dependent upon what changes.**

That is the problem.

And the Buddha's teaching is so radical because it doesn't merely promise a better collection of experiences.

It questions the entire project.

What if happiness isn't ultimately secured through acquiring enough?

What if the suffering is not simply that you haven't found the correct possession?

What if your compulsive search for completion is itself helping reproduce the feeling of incompleteness?

What if you've been mistaking the activity of grasping for the fundamental activity of being alive?

And what if that activity can cease?

Not through destroying the world.

Not through hating the body.

Not through denying responsibility.

Not through suppressing thought.

Not through discovering an eternal witness that permanently owns all experience.

But through understanding the conditions of grasping so profoundly that its underlying causes are eradicated.

That's Nibbana as Nanananda has been trying to make it intelligible.

The stilling of the preparations that sustain bondage.

The relinquishment of the possessions through which the mind attempts to secure itself.

The ending of craving.

Dispassion.

Cessation.

Release.

Not a more magnificent prison.

**The end of imprisonment.**

## Why the path is worth undertaking

Because the alternative isn't merely having a few more uncomfortable feelings.

Within the traditional Buddhist understanding, the alternative is the continued operation of the entire machinery of suffering and rebirth.

The mind remains governed by ignorance.

Craving continues.

Clinging sustains becoming.

And the cycle goes on.

That rebirth claim is a traditional religious doctrine, not something established beyond dispute by contemporary scientific evidence.

But even before resolving every question about rebirth, the immediate psychological and ethical significance is immense.

You can investigate how desire becomes compulsion.

How a feeling becomes a story.

How a story becomes an identity.

How an identity becomes something that must be defended.

How the defence creates conflict.

And how understanding can weaken the entire sequence.

The path gives you a method.

Ethical conduct to restrain the actions that reinforce suffering.

Mindfulness to recognise the relevant processes.

Right effort to prevent and abandon unskilful states and cultivate skilful ones.

Concentration to stabilise the mind.

Wisdom to understand conditionality.

And non-identification to prevent even the path's achievements from becoming new possessions.

The practice doesn't require pretending that every unpleasant experience can be instantly dismissed.

Nor that serious trauma, illness or external hardship can be solved through a philosophical slogan.

It is a gradual, demanding training.

But it has a clear direction.

**Less bondage through less ignorance and grasping.**

And the deepest promise is that the ending can be complete.

Not simply a better mood.

Not merely a calm afternoon.

The eradication of the defilements that sustain the cycle.

The traditional achievement called arahantship.

That's why Nibbana is worth wanting.

But the desire to realise it must eventually fulfil its purpose.

And this is the final paradox.


## The desire that puts an end to the need for desire

You begin because you want freedom.

Of course.

Something about the ordinary project of existence no longer satisfies you.

Perhaps you've experienced profound suffering.

Perhaps you've noticed the instability of pleasure.

Perhaps you've seen how rapidly identity becomes a source of fear.

Perhaps you've discovered that even the most beautiful experiences cannot provide a permanent guarantee of peace.

So you become interested in liberation.

That interest is useful.

It motivates study.

Ethical commitment.

Meditation.

Investigation.

You begin learning the path.

And through the path, the mind gradually understands the very mechanisms through which it has been bound.

But the aim isn't to become eternally fascinated with Buddhism.

It's not to construct the world's most sophisticated philosophical identity.

It's not even to remain forever someone who is trying to become liberated.

The aim is the ending of the relevant causes of bondage.

And when the task is complete, there's nothing left for that compulsive project to accomplish.

**The desire for liberation has led towards the ending of the desire that perpetuates bondage.**

That doesn't make the path self-contradictory.

It makes the path instrumental.

The chariot gets you to the next chariot.

The raft gets you across the river.

The carpenter's plane removes the rough surfaces.

The investigation exposes the illusion.

And the cessation of craving brings the compulsive sewing to an end.

The tools have done their work.

Now listen carefully to the distinction that I think gives the entire ten-part series its philosophical unity.

**Nibbana is not a prize awarded to the most successful self. It is the release from the ignorance and grasping through which the self is made into a prison.**

And that's why you cannot simply obtain it in the ordinary way.

The act of trying to possess it can become another manifestation of the problem.

But neither can you refuse to practise.

The causes of bondage must be understood and uprooted.

The work is real.

The training is necessary.

And the ending is possible within the Buddhist account.

So where does that leave us?

With one final question.

The question that began everything.


## What the heck is Nibbana?

Not a magnificent place where an eternal soul will spend the rest of infinity.

Not the complete annihilation of a substantial self.

Not an infinitely intense pleasure.

Not a permanent state of ordinary concentration.

Not the mystical discovery that an individual consciousness was secretly the universal Self all along.

Not a philosophical position you can claim as your own.

And not merely the intellectual understanding that everything is impermanent.

**It is the cessation of craving and the liberation realised when the conditions sustaining bondage have been brought to an end.**

Nanananda's distinctive interpretation strongly emphasises the cessation of the constructed experiential world and the special attainment of arahant fruition.

Classical Theravada gives a different account of the unconditioned status of Nibbana and the consciousness which takes it as an object.

Analayo's comparative scholarship shows that some of the canonical passages central to Nanananda's argument have genuinely difficult variant readings and alternative explanations.

Those disagreements matter.

We shouldn't pretend every metaphysical question has been conclusively resolved.

But the central teaching which has animated all thirty-three sermons is unmistakable.

The Buddha identifies ignorance and craving as causes of suffering.

He teaches a path through which they can be abandoned.

And he presents their complete cessation as liberation.

That's the practical core.

And now here's the final cut.

You've spent your life assuming that peace will arrive when the world finally becomes what you want.

When the body is safe.

The future secure.

The relationships guaranteed.

The identity admired.

The knowledge complete.

The consciousness permanently established in the perfect state.

And the Buddha has been patiently explaining that none of those conditions can provide what craving demands.

Not because life lacks beauty.

Not because pleasure is forbidden.

But because no conditioned possession can secure an independent owner forever.

So you have a choice.

Continue attempting to build the perfect arrangement.

A more comfortable prison.

A more magnificent self.

A more impressive collection of experiences.

And keep wondering why every achievement produces another reason to be afraid.

Or investigate.

Right here.

In the next moment of wanting.

The next moment of aversion.

The next moment when an experience becomes something that must belong to *you*.

Notice what is happening.

Understand its conditions.

Cultivate what is skilful.

Relinquish what sustains bondage.

And continue until the underlying ignorance is uprooted.

Not because the present moment is secretly an eternal metaphysical absolute.

Not because everything is already perfect.

But because the conditions of suffering can be known, and what is conditioned can cease when its supporting causes cease.

And now think of the magician's theatre.

The audience is sitting in darkness.

The show is magnificent.

Every illusion appears convincing.

They cheer.

They cry.

They become terrified.

They fight over what the performance means.

Some attempt to possess the beautiful characters.

Others attempt to escape the frightening ones.

Some begin constructing an elaborate philosophy of the projector.

Others announce that the theatre must be eternal.

Others insist it will be annihilated.

And everybody is absolutely absorbed.

Then somebody turns on the lights.

And the extraordinary thing is that the light doesn't need to defeat the magician.

It reveals the conditions of the performance.

The audience can see the stage.

The machinery.

The wires.

The projected images.

And the entire production loses the power it possessed through misunderstanding.

Now remember the dog.

The dog is still standing on the plank.

Staring into the water.

Growling at the apparent enemy.

It has been there for the entire ten-part series.

The poor sod.

Fighting.

Defending itself.

Attempting to defeat the other dog.

And the extraordinary thing is that no second dog has ever been there.

The dog doesn't need to win the fight.

It doesn't need to destroy its reflection.

It doesn't need to become the infinite cosmic Dog beyond all forms.

It simply needs to understand the reflection.

And then the fight can end.

That's the whole flipping thing.

Not the acquisition of the ultimate victory.

**The ending of the fight that never needed to happen.**

The carpenter puts down the plane.

The king leaves the final chariot.

The traveller releases the raft.

The children abandon the sandcastles.

The magician's audience sees the trick.

The whirlpool subsides.

The seamstress puts down her needle.

And the mind no longer has to keep constructing a permanent owner out of what continually changes.

The Buddha's path is not an invitation to become the greatest prisoner in existence.

It's an invitation to understand the prison so completely that its causes can cease.

So don't merely admire the teaching.

Don't simply collect the metaphors.

Don't spend the rest of your life becoming the world's foremost expert on the theory of letting go.

Study.

Practise.

Investigate.

Develop wisdom.

And see what can actually be relinquished.

Because the final achievement is not that you become somebody who possesses Nibbana.

It is that the causes of bondage have ended.

**Nothing more needs to be made into *me*. Nothing more needs to be made into *mine*. And nothing more needs to be compulsively acquired in order to make an imagined owner complete.**

The work is done.

The burden is laid down.

The mind is stilled.


**End of Chapter X. End of all 33 sermons.**

## Textual grounding and qualifications

The finale follows the actual order of Nanananda's final five sermons. The primary source is the complete text of The Mind Stilled. Analayo's annotated Sermon 29, Sermon 30, Sermon 31, Sermon 32 and Sermon 33 were used for comparisons.

Important distinctions remain: Nanananda's identification of certain thought-free states with arahant fruition is not universally accepted; the Chinese parallels sometimes differ in their accounts of noble silence and the stages of purification; the thirty-seven awakening factors are canonical, while his claim that they form a strictly ascending sequence is an interpretive synthesis; and his analysis of consciousness as occupying the division between name and form is a distinctive philosophical proposal rather than an uncontested early Buddhist definition.

The recurring dog, carpenter, raft, chariots, whirlpool and seamstress were used in connection with Nanananda's attested discussions. Modern examples and comic dialogue are original narrative illustrations, not historical quotations.


The ten-chapter reading is complete. The optional afterword translates the philosophical argument into a structured, source-grounded practice.
