# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter IX of X · The Magic Show · Nanananda's Sermons 25-28, with Analayo's comparative materials

## Why does the Buddha compare consciousness to a magic show?

**Because consciousness can produce a completely convincing experience without establishing the independently existing things-and the independent experiencer-that we imagine behind it.**

And the truly extraordinary feature is that understanding the trick doesn't necessarily require destroying the performance.

You can still see.

Hear.

Think.

Understand language.

Recognise people.

Respond intelligently.

But the whole structure no longer needs to become the possession of someone called *me*\.

That's the distinction Nanananda has been developing through twenty-four sermons.

And now he's going to take it apart with a magician, a dog, an orchestra, an enormous philosophical argument about truth, a game of chess that becomes a matter of life and death, a parasitic creeper, and finally the destruction of the entire cosmic stage upon which we've been performing our drama.

The progression of these four sermons is exact.

Sermon 25 asks why the Buddha can know what everyone else knows without becoming trapped in their ideas of truth and falsity.

Sermon 26 introduces the magician and reveals how understanding the mechanism destroys the illusion's authority.

Sermon 27 asks why that understanding transforms the entire relationship between signs, craving and existence.

And Sermon 28 turns it into the beginning of an actual path: how to examine the source of suffering rather than spending your life being fascinated by its products.

But first, we have to demolish an extraordinarily persistent spiritual misunderstanding.

**The Buddha isn't claiming that nothing happens. He's challenging what we think the happening establishes.**

And there's an enormous difference.


# Sermon 25 - The Buddha who knows everything, but refuses to buy any of it

## 1\. The greatest philosophical mystery: why doesn't the Buddha take a position?

Chapter VIII ended with the *Kalakarama Sutta*\.

The Buddha declares that the world of sights, sounds, sensations, thoughts and ideas is known to him.

Not merely encountered.

Understood.

Yet he refuses to become established upon it through the usual grasping.

Now imagine an ordinary philosopher hearing this.

"Excellent. So you know what the world knows?"

Yes.

"And you've understood it more profoundly?"

Yes.

"Therefore you must possess the ultimate correct position."

Not in the way you imagine.

"BUT HOW CAN YOU KNOW WITHOUT TAKING A STAND?"

And there it is.

The confusion which Nanananda spends Sermon 25 dismantling.

The ordinary philosopher assumes that knowledge naturally culminates in possessing a viewpoint.

You investigate something.

You discover an explanation.

You become convinced.

Then you establish yourself as the person who knows.

And if someone challenges the explanation, the entire structure begins defending itself.

The argument has become personal.

The understanding has become property.

You are no longer merely investigating whether a claim is correct.

You are protecting *your* claim because the claim has become part of *you*\.

Nanananda argues that the Buddha possesses a deeper understanding precisely because he does **not** undergo that additional appropriation.

Now that's a very different picture of enlightenment.

Ordinarily, we imagine the supreme knower as a cosmic intellectual who possesses every conceivable truth.

An infinite database.

Complete knowledge of everything.

The ultimate metaphysical Wikipedia administrator.

But Nanananda's emphasis is not simply the quantity of information.

It is the freedom of knowing without requiring knowledge to establish an independent knower.

And suddenly the *Kalakarama Sutta* becomes an astonishing investigation of the difference between **knowledge** and **possession of knowledge**\.

### The philosopher who has become his own argument

Imagine somebody spends ten years developing a theory of consciousness.

It's sophisticated.

Supported by research.

They know the literature.

They can defend its central propositions.

Wonderful.

Then somebody presents a serious objection.

The appropriate intellectual response is to investigate it.

Perhaps the objection is wrong.

Perhaps it reveals a weakness.

Perhaps the theory requires revision.

But now suppose the philosopher is personally identified with the theory.

The objection arrives.

Their stomach tightens.

They become defensive.

They begin searching for reasons to reject it before they've properly understood it.

Why?

Because the claim isn't merely a proposition anymore.

It is a possession.

And the proposition's possible falsification now threatens the person.

**The truth has been converted into an identity-maintenance project.**

That's the mechanism Nanananda is exposing.

But notice an important qualification.

The Buddha's freedom from dogmatic attachment does not mean that every proposition becomes equally valid.

Nor that evidence is irrelevant.

Nor that an awakened person cannot make a definite statement.

The Buddha does precisely that throughout the discourses.

He identifies suffering.

Explains its conditions.

Distinguishes skilful from unskilful conduct.

Teaches a path.

What he does not need is the self-defining attachment that makes disagreement into a threat to an independent owner.

And this is why Nanananda thinks the *Kalakarama Sutta* is so radical.

It isn't simply presenting a superior proposition.

It's exposing the possessive mechanism through which propositions become bondage.

## 2\. Four ways to describe knowledge. The Buddha refuses the trap.

Now Nanananda returns to the fourfold structure we encountered in our discussion of death.

Does the Buddha know the world?

Does he not know it?

Does he both know and not know it?

Does he neither know nor not know it?

At first glance, this resembles the famous four alternatives about the Tathagata after death.

But the *Kalakarama Sutta* has a particularly peculiar structure.

The Buddha affirms his knowledge of the world's sensory and conceptual content.

Then he distinguishes that knowledge from the possessive standpoint ordinarily associated with claiming knowledge.

And the remaining alternatives fail to describe the distinctive character of that understanding.

Nanananda reads the passage as deliberately exposing the inadequacy of an ordinary fourfold classification of knowledge.

But here's an essential scholarly qualification.

**The second declaration has different readings in the textual tradition.**

One reading has the Buddha reject the claim that he *doesn't* know.

Another, adopted in the commentary, can be read as rejecting the claim that he *does* know.

That makes a tremendous difference to the apparent argument.

Nanananda strongly prefers the first, on the grounds that the Buddha has already affirmed his knowledge. The lecture materials preserve Bhikkhu Bodhi's alternative translation and discuss the textual disagreement.

So we should be careful.

The neat interpretation of all four declarations as a philosophical structure is Nanananda's reading of a difficult text.

It is not a universally agreed philological fact.

But the deeper canonical problem remains.

**How can someone know what appears without becoming enslaved to the appearance?**

That is the question.

And Nanananda thinks the answer is concealed in an even more extraordinary part of the discourse.

The Buddha does not construct an independent thing behind what has been seen.

Nor does he posit an independent seer who owns the seeing.

This is where Bahiya's instruction returns.

We thought we'd finished with the fellow.

Not a chance.

The man has become the philosophical equivalent of a recurring character who appears whenever consciousness attempts to smuggle an eternal owner into the story.

## 3\. The dog who thinks there's another dog inside the water

Imagine a dog crossing a plank over a stream.

It looks down.

There is a dog looking up.

Interesting.

It wags its tail.

The other dog wags its tail.

Perhaps this is a new friend.

Then the dog becomes suspicious.

It growls.

The other dog growls.

Now it's personal.

The animal begins thinking, as dogs presumably do in their own thoroughly canine fashion\:

**"THIS LITTLE SOD IS MOCKING ME."**

The whole encounter has become a conflict.

But there's no second dog.

There's a reflection.

The dog sees an appearance and assumes an independently existing animal corresponding to it.

And because it misunderstands the conditions of the appearance, it continually reinforces the illusion through its own behaviour.

Nanananda explicitly identifies this as a noncanonical fable he uses to clarify the Buddha's teaching.

Now here's the philosophical cut.

The reflection isn't absolutely nonexistent.

Something is visibly present.

Light is involved.

Water is involved.

The dog's eyes are involved.

A conditioned appearance arises.

But the supposed independent animal under the water is not established.

And those are two different claims.

**A dependent appearance can occur without being what it seems.**

Nanananda argues that the ordinary mind repeatedly makes this mistake with consciousness and its objects.

Seeing occurs.

The mind assumes there's a self-existing thing behind the seen.

Then it assumes there's a self-existing seer who possesses the seeing.

And the whole structure becomes a world of independent subjects confronting independent objects.

But the early Buddhist account begins with conditions.

The eye.

Visible forms.

The arising of visual consciousness.

Contact.

Feeling.

Perception.

And further mental activity.

Ordinary consciousness is not treated as an eternal observer exempt from that conditionality.

And the ordinary object *as experienced* is not established independently of the conditions through which it becomes known.

This isn't proof that external physical objects do not exist.

That would be a stronger claim.

The philosophical point is that **the existence of a sensory experience does not establish everything the mind imagines about the independent substance of its object or owner**\.

And once you understand that distinction, the dog becomes an extraordinarily useful teacher.

Not because the dog has achieved enlightenment.

Clearly not.

The fellow is still engaged in international diplomatic hostilities with his own reflection.

But because the illusion shows precisely how ignorance turns an appearance into a compelling independent thing.

### The moment the dog understands

Suppose the dog finally recognises the reflection.

What happens?

It doesn't need to kill the underwater dog.

It doesn't need to develop an elaborate theory of how the underwater dog has merged with the surface.

It doesn't need to become an eternal Witness-Dog contemplating the cosmic unity of all canine existence.

It simply understands what is happening.

And the apparent opponent loses its hold.

That is the contemplative distinction.

**Understanding the conditions of the illusion can end the bondage generated through believing the illusion.**

Nanananda applies this to the Buddha's refusal to imagine an independent seer, thing seen, or thing worth obtaining behind the experience.

But we should distinguish his philosophical interpretation from the textual alternatives.

Bhikkhu Bodhi's rendering emphasises the Buddha's freedom from misconceiving what is seen, unseen, hearable and known, including any independent seer or knower.

Nanananda places greater emphasis on not positing something substantial *apart from* the experience.

His reading depends partly upon contested grammatical and textual decisions.

That's not a reason to discard it.

It is a reason not to confuse a sophisticated interpretation with an uncontested canonical quotation.

And now Nanananda makes the whole problem even more immediate.

## 4\. The orchestra that becomes a possession

Imagine hearing an orchestra.

Violins.

Cellos.

Percussion.

Woodwinds.

Dozens of musicians performing together.

A complex arrangement of sounds.

You call it music.

Perfectly normal.

But now imagine taking the abstraction *music* as though it were a separate substance existing behind the sounds.

Then you become obsessed.

You want to possess the music.

You want the music to continue.

You construct a personal identity around the music.

Perhaps you become offended when somebody criticises it.

What began as hearing has become an extraordinary personal drama.

Nanananda uses the orchestra to illustrate how our concepts can make a collection of conditioned events appear as a single substantial thing.

Now this doesn't mean music is unreal in the ordinary sense.

Nor that music is worthless.

Nor that aesthetic experience is somehow spiritually forbidden.

The orchestra is genuinely playing.

The sounds occur.

The performance can be beautiful.

But the conventional unity called *music* does not require a metaphysical Music-Substance concealed beneath the notes.

And, more importantly, your enjoyment does not require turning the performance into an existential possession.

You can hear the music.

Enjoy it.

Understand it.

Without requiring the music to guarantee your identity.

That's the difference.

Nanananda's point isn't that the Buddha becomes incapable of appreciating sound.

It's that the additional imagination of an independent hearer and an independently possessable object no longer binds him.

Now this makes the practical problem extraordinarily precise.

**What exactly is happening when you feel that an experience has become part of you?**

And the answer takes us to one of the deepest ideas in Sermon 25.

## 5\. Suchness doesn't mean everything is one. It means you stop becoming made of everything.

Nanananda now develops the Buddha's quality of *suchness*\.

Forget the mystical packaging.

The crucial point is wonderfully simple.

The Buddha remains unshaken by gain and loss.

Praise and blame.

Pleasure and pain.

Fame and disgrace.

But Nanananda insists that this steadiness must not be imagined as psychological rigidity.

It's not the attitude of someone who has become a marble statue.

Nor somebody who suppresses every emotion through extraordinary willpower.

He understands the conditioned character of experience and no longer turns it into an independent possession.

Nanananda connects this with **non-identification**\: freedom from becoming psychologically constituted by the objects one grasps.

Now imagine somebody praises you.

The praise is heard.

Its meaning is understood.

You may recognise that the compliment is deserved.

But you don't have to construct an entire superior identity around it.

And imagine somebody criticises you.

The criticism is heard.

Its meaning is understood.

Perhaps it contains useful information.

But you don't have to become a diminished person because another mind has produced an unfavourable judgment.

The experience occurs.

But the additional activity of self-construction need not follow.

**You don't have to become made of whatever happens to you.**

That's the point.

And Nanananda gives a lovely analogy.

### The playground swing that remains upright because it can move

Imagine a revolving playground swing.

The seats are suspended so they can adjust as the structure rotates.

As the swing moves, the seats change their orientation.

But the passengers aren't continually thrown upside down.

Now imagine fixing the seats rigidly to the revolving frame.

The system turns.

And the passengers are abruptly inverted.

A fairly exciting method of discovering why rigidity isn't always stability.

Nanananda uses this to illustrate the resilience of the liberated mind.

Its steadiness comes from **not clinging**, rather than from attempting to hold something permanently fixed.

That's profound.

Ordinarily, you imagine stability requires a firm grip.

Hold on to your identity.

Defend your positions.

Protect your possessions.

Make sure nothing changes.

But the more absolutely you depend upon those supports, the more vulnerable you become to their alteration.

The grip becomes the instability.

And freedom can look almost paradoxical.

The person who no longer requires a permanent foothold can be more unshakeable than the person desperately holding on.

Now the objection arises.

Does this mean the Buddha considers every claim equally true?

Is liberation just an elaborate refusal to take anything seriously?

No.

And Nanananda ends Sermon 25 by confronting exactly this misunderstanding.

## 6\. The philosophical barb: “I know, therefore everybody else is an idiot”

Imagine a debate.

Two people disagree.

Each possesses arguments.

Each believes their interpretation is correct.

So far, ordinary intellectual disagreement.

But now a new movement occurs.

One person begins thinking\:

"My position is correct."

"Therefore my opponent is ignorant."

"Therefore I am superior."

"Therefore the entire purpose of this conversation is to establish my superiority."

And suddenly a discussion has become a fight over identity.

The propositions have become weapons.

The truth has become private property.

Nanananda turns to the early verses of the *Sutta Nipata*, especially the *Culaviyuha*, *Mahaviyuha* and *Pasura* discussions, to explain how dogmatic standpoints become grounds for conflict.

He emphasises that disputants treat their interpretations as private certainties and then attack the interpretations of others.

The Buddha, having understood the conditioned and perspectival background of such views, does not become entangled in their self-defining disputes. \[buddhistinquiry.org; buddhistinquiry.org\]

But the meaning isn't\:

"Nothing is true."

Nor\:

"Logic is worthless."

Nor\:

"Every religious tradition is equally correct."

Those would themselves be broad philosophical positions requiring argument.

The target is the **dogmatic appropriation** through which views become instruments of craving and conceit.

And now Nanananda's central claim becomes beautifully clear.

The Buddha can recognise how a view has arisen.

He can understand the conditions supporting it.

He can identify its errors.

But he doesn't need the position to establish an independent superior self.

That's what makes his knowledge different.

And in the closing movement of Sermon 25, Nanananda finally lifts the bonnet of the machine.

The six sense bases, their objects, contact, feeling, intention and perception all operate dependently.

Nothing in the machine is independently permanent.

And the apparent stability of the resulting perceptual world is precisely what the mind must investigate.

Then comes the decisive canonical image.

The Buddha compares consciousness to a magician's illusion.

Not merely to a reflection.

Not merely to a dream.

A full-blown magic show.

And that's where Sermon 26 begins.

Because now Nanananda stops merely describing the illusion.

He's going to take us behind the stage and show us **how the flipping trick is performed**\.


# Sermon 26 - The man who watched the magic show so carefully that he missed it

## 7\. The magician opens his theatre, and one spectator refuses to be impressed

Imagine an enormous theatre.

The audience arrives.

A magician is about to perform.

There are magnificent decorations.

Exotic lighting.

Mysterious boxes.

Curtains.

Hidden compartments.

And the unmistakable atmosphere of something extraordinary about to occur.

The spectators settle into their seats.

They want wonder.

Astonishment.

The impossible made visible.

But one rather suspicious fellow has decided to conduct an investigation.

He sneaks into a concealed position beside the stage.

From there, he can see the machinery.

The hidden strings.

The false bottoms.

The concealed assistants.

The carefully timed distractions.

The objects prepared in advance.

And as the performance begins, he discovers exactly how the apparent miracles are produced.

The audience gasps.

He sees somebody pulling a string.

The audience is terrified.

He notices the trapdoor.

A woman apparently vanishes.

He watches her step into the hidden compartment.

And gradually something extraordinary happens.

**The magic loses its power over him.**

The audience sees miracles.

He sees conditions.

The audience experiences astonishment.

He understands the preparations.

The audience becomes emotionally involved.

He recognises the mechanism.

Nanananda builds Sermon 26 around this original parable, inspired by the Buddha's comparison of consciousness to a magic show in SN 22.95. The Chinese parallel SA 265 preserves the central simile and elaborates it with an apparent army of elephants, horses, chariots and soldiers.

The canonical discourse compares consciousness to a magical illusion.

Nanananda's hidden spectator, stage machinery and dialogue are his own explanatory development.

That distinction matters.

Because the early text establishes the insubstantiality of consciousness when examined through wisdom; Nanananda is developing a particular account of how ignorance makes appearances compelling.

And the parable is flipping magnificent.

### The friend who thinks you've lost your mind

After the show, the investigating spectator meets an excited friend.

The friend is delighted.

"Did you see that?"

Yes.

"The disappearing woman?"

Yes.

"The impossible levitation?"

Yes.

"The extraordinary miracle with the box?"

Yes.

"But you're not excited\!"

No.

"Were you asleep?"

No.

"Were you not paying attention?"

Quite the opposite.

And here is the paradox\:

**The person who understood the performance most completely was the least deceived by it.**

The friend assumes that a proper spectator must be astonished.

But the investigating spectator's freedom from astonishment does not arise from ignorance.

It arises from understanding.

Nanananda uses this reversal to explain the Buddha's seemingly contradictory statements in the *Kalakarama Sutta*\.

The Buddha knows what the world knows.

But he does not take the same possessive standpoint towards it.

The world's ordinary account of knowing includes becoming entangled in the significance of appearances.

The Buddha's liberating knowledge exposes that entanglement.

And now think about what this implies.

Ordinarily, we assume that increasing knowledge means accumulating more facts about the objects we already believe exist.

You learn more.

Catalogue more.

Describe more.

And become an increasingly informed spectator.

But Nanananda is pointing towards another kind of knowledge.

**Knowledge which changes the apparent nature of the objects because it reveals the conditions through which they appear.**

The magician's elephant can remain visible.

But it no longer represents what the audience imagined.

The effect has been understood.

And the delusion has lost its hold.

### The important limit of the analogy

Don't conclude that ordinary experience is merely fake.

The magician's stage is real.

The spectators are real.

The equipment is real.

The physical events are real.

What is deceptive is the appearance of a supernatural achievement which the actual conditions do not support.

Similarly, an experience can be genuinely occurring without possessing the independent metaphysical essence attributed to it.

The difference between *occurrence* and *independent substantiality* is indispensable.

Nanananda sometimes pushes the language of illusion very far.

But his argument is strongest when read as an investigation of the mistaken substantiality and possessive significance imposed upon conditioned phenomena.

Not as proof that an external physical universe doesn't exist.

And this leads to the greatest question in Sermon 26.

**What happens to the mind's constructions when their supporting ignorance is understood?**


## 8\. The stage machinery still exists. But it no longer produces the same magic.

Imagine the magician has a box.

The audience thinks the box contains an invisible supernatural force.

It opens.

Something appears.

The audience erupts.

But you know there's a concealed compartment.

Now watch what happens when the trick is repeated.

The same box.

The same movements.

The same dramatic gestures.

But you no longer expect a miracle.

The external performance is similar.

Your relation to it is different.

And that's the precise distinction Nanananda wants.

**Understanding doesn't always require the immediate abolition of conditioned activity. It can remove the ignorance through which that activity becomes a basis for bondage.**

Now consider the living arahant.

The body continues.

Perceptions occur.

The senses function.

Thought and ordinary communication remain possible.

But the formations generated under ignorance no longer have the same role in sustaining grasping and renewed becoming.

Nanananda uses the magic-show parable to explain how conditioned activities, recognised as conditioned, cease being the basis for the old delusive construction.

But here we need to be exact.

It would be wrong to say that every formation stops occurring in a living arahant.

The organism continues functioning.

The person can make practical decisions.

Speak.

Walk.

Eat.

Remember.

The distinction concerns the defilements and karmically productive activities that sustain bondage.

So when Nanananda speaks of preparations losing their deceptive power, we shouldn't turn that into a claim that the awakened person has become a motionless biological object.

The magician's equipment remains available.

It simply doesn't command belief in an impossible miracle.

### The deepest difference between the Buddha and the ordinary observer

The ordinary observer sees things.

And immediately attributes independent existence and personal significance.

The Buddha sees their dependence.

And therefore has no need to turn them into possessions.

This is not the difference between someone with eyes and someone without eyes.

It's the difference between **perception accompanied by ignorance** and **perception illuminated by wisdom**\.

And here's the extraordinary consequence.

The same world that serves as fuel for craving can become the occasion for the ending of craving.

That isn't because external objects have become physically different.

It's because understanding changes the relationship through which they become significant.

Which means the path is much more intelligent than merely avoiding everything attractive.

You can examine the object.

Understand its conditions.

Recognise its impermanence.

And cease demanding that it provide unconditional security.

The understanding itself works against grasping.

And that brings us to the structure underlying the entire show.


## 9\. The twelve-link formula is not a blimming shopping list

Nanananda now turns from the magician to dependent arising.

And he makes an important complaint.

People memorise the twelve links.

Ignorance.

Formations.

Consciousness.

Name-and-form.

The six sense bases.

Contact.

Feeling.

Craving.

Clinging.

Becoming.

Birth.

Aging and death.

Very impressive.

You can recite the entire sequence.

Perhaps backwards.

Perhaps at extraordinary speed.

Perhaps with a voice so solemn that everybody in the meditation hall assumes you've personally understood the fabric of existence.

But Nanananda asks\:

**Do you understand the principle, or have you merely memorised the categories?**

He compares the mistake to someone being shown a forest and immediately becoming obsessed with counting individual trees.

The forest is a relationship, a whole organisation of conditions.

You don't understand it merely by naming one tree after another.

Likewise, the twelve-link sequence illustrates a more fundamental principle\:

When the relevant conditions are present, dependent phenomena arise.

When those conditions cease, the corresponding phenomena cease.

Nanananda insists that attention must penetrate this relationship rather than stop at an inventory of the links. \[buddhistinquiry.org; buddhistinquiry.org\]

Now this is extraordinarily important.

Because you can know the entire map of dependent arising while remaining completely enslaved to its operation.

Imagine a person who knows that anger is conditioned.

They understand contact.

Feeling.

Perception.

Interpretation.

Craving.

They can explain the entire sequence.

Then somebody insults them.

And suddenly they become furious.

Why?

Because **knowledge of the list is not identical to insight into the process as it occurs**\.

The magician's assistant can describe every piece of equipment.

But that doesn't necessarily mean they understand how the audience becomes deceived.

And it certainly doesn't mean they've ceased craving the applause.

Nanananda wants the practitioner to examine both arising and cessation.

Not simply\:

"This condition produces that condition."

But\:

"If this condition is absent, what happens to the dependent process?"

That's the genuinely liberating question.

### Why ignorance isn't some eternal first cause

Now here's a subtle point.

The twelve-link formula begins with ignorance.

And it's tempting to assume that ignorance must be the ultimate first cause of everything.

Some kind of metaphysical substance.

A primordial cosmic darkness.

An eternal principle standing outside the chain.

But Nanananda rejects that.

Ignorance itself is conditioned.

The early discourses discuss the influxes that sustain ignorance, and ignorance's reciprocal relationship with those influxes.

So the beginning of the familiar sequence doesn't establish ignorance as an independently existing first substance.

Nanananda particularly stresses that **the principle of conditionality is more fundamental than selecting any one link as the ultimate origin**\.

And now we see why the magic-show analogy matters.

The audience is deceived because the preparations are not understood.

But once the preparations are understood, the illusion loses its authority.

Similarly, insight into dependent arising reveals the conditionality of the processes through which suffering is perpetuated.

The point isn't to identify a cosmic first object.

It's to understand what supports bondage and how that support can cease.

This does not mean the twelve links are merely psychological metaphors without any relationship to rebirth.

The Buddhist teaching includes their role in the continuation of existence across lives.

Nanananda's distinctive emphasis is that the underlying conditionality can also be investigated in present experience.

And this is what turns the teaching from metaphysical speculation into contemplative enquiry.

### The forest you can't see because you've memorised every tree

Suppose you've learned all twelve links.

You're delighted.

Now somebody asks\:

"What happens when a pleasant feeling arises?"

You reply\:

"Well, according to the canonical sequence, feeling conditions craving."

Fine.

"But can you notice the actual movement through which pleasure becomes a demand for repetition?"

You hesitate.

"Can you recognise the assumption that possessing the next experience will finally make you secure?"

More hesitation.

"Can you observe the feeling cease without immediately constructing another object of desire?"

Silence.

And suddenly you realise something.

You have memorised the forest.

But you haven't seen it.

That's the danger Nanananda identifies.

The point isn't to know which tree comes after which.

It's to understand the conditional relations through which the whole forest is established.

And that brings us directly to the most practical aspect of Sermon 26.


## 10\. The machinery that becomes useless when you understand how it works

Nanananda compares the six sense bases to a high-speed machine.

From outside, it appears to be one continuous functioning whole.

A magnificent engine.

But open the bonnet.

You discover moving parts.

Rotating components.

Interdependent mechanisms.

Nothing inside is independently permanent.

And the operation depends upon conditions.

He invokes the *Dvaya Sutta*, which emphasises that the sense faculties, their objects and the experiences arising through contact are all subject to change.

Now suppose your entire identity depends upon the belief that the machine is one solid, unchanging object.

You cling to its apparent unity.

You make its continued operation the guarantee of your existence.

But when you investigate the conditions, the appearance of independent solidity becomes untenable.

The engine doesn't have to be destroyed for you to understand it.

The apparent mystery is dissolved through examining its construction.

And Nanananda thinks the same applies to consciousness.

Ordinary consciousness seems like an independent continuous knower.

But the Buddha analyses it in relation to specific faculties, objects and conditions.

There isn't simply one ordinary sensory consciousness operating as an eternal soul behind everything.

The visual process depends upon its conditions.

The auditory process upon others.

And the mind's participation in the entire arrangement is likewise conditioned.

Now this is where Buddhist anatta remains distinct from an eternal universal consciousness metaphysics.

It doesn't establish an absolute Witness hidden behind the moving machinery.

It asks what justifies positing such a Witness in the first place.

And Nanananda believes a clear understanding of conditionality removes the very need for that assumption.

### The rickety machine with wonderful paintwork

Nanananda gives another practical analogy.

You buy a machine.

It looks magnificent.

Beautifully polished.

Freshly painted.

A very impressive piece of equipment.

You bring it home.

And the next day it breaks.

Turns out the whole blimming thing was held together by appearances.

Now you're furious.

You believed you had acquired something reliable.

Instead, you bought a collection of unstable components beautifully disguised as a permanent possession.

Nanananda uses this to describe the disappointment inherent in conditioned bodily existence when it's treated as a guaranteed source of security.

Again, this isn't a statement that the body is worthless.

Or that ordinary health care is pointless.

The issue is the expectation.

You have acquired a conditioned organism.

It requires food.

Sleep.

Protection.

Continuous physiological regulation.

And eventually it will cease.

Yet the mind insists that the organism must provide unconditional security.

The guarantee was never available.

And the disappointment arises partly because you expected what the conditions could not provide.

**The problem isn't that reality broke its promise. The problem is that craving invented the promise.**

That's the cutting point.

And now Nanananda makes a subtle observation.

Ignorance can become habitual.

Even when the teaching is explained, the mind keeps returning to its old assumptions.

It prefers the show.

It doesn't want the lights turned on.

Why?

Because the show is familiar.

The stories are compelling.

The identities are meaningful.

And the entire machinery has been operating through these habits for an immeasurable period within the Buddhist account of samsara.

Nanananda uses this to explain why even an intelligent practitioner doesn't necessarily eradicate all defilements immediately. The traditional path distinguishes stages of awakening because different fetters and underlying tendencies are abandoned progressively.

And this answers our question from Chapter IV.

Why doesn't one moment of insight permanently stop the machinery?

Because recognising one manifestation of ignorance doesn't necessarily uproot every latent condition that can reproduce it.

The magician has more than one trick.

And the spectator may understand the disappearing woman while remaining completely deceived by the floating elephant.

That's why the path requires cultivation.

But Nanananda has an extraordinary final story.


## 11\. The Buddha who didn't notice a thunderstorm killing six living beings

In the *Mahaparinibbana Sutta*, a man named Pukkusa tells the Buddha about his former teacher, Alara Kalama.

Alara was once sitting beside a road in profound concentration.

Five hundred carts passed.

The noise must have been enormous.

But he didn't hear them.

And Pukkusa is impressed.

Such extraordinary tranquillity.

Such concentration.

Such apparent freedom from sensory disturbance.

Then the Buddha describes an episode from his own life.

A violent storm occurred near Atuma.

Lightning flashed.

Thunder roared.

Two farmers and four oxen were killed.

People gathered.

And the Buddha later explained that he had been awake and conscious but hadn't perceived the storm's sights and sounds.

The canonical narrative presents this as an exceptional attainment of concentration.

Now Nanananda makes an ambitious interpretive move.

He argues that the Buddha was in arahant-fruition concentration, realising the cessation of the six sense spheres.

He rejects the explanation that this was simply the ordinary cessation of perception and feeling, because the narrative explicitly describes the Buddha as conscious and awake.

And he connects it with the difficult passage from the *Kalahavivada Sutta* examined in earlier chapters.

That's an interesting reading.

But we must distinguish the levels of evidence.

The ancient narrative describes extraordinary concentration and the absence of particular sensory perceptions.

It does not explicitly identify the episode as the precise fruition attainment Nanananda proposes.

His explanation depends upon his wider interpretation of the cessation passages.

And those passages are disputed, as we've already seen.

So it would be wrong to claim that the story proves arahant-fruition concentration is always a state in which the six sense spheres cease.

Nor does it provide modern scientific evidence for consciousness operating independently of the senses.

The doctrinal argument can be examined without making those unsupported leaps.

And now here's the actual philosophical payoff.

Even the most extraordinary concentration is not automatically liberation.

A person can become oblivious to an entire procession of carts.

Or a violent storm.

But the question of bondage remains.

**Has ignorance ended? Has craving been uprooted? Has the mind ceased constructing an independent owner around experience?**

That's the criterion.

An impressive meditative feat is not sufficient merely because it is impressive.

And that takes us directly into Sermon 27.

Because Nanananda is about to ask why the knowledge of how the magic works has such extraordinary power to end the desire to keep watching.

Not merely a change in belief.

A transformation of the entire relationship between perception and existence.


# Sermon 27 - The chess game in which two friends accidentally invented life and death

## 12\. The Buddha didn't defeat the magician. He understood the trick.

Nanananda begins Sermon 27 by returning to the two ways of seeing the magic show.

The spectator in the audience sees a miracle.

The investigator behind the stage sees the conditions.

But now he makes an important connection.

This difference corresponds to two directions of dependent arising.

**When the supporting conditions are present, the relevant phenomena arise.**

And\:

**When those conditions cease, the relevant phenomena cease.**

The first explains the appearance of the show.

The second explains why understanding its construction can free the mind from its apparent magic.

Nanananda connects this with the first three discourses of the *Udana*, where the newly awakened Buddha contemplates dependent arising in its forward and reverse directions.

Now this is the crucial point.

A magician performs a trick.

You see the result.

You're astonished.

Then somebody explains how it works.

At first, the trick seemed to violate ordinary reality.

But now you recognise the conditions.

A hidden compartment.

An assistant.

A carefully controlled line of sight.

The apparent miracle is understood as a dependent event.

And what happens to your desire to keep treating it as a miracle?

It weakens.

You may still enjoy the artistry.

You may admire the magician's skill.

But you're no longer compelled by the belief that something supernatural has occurred.

**Understanding the cause changes the significance of the effect.**

And Nanananda believes the same can happen with the ordinary structures of experience.

The body.

The world.

Consciousness.

The apparent observer.

The objects that observer supposedly owns.

All can be investigated through conditions.

And when their dependent character is understood, the additional claims of independent substantiality become untenable.

But the goal isn't merely producing a better explanation of the world.

It's ending the bondage sustained by misunderstanding it.

### The mind doesn't merely need more information. It needs a different relationship to information.

Consider somebody who knows perfectly well that social status is impermanent.

They can explain it.

They can provide examples.

They may have written an excellent essay about the emptiness of reputation.

Then somebody publicly humiliates them.

And suddenly the entire psychological empire mobilises.

They become furious.

They rehearse arguments.

They imagine revenge.

They worry what other people think.

Their theory was correct.

But their relationship to the situation remains governed by grasping.

They haven't genuinely understood the mechanism deeply enough to uproot the relevant tendencies.

Nanananda's argument is that insight must penetrate the conditions supporting that appropriation.

Not merely supply another proposition to defend.

And this leads to a wonderfully sharp question.

**What makes an otherwise ordinary appearance become something the mind cannot stop chasing?**

He answers through the problem of signs.


## 13\. The sign is not the problem. What makes the sign important is the problem.

Imagine seeing an expensive watch.

You recognise it.

A useful object.

It tells the time.

But suppose you've become fascinated by wealth and prestige.

Now the watch carries an extraordinary significance.

It represents status.

Success.

Belonging.

Superiority.

Perhaps the life you want.

And suddenly the object is much more than a device.

It's a symbol of personal completion.

You begin imagining yourself wearing it.

Other people admiring it.

The kind of person you would become.

And the desire grows.

Now here's the key.

**The watch doesn't have to inject the desire into your mind like a chemical syringe.**

Its significance depends partly upon the conditions already present in you.

The interests.

Values.

Fears.

Expectations.

Desires.

And your existing sense of identity.

Nanananda turns to a discussion in the *Mahavedalla Sutta*, MN 43, where greed, hatred and delusion are described as factors that generate signs.

He argues that these tendencies help make appearances psychologically compelling.

This is one of the deepest parts of the sermon.

Because people often assume they desire things simply because the things are desirable.

The object supposedly possesses an independent force.

It appears.

And the desire follows.

But Nanananda is investigating the contribution of the mind.

The object becomes important because it resonates with underlying tendencies.

And those tendencies influence what the mind notices, imagines and pursues.

The object can be conventionally real.

Its practical qualities may genuinely differ from other objects.

But the existential power attributed to it is something additional.

And that additional significance can be dismantled through understanding.

### Why a sign can lose its authority without disappearing

Suppose somebody has been obsessed with a particular status symbol.

They spend years wanting it.

Then they discover how little the symbol can actually provide.

Perhaps they see somebody possessing it and remaining profoundly unhappy.

Perhaps they obtain it and discover the anticipated transformation doesn't occur.

Or perhaps contemplation reveals how the desire has been operating.

Now the object can remain visible.

Its conventional value may remain.

But the psychological significance changes.

It no longer represents the person's imagined completion.

The sign has lost its hold.

And this is why Nanananda argues that the ending of greed, hatred and delusion transforms the relationship between the mind and the perceptual world.

He relates this to the *Magandiya Sutta*\: the liberated person is no longer bound through perceptions and views in the way ordinary people are.

Now a qualification.

The absence of greed, hatred and delusion doesn't mean an arahant loses the capacity to recognise signs.

They can still distinguish a road from a river.

Food from poison.

A friend from a stranger.

The Buddhist claim isn't that all useful perception disappears.

Nanananda's emphasis is that signs no longer function as the basis for delusive appropriation.

**An object can be recognised without becoming an existential demand.**

And that is freedom.

But now an even deeper question appears.

If greed and hatred are what make objects into personal possessions or threats, what happens to the basic division between *me* and *the world*?

Nanananda's answer returns to the most dangerous game ever played by two friends.


## 14\. Two friends build a chessboard. Then somebody introduces a prize.

Imagine two friends.

They're sitting together.

Having a lovely afternoon.

They decide to make a chessboard.

They find some discarded wood.

Paint the pieces.

Construct the board.

And sit down to play.

At first, it's just entertainment.

The pieces are scraps of wood.

Their colours distinguish their roles.

The arrangement creates a game.

But then somebody introduces a prize.

Now winning matters.

The players become more competitive.

They begin watching each other suspiciously.

Every move acquires greater significance.

Then somebody introduces an even more extreme condition.

The winner gets the prize.

The loser will be killed.

Now the atmosphere changes completely.

The two friends are no longer simply enjoying a game.

They're fighting for their lives.

The wooden pieces become immensely important.

Every movement threatens catastrophe.

The board has become their entire world.

Nanananda constructs this deliberately escalating chess allegory in Sermon 27.

Now here's the brilliant philosophical point.

The pieces remain scraps of wood.

The board remains a painted surface.

But the players' relationship to the objects has changed.

Their desire to win.

Fear of losing.

Identification with their respective sides.

And the threat of destruction have made the entire arrangement psychologically overwhelming.

**The importance of the game is not simply present in the wooden objects. It arises through the conditions of participation.**

And suddenly we can understand how a world of conflict is constructed.

A distinction becomes established.

My side.

Your side.

My possessions.

Your possessions.

My victory.

Your defeat.

And the entire psychological structure depends upon preserving the distinction.

### The extraordinary irony: the game needs two sides, but the players have made the sides into enemies

The friends built the board together.

There was no original hatred.

They cooperated.

But once the game begins, their relationship is organised around opposing positions.

And when the stakes become absolute, the opposition becomes existential.

Now Nanananda extends the image to ordinary life.

The mind experiences a world divided into an owner and the things owned.

Self and other.

Gain and loss.

Victory and defeat.

And these distinctions become the basis of craving, conceit and conflict.

He connects the dichotomy to the reciprocal dependence of consciousness and name-and-form. That particular mapping is his interpretive synthesis, not something the chess allegory independently proves.

But the psychological insight is formidable.

**Once identity is invested in one side of a distinction, the other side becomes a potential threat.**

And the harder you defend the distinction, the more compelling the conflict becomes.

Now consider the religious version.

"My tradition is correct."

"Your tradition is inferior."

"My interpretation is ultimate."

"Your interpretation threatens the truth."

And suddenly two people who were ostensibly seeking freedom from craving are fighting to defend their metaphysical flags.

The chessboard has become a monastery.

The king has become a philosophical doctrine.

And the entire institution is preparing for holy war.

### The arahant isn't trying to win the game

Nanananda now introduces a remarkable verse from the *Dhammapada*\.

Victory generates hostility.

Defeat produces misery.

The peaceful person has abandoned the whole opposition of victory and defeat.

Now imagine somebody hearing that.

"So the Buddha recommends deliberately losing?"

No.

That would merely identify with the losing side.

"Then perhaps you should always arrange a draw?"

Closer as an illustration, but not quite.

The issue isn't simply how to conclude the game.

It's the compulsive identification through which winning and losing become statements about what you are.

Nanananda argues that the arahant is free from the very opposition as a basis of existential self-construction.

And this allows us to understand an extraordinary possibility.

You can participate in ordinary activities without requiring their outcomes to establish your independent worth.

You can succeed without becoming possessed by success.

You can fail without becoming defined by failure.

You can defend a proposition without making the proposition your identity.

And you can understand the rules of the game without being psychologically enslaved to the game.

**The freedom isn't merely to win. It's to stop needing victory to make you real.**

Now don't confuse that with apathy.

The Buddha's path involves ethical commitment.

The avoidance of harm.

The cultivation of wisdom.

And appropriate responses to the world.

The fact that victory need not become a basis for conceit doesn't imply that injustice should simply be ignored.

But the underlying psychological bondage can cease.

And Nanananda makes this point even more sharply through the story of Adhimutta, the arahant threatened by bandits.

Adhimutta's fearlessness isn't explained by the belief that his body cannot be killed.

He recognises the aggregates as vulnerable and does not construct an independent owner whose eternal survival must be defended.

That is the philosophical point of the narrative.

But now the chapter takes a major turn.

We've spent twenty-seven sermons examining the nature of the goal.

And Nanananda suddenly announces that it's time to stop merely discussing the target.

**We need to explain how to practise.**


## 15\. The arrow cannot hit a target you've misunderstood.

This is the pivotal transition in the entire 33-sermon series.

Nanananda says that much of what came before has been concerned with correcting misconceptions about Nibbana.

The mind habitually imagines liberation as an exalted form of existence.

A permanent experience.

A transcendent realm.

A superior state of consciousness.

And Nanananda has spent sermon after sermon challenging those pictures.

Why?

Because if you misunderstand the goal, your practice may become directed towards the wrong thing.

You might cultivate extraordinary concentration.

Develop refined experiences.

Become a sophisticated philosopher.

And still remain bound through craving.

In Nanananda's words, the preceding sermons have been the medicine that clears away mistaken understandings; what follows will increasingly explain the path itself.

Now this is incredibly important for our ten-part structure.

Because the question isn't simply\:

"What is Nibbana?"

Nor even\:

"How does consciousness become deluded?"

It's now\:

**"If I've understood the delusion, what exactly should I do?"**

And the answer will not be another grand metaphysical theory.

It will concern how attention is directed.

How one investigates experience.

And how the conditions of craving can be understood at their actual source.

But first, Nanananda asks one final question about the very nature of our defilements.

Do greed, hatred and delusion come from the world?

Or are we misidentifying their origin?

And he introduces two extraordinary images.

A tree destroyed by its own fruit.

And another tree buried beneath the offspring it produces.

### The fruit that kills the tree

Imagine a reed.

It grows.

Develops.

Produces fruit.

But the reproductive process is associated with the decline and death of the plant.

The very structure which grew from the organism becomes implicated in its destruction.

Nanananda invokes a canonical verse comparing greed, hatred and delusion to something that arises from within and harms the person in whom it arises.

And the point is subtle.

Ordinarily, you think\:

"That object made me greedy."

"That person made me angry."

"That situation made me deluded."

But the external event alone doesn't explain the whole reaction.

There are conditions within the mind.

Habits.

Expectations.

Tendencies.

Desires.

And those conditions participate in producing the response.

The relevant object may be a genuine occasion for the reaction.

But it isn't sufficient to explain the entire mechanism.

**The mind is not simply a passive victim of external appearances. It participates in the production of its own bondage.**

Now that doesn't mean other people cannot harm you.

They can.

Nor does it justify blaming victims for their suffering.

The ethical and practical reality of harm must be recognised.

The Buddhist analysis concerns the additional processes through which craving, aversion and delusion arise.

And Nanananda thinks understanding their actual source is the beginning of liberation.

But the most extraordinary image is still to come.


## 16\. The banyan tree that grows so many roots it forgets which one is the trunk

Imagine an enormous banyan tree.

It develops aerial roots from its branches.

The roots descend.

Reach the ground.

Establish themselves.

Grow thicker.

And eventually begin resembling additional trunks.

After enough time, the tree becomes an immense network of supports.

And somebody looking at it may struggle to identify the original central trunk.

The tree has produced so much secondary growth that its original structure has become obscured.

Nanananda uses this image to describe the mind's proliferating constructions.

Thoughts arise.

They attach themselves to objects.

Generate further thoughts.

Establish additional associations.

And eventually the mind becomes so deeply entangled in its products that the conditions from which they arose are no longer recognised.

Think about anxiety.

A simple uncertainty arises.

You interpret it.

Then imagine possible consequences.

The consequences produce further fears.

Those fears lead to additional interpretations.

Eventually you're dealing with an enormous network of apparently independent problems.

But the original conditions have disappeared beneath the proliferating structure.

You're standing inside an entire forest of mental constructions.

And you've forgotten the original seed.

The banyan is a perfect image.

Because the proliferation is not merely a series of random thoughts.

It creates a network that can appear to possess its own independent solidity.

The branches become roots.

The roots become trunks.

The trunks support further branches.

And the entire structure continually reinforces itself.

### The parasitic creeper that devours its own host

Nanananda adds another image.

A creeper begins growing upon a tree.

At first, it's small.

Almost insignificant.

But as it grows, it spreads over the host.

Its branches and leaves become increasingly extensive.

Eventually the original tree can be obscured or strangled.

Nanananda compares this with the way defiling thoughts proliferate around external objects until their source becomes concealed.

Now here's the exact philosophical point.

The mind experiences craving.

Then begins constructing interpretations around the desired object.

Those interpretations become increasingly elaborate.

And eventually the mind becomes convinced that the object itself is the independent origin of all its suffering.

Meanwhile, the craving that helped produce the entire network has become almost invisible.

You're obsessed with the story.

And you've stopped examining the conditions that generated it.

The creeper has covered the tree.

The tiger has eaten the magicians.

The chess game has become a matter of life and death.

And the magician's audience is still staring at the stage, convinced that the impossible has occurred.

Now Nanananda brings us to a genuine textual controversy.

Because the Buddha's verse describing the movement of thoughts has two markedly different interpretations.

One involves children leaving their mother's lap.

The other involves boys tormenting a crow.

### The crow and the children: why the textual disagreement matters

The traditional commentary explains the verse through a game in which children tie a string to a crow, release it and pull it back.

Nanananda dislikes this explanation.

He argues that a variant reading supports the image of children departing from their mother's lap.

He thinks that interpretation fits the idea of mental activities springing from within and becoming attached to external objects.

The commentary's crow, he believes, distracts from the central question about their source.

But Analayo's comparative materials add an important complication.

The Chinese parallel SA 1324 and a Sanskrit witness preserved in the *Yogacarabhumi* also contain an image of infants or children in relation to a nurse or caregiver.

That supports the broader child-and-caregiver imagery.

However, it does not simply establish Nanananda's exact interpretation of children *leaving* the mother's lap; the parallel language can instead suggest dependence or clinging.

So there is a real textual issue.

And we shouldn't resolve it merely by preferring whichever image makes the most elegant philosophical metaphor.

But both explanations direct attention towards something important.

Thoughts arise dependent upon conditions.

They can become entangled with objects.

And that entanglement can obscure their origins.

The practical challenge is to investigate the conditions at the source rather than remain fascinated by the products.

And this is precisely what Sermon 28 develops.


# Sermon 28 - Stop chasing the illusion. Investigate what makes it appear.

## 17\. The beginning of liberation is a very specific kind of attention

Nanananda now makes the decisive transition from understanding the goal to cultivating the path.

He begins with right view.

Not as a collection of theological opinions.

Not as membership in the correct Buddhist institution.

But as an understanding of suffering, its arising, its cessation and the path leading to its cessation.

And then he identifies two conditions supporting the emergence of right view.

Hearing the teaching from another.

And attending carefully to the way phenomena arise through conditions.

Nanananda prefers to call this second activity **radical reflection**-attention directed towards the actual source of an experience.

The accompanying materials compare his rendering with Bhikkhu Bodhi's more conventional translation, *careful attention*\.

And this difference in emphasis is important.

Nanananda isn't merely recommending that you pay more attention.

You can attend very intensely to the wrong thing.

You can obsessively examine the details of your resentment.

Think about them for hours.

Develop increasingly sophisticated explanations.

And still remain completely captive to the original resentment.

You might even become the world's foremost scholar of why a particular person is a complete sod.

A magnificent academic career.

But have you understood the source of the bondage?

Perhaps not.

The relevant investigation asks\:

**What conditions are sustaining this experience of suffering?**

Not merely\:

"What's the content of my anger?"

But\:

"What is generating the anger's compulsive hold?"

And this changes the entire direction of attention.

### The difference between investigating a problem and feeding it

Imagine somebody insults you.

Ordinary proliferation begins.

"What exactly did they mean?"

"Why did they say it?"

"Have they always disliked me?"

"What do other people think?"

"What should I say to restore my reputation?"

Each question may generate additional thoughts.

And the thoughts themselves may deepen the emotional investment.

You're investigating the story.

But you haven't necessarily investigated the mechanism.

Now ask differently.

What was contacted?

What feeling arose?

What did I start wanting?

What identity appeared threatened?

What assumption made the event a statement about my independent worth?

And what happens when that assumption isn't automatically accepted?

The questions point towards the conditions of the experience rather than merely expanding its narrative content.

**Instead of investigating every branch of the tree, you're moving towards its roots.**

That's what Nanananda means by radical reflection.

And it is extraordinarily practical.

Because the mind can become more intelligent without becoming freer.

It can explain its own suffering in exquisite detail while continually reproducing it.

Radical reflection aims to expose the conditions supporting the reproduction.

And Nanananda thinks this leads us back to the whirlpool.


## 18\. The mind invents two sides, then spends its life fighting on one of them

Imagine our two friends playing chess.

The board has two sides.

The pieces are arranged.

The game becomes possible.

But the players forget that the opposition exists within a conditioned arrangement.

They identify with their respective sides.

Then the game becomes a conflict.

And eventually, with sufficient emotional investment, the distinction appears absolute.

*My side.*

*Your side.*

*My victory.*

*Your destruction.*

Nanananda argues that the ordinary sense of self involves a comparable mistake.

The dependent relationship between consciousness and name-and-form is misconceived as the opposition of an independent experiencer and independently possessed objects.

He connects this with the apparently self-generated character of greed, hatred and delusion.

The defilements arise within the system, but become attached to objects that the mind then treats as their independent causes.

Now consider what happens when you're attracted to somebody.

The person appears.

Pleasant feeling arises.

Desire develops.

You imagine a relationship.

The relationship becomes a possible possession.

And the possession becomes part of your future identity.

Eventually you may think\:

"This person is the source of my happiness."

But the person isn't independently manufacturing every feature of your psychological response.

Your expectations are participating.

Your needs.

Your memories.

Your fears.

Your conceptions of what love must provide.

And the entire structure becomes organised around an apparent opposition.

*Me.*

*The person I want.*

Now imagine the relationship becomes uncertain.

You feel threatened.

And begin producing anxiety around preserving the connection.

The suffering is real.

But the source cannot simply be reduced to the other person's existence.

The mind's own conditions are involved.

Nanananda wants the practitioner to understand those conditions.

**The aim isn't to deny relationships. It's to stop treating relationships as possessions which must guarantee the existence of an independent self.**

And this leads to an extraordinarily subtle point.

The self can exist as a conventional designation without being established as an independent metaphysical substance.

You can speak about yourself.

Have responsibilities.

Make decisions.

Form relationships.

But the claim that all this activity must belong to an eternal owner is additional.

And Nanananda thinks the conceit of comparison reveals exactly how that owner becomes established.

## 19\. Superior, inferior, equal: three ways of keeping the self alive

Imagine entering a room full of people.

The mind begins evaluating.

"That person is more successful than me."

"That person is less intelligent."

"We're about equal."

"Perhaps I'm more spiritually advanced."

"That monk looks enlightened."

"I'm probably doing better than him."

And immediately an elaborate social universe appears.

Rankings.

Comparisons.

Expectations.

Anxieties.

Opportunities for pride.

Threats of humiliation.

But notice the extraordinary fact.

**Even equality can become a form of conceit.**

Because the comparison still assumes a person who must be located within a hierarchy.

You don't have to imagine yourself superior.

You can identify as inferior.

Or equal.

The underlying activity of self-measurement remains.

Nanananda brings forward verses from the *Sutta Nipata* describing the sage who no longer constructs an identity through superior, inferior or equal comparisons.

He connects this with abandoning the dualistic structure through which grasping becomes possible.

Now the distinction is important.

You can recognise that somebody possesses greater technical expertise than you.

That is an ordinary factual judgment.

A skilled surgeon may know more anatomy than a medical student.

A Sanskrit scholar may understand a text more deeply than a beginner.

The Buddhist problem isn't the capacity to make useful comparisons.

It's the conceit through which those comparisons become statements about an independently existing person's ultimate worth.

"I am superior."

"I am inferior."

"I am equal."

All three can become identities.

And all three can create bondage.

### The spiritual hierarchy is especially dangerous

Imagine somebody has spent years practising meditation.

They've attained extraordinary states.

They understand difficult teachings.

They've become respected by other practitioners.

And slowly a new identity forms.

**"I am spiritually advanced."**

Now somebody else describes an experience that appears more profound.

Suddenly\:

Threat.

Comparison.

Jealousy.

Defensiveness.

The old machinery has returned.

The mind has exchanged worldly status for spiritual status.

But the underlying self-measurement is unchanged.

Nanananda argues that true non-identification undermines this entire structure.

Not because the liberated person loses the capacity to recognise differences.

But because those differences no longer establish an independent self.

And now his argument reaches an interesting distinction.

If the self is constructed through taking up and rejecting, what happens when neither movement binds the mind?


## 20\. The water pump that accidentally invented an independent self

Imagine a pump.

Water enters.

Water exits.

There are two complementary functions.

Intake.

Discharge.

And because these operations form a recognisable coordinated process, we call the whole arrangement *a pump*\.

Perfectly useful.

But now imagine concluding that there must be an eternal Pump-Soul hidden behind the movement.

A permanent independent pump-entity.

Something metaphysically separate from the mechanism which performs the intake and discharge.

The practical designation has become an unwarranted ontological assertion.

Nanananda uses the water pump to illustrate how the mind treats complementary movements of taking up and rejecting as the activities of an independent unit.

And this is exactly what happens psychologically.

The mind wants pleasant things.

It rejects unpleasant things.

It constructs an identity around those movements.

*I'm the one who wants this.*

*I'm the one who hates that.*

*I'm the one who must preserve my preferences.*

Then it imagines a permanent independent owner behind the preferences.

But the owner is being established through the very processes under investigation.

That is the circularity.

The pump's operations become evidence for a separate Pump-Soul.

And the Pump-Soul is then invoked to explain the operations.

A magnificent piece of circular reasoning.

Now we must be careful.

Nanananda's argument does not prove that organisms lack all forms of agency.

Nor that causal regulation and decision-making are unreal.

It concerns the Buddhist rejection of an independently existing self behind conditioned activities.

The organism can function.

Decisions can arise.

Actions can have moral consequences.

But none of that necessarily establishes an eternal possessor of the process.

And this is why the sage's freedom from compulsive taking up and rejecting matters.

The apparent owner no longer has to be continually reconstructed through those movements.

But Nanananda is about to make this practical in a way that might initially seem rather brutal.

The contemplation of bodily elements.

And the image of a butcher cutting up a cow.


## 21\. The butcher who discovers that the cow isn't an eternal substance

Imagine a butcher.

He has slaughtered a cow.

He divides the carcass into separate portions.

Different cuts of meat.

Bones.

Organs.

The materials of the animal's body.

And as the process continues, the original unified appearance of *a cow* becomes less compelling.

The butcher recognises constituent parts.

Now the Buddha uses this as an analogy for contemplating the body through its material elements.

Nanananda takes up the passage from the *Satipatthana Sutta* and its parallels, arguing that its purpose is to weaken the identification through which the body becomes an independently substantial possession.

But don't misunderstand the analogy.

The Buddha isn't claiming a living animal is functionally identical to a pile of meat.

Nor that the distinction between a living organism and its separated parts has no significance.

Nor that the person should become indifferent to suffering.

The point concerns the *perception of compactness*\.

Ordinarily, you experience your body as a single thing.

And immediately\:

**"This is me."**

"My face."

"My strength."

"My appearance."

"My health."

"My life."

The body becomes the foundation of an independent identity.

But the contemplation investigates its conditioned material character.

Solidity.

Fluidity.

Temperature.

Movement.

These qualities are not uniquely owned by a permanent self.

They are also found outside the organism.

And the body depends continuously upon its environment.

Food.

Water.

Air.

Temperature.

Other material conditions.

Nanananda argues that the distinction between internal and external materiality can be examined without treating the body as metaphysically separate from the wider material world.

He places particular emphasis on the *Mahahatthipadopama Sutta*, MN 28, which instructs practitioners to view the internal and external earth element as the same type of element and not as self or the possession of a self. The Chinese parallel MA 30 preserves the same central analysis.

And now the philosophical cut becomes extraordinarily clear.

**The body is intimately experienced, but intimacy does not establish absolute ownership.**

You can care for it.

Nourish it.

Exercise it.

Treat its illnesses.

Protect it.

But you cannot make it independently permanent merely because you've called it *mine*\.

The claim of ownership doesn't confer sovereign control over its conditions.

And the body will eventually cease.

### Why this isn't a lesson in chemistry

Nanananda now makes a strong criticism of certain later Buddhist analyses.

He thinks the purpose of the elemental contemplation has sometimes been obscured by increasingly elaborate technical classifications of matter.

The practitioner begins with a teaching intended to undermine attachment to bodily form.

Then becomes fascinated by the detailed classification of physical constituents.

And eventually accumulates an entire philosophical system of matter.

Now the danger is that the system itself becomes another object of attachment.

Nanananda argues that the original point was contemplative: to weaken possessive identification and reveal the conditioned nature of bodily form.

But we should distinguish that argument from a dismissal of physical science.

Modern chemistry, physiology and neuroscience are perfectly capable of studying real, empirically testable properties of organisms.

Their scientific value doesn't depend upon whether they directly produce Nibbana.

And the fact that an analysis is not itself liberating doesn't make it false.

The more defensible distinction is\:

**Scientific investigation asks how physical processes operate. Buddhist contemplative investigation asks how identifying with conditioned processes sustains suffering and how that bondage can end.**

Those enquiries may illuminate one another.

But they are not automatically interchangeable.

And neither should be used to dismiss the legitimate achievements of the other.

### The body you love and the body you hate

Now consider something Nanananda points out.

Certain parts of your body are cherished.

Your face.

Your hair.

Perhaps your eyes.

Your hands.

Other parts are treated with disgust.

Waste.

Bodily fluids.

Unpleasant smells.

But all are aspects of the organism's conditioned functioning.

The mind's attachment and revulsion are not determined solely by the material properties.

They are influenced by how the parts are perceived and valued.

And contemplation can reveal that process.

The purpose isn't to become disgusted by the body.

That would simply replace attachment with aversion.

The purpose is equanimity through understanding.

**The material process doesn't need to become an object of craving or hatred.**

And Nanananda insists this is where elemental contemplation becomes genuinely liberating.

But now he makes an even more difficult move.

If the body depends upon food, and food sustains its continued functioning, how can the Buddha speak of abandoning dependence upon food *through food itself*?

That's a wonderfully sharp paradox.


## 22\. You can eat food without becoming its prisoner

Imagine a monk receiving alms food.

The food is nutritious.

Perhaps pleasant.

Perhaps not.

It sustains the body.

The monk eats.

Now ordinarily, a pleasurable meal can produce craving.

The mind wants more.

It compares today's meal with yesterday's.

It begins planning tomorrow's menu.

Perhaps it becomes dissatisfied with the food available.

Perhaps it develops a whole identity around being a person of exceptionally refined taste.

The meal has become a psychological enterprise.

But the Buddha's instructions on the monastic requisites involve a different relationship.

Food is received and used without the same bondage of greed and infatuation.

Nanananda connects this with a paradoxical formulation about using food in order to go beyond dependence upon food. He relates it to the *Ariyavamsa Sutta*, AN 4.28.

This is one of the most practical insights of the series.

You don't have to refuse nourishment to overcome attachment to nourishment.

You don't have to hate pleasurable taste.

You don't have to pretend that the body no longer requires food.

You can use what is necessary while understanding the danger of turning it into a possession.

And the same principle applies to other aspects of life.

Use money without making money the foundation of your identity.

Study philosophy without making philosophical knowledge your independent self.

Develop concentration without treating concentration as an eternal possession.

Enjoy beauty without requiring beauty to guarantee your happiness.

You can participate without becoming psychologically imprisoned by participation.

**The path doesn't necessarily demand the destruction of ordinary functioning. It demands the ending of the grasping that converts functioning into bondage.**

And here we return to your central insight from the beginning of the whole project.

The mind can be directed towards Nibbana because the goal is an undoing.

The contemplation progressively reveals how ordinary things become objects of dependence.

And wisdom allows those relationships to weaken.

The path uses conditioned means.

Attention.

Effort.

Concentration.

Understanding.

But the goal is the ending of the compulsion that keeps reconstructing the prison.

Now Nanananda brings the whole argument to an almost apocalyptic conclusion.

The earth itself.

The stage on which the entire drama of existence unfolds.

Even that will not last.


## 23\. Seven suns, the destruction of the earth, and the end of the flipping theatre

Nanananda ends Sermon 28 by examining two canonical cosmological narratives.

The first is the *Agganna Sutta*, DN 27.

It presents an ancient account of the world developing through a succession of changes, including beings who become increasingly engrossed in sensual enjoyment and acquire coarser forms of existence.

The second is the *Sattasuriya Sutta*, AN 7.66.

That discourse describes a future cosmic destruction in which additional suns appear.

The earth dries.

Rivers disappear.

Oceans evaporate.

Mountains burn.

And eventually the world is consumed by an enormous conflagration.

The central lesson is the impermanence of conditioned phenomena.

Nanananda uses these narratives to undermine the tendency to imagine the physical world as a permanent stage upon which the drama of existence can continue indefinitely.

Now let's distinguish the claims properly.

These are traditional Buddhist cosmological narratives.

They are not scientifically verified accounts of the earth's formation or future evolution.

The ancient description of multiple suns should not be presented as a literal prediction confirmed by astrophysics.

Nor should the *Agganna Sutta* be mistaken for a laboratory account of biological evolution.

The doctrinal significance is what concerns Nanananda.

**Even the stage is conditioned.**

Even the apparently enormous solidity of mountains and continents is not an unconditional guarantee.

Everything upon which ordinary existence depends is subject to change.

And the mind has been trying to establish permanent security through precisely those changing things.

Now imagine the cosmic drama.

A person spends their life accumulating possessions.

Establishing an identity.

Defending their opinions.

Planning their future.

Trying to become somebody important.

And all of it occurs upon a physical stage that is itself impermanent.

The person wants permanence.

The possessions are impermanent.

The body is impermanent.

The social world is impermanent.

The environment is impermanent.

Even the grand cosmic structures through which the world appears substantial are not immune to change.

And yet the mind continues insisting\:

**"SURELY THERE MUST BE SOMETHING HERE THAT CAN MAKE ME PERMANENTLY SECURE."**

That's the tragicomedy.

But Nanananda's answer isn't simply\:

"Everything will be destroyed, therefore despair."

The Buddhist conclusion is different.

Because conditioned phenomena are unreliable as sources of unconditional security, their true character can be understood.

Understanding can give rise to disenchantment.

Disenchantment to dispassion.

Dispassion to release.

The cessation of bondage does not require a permanent physical stage.

It requires the ending of the ignorance and craving through which the stage becomes an existential possession.

And this is why the cosmic destruction narrative belongs at the end of Sermon 28.

It isn't a spectacle intended merely to frighten people into religion.

It is the final dismantling of the assumption that any conditioned construction can provide what craving demands.

Even the entire theatre is impermanent.

**So stop trying to make the theatre guarantee your existence. Understand the performance.**

And when the performance is understood, the magic loses its hold.


# The final cut - The Buddha sees the trick. We keep trying to buy tickets.

Let's gather the four sermons into one argument.

**Sermon 25 asks what makes the Buddha's knowledge different from ordinary knowledge.**

The Buddha can know, recognise and understand without turning knowledge into an existential possession.

He doesn't need a permanent seer behind seeing.

Nor an independently substantial thing behind the seen.

And he doesn't need the identity of someone who possesses the final correct viewpoint.

Nanananda calls this freedom from identification a form of *suchness*\.

It isn't rigidity.

It's the unshakeability that becomes possible when experience no longer has to support an independent owner.

**Sermon 26 asks why the apparent world is so compelling.**

The magic show is convincing because the audience doesn't understand its conditions.

The hidden spectator understands the preparations.

And the apparent miracle loses its power.

Nanananda uses this to explain the relationship between ignorance, conditioned formations, consciousness and the accumulation of grasping.

The point of dependent arising isn't merely to memorise a sequence.

It's to understand the actual conditions through which the show arises and ceases.

**Sermon 27 asks why signs and appearances become such powerful objects of attachment.**

Nanananda argues that greed, hatred and delusion help confer their compelling significance.

The chessboard becomes a world.

The pieces become possessions.

The players become enemies.

And the entire game becomes a struggle over life and death.

But once the conditioned character of the game is understood, its outcomes no longer need to establish an independent self.

And Nanananda then announces the decisive shift.

We've spent enough time clarifying the target.

Now we must understand the path.

**Sermon 28 asks how that understanding can actually begin.**

The answer is right view supported by careful, source-directed attention.

Not merely thinking harder.

Not merely accumulating more facts.

Not merely building an ever more sophisticated philosophy of the world.

But investigating the conditions through which craving, conceit and delusion arise.

The banyan tree.

The parasitic creeper.

The divided chessboard.

The whirlpool.

All describe ways in which conditioned processes become so elaborate that their own sources are forgotten.

And Nanananda's practical response is to return attention to those sources.

Investigate the body.

Its material elements.

Its dependence upon the environment.

The feelings that arise.

The desire that follows.

And the identities constructed around it.

Not to hate experience.

Not to annihilate the body.

But to end the grasping through which the body and the world become the possessions of an imagined permanent owner.

And now the extraordinary conclusion.

**The world doesn't have to be a supernatural illusion for us to be profoundly deceived about it.**

A reflection can genuinely occur without containing the dog imagined beneath the water.

A magician can genuinely perform an act without possessing supernatural powers.

An orchestra can genuinely produce beautiful music without requiring a separate Music-Substance behind the sounds.

A chess game can genuinely take place without the pieces possessing inherent, independent significance as objects of mortal struggle.

And conscious experience can genuinely occur without establishing an eternal observer who independently owns it.

That is the philosophical cut.

The mistake isn't necessarily that nothing happens.

It's that the mind takes what happens and constructs far more than the happening establishes.

Then it becomes attached to those constructions.

And the attachment sustains the appearance of a world in which the imagined owner must continually strive, defend, acquire, reject and become.

The show becomes convincing.

The audience becomes absorbed.

The characters become real in the way the story presents them.

And the spectator forgets the conditions of the performance.

Then somebody begins investigating.

They discover the wires.

The hidden compartments.

The mechanisms.

The dependency.

And suddenly the entire relationship changes.

**The performance is no longer capable of compelling the same belief.**

Now here's why this is more radical than saying\:

"Don't get attached."

Because the teaching isn't simply issuing a command to suppress desire.

It's exposing what makes desire so compelling.

The mind has been responding to its own constructed interpretations as though they were independently established realities.

And Nanananda thinks a sufficiently profound understanding of the conditions can remove the basis of that response.

You don't have to spend eternity fighting each object separately.

You investigate the machinery that continually makes objects into existential possessions.

That's why contemplation works.

That's why right view matters.

That's why the Buddha places such importance on understanding dependent arising.

And that's why the goal is described as the ending of craving.

Not the acquisition of a more magnificent experience.

The undoing of the bondage that made acquisition seem necessary.

But there's one final difficulty.

**We've spent twenty-eight sermons learning what Nibbana is and why the mind doesn't recognise it. What exactly does the Buddha instruct us to do?**

Because understanding the argument isn't enough.

You can admire the magician's mechanism while remaining obsessed with the next show.

You can analyse craving while continuing to feed it.

You can understand the construction of self and then become terribly proud of being the person who understands selflessness.

You can even make Nibbana itself into the ultimate carrot.

So we need something more.

Not another metaphysical theory.

A path.

How to cultivate the understanding.

How to recognise the conditions of suffering as they arise.

How to develop the factors of awakening.

How to use concentration without turning concentration into a possession.

How to transform the desire for liberation into the actual ending of the processes that sustain bondage.

And finally, whether there is a point at which the whole task is complete.

That's Chapter X.

The final five sermons.

Nanananda has cleared away the misconceptions.

He's shown us what freedom means.

And now comes the final question.

**If you've understood that the prison is constructed, are you actually going to stop building it?**

Because the entire purpose of the Buddha's teaching is not to provide an extraordinary intellectual account of the prison.

It's to open the door.

And the most astonishing possibility of all is that the door may not require the acquisition of anything you've been chasing.

It may require putting something down.


**End of Chapter IX - Sermons 25-28 of 33.**
