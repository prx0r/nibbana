# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter V of X · Sermons 13-15 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, with Analayo's comparative scholarship

## How does a process become someone called “me”?

**The answer: Experience doesn't merely happen. Through ignorance, the mind appropriates what happens, imagines an owner, and then spends its life trying to secure that owner's existence.**

But here's the particularly vicious part.

You might discover that your body isn't a permanent self.

You might understand that your thoughts aren't a permanent self.

You might see that perception, memory and consciousness arise through conditions.

Wonderful.

Then you announce\:

**"I am the one who has realised there is no self."**

And the sod has returned.

Not as a body.

Not as a thought.

Not even as an ordinary personality.

Now it's the enlightened observer of everything.

You've evicted the ego from the ground floor, and it's moved into the penthouse wearing a white robe and calling itself Pure Awareness.

Nanananda's analysis is designed to prevent precisely this manoeuvre.

The problem isn't only *what* you identify with.

It's the activity of identification itself.

And the most extraordinary feature of Sermons 13-15 is that Nanananda doesn't merely explain how the imaginary owner is constructed.

He examines how the whole process of ownership can end **without perception, intelligence and ordinary functioning having to disappear**\.

That is the question we're going to pursue\:

If experience no longer belongs to an independently existing someone, what happens to experience?

And how can the mind stop constructing a possessor without constructing a new possessor called *the one who has stopped constructing*?


# Sermon 13 - The enlightened person who doesn't even own Nibbana

## 1\. Three people encounter the same world. Only one is finished with it.

Chapter IV ended with the *Mulapariyaya Sutta*, the Buddha's extraordinary investigation of how people relate to the objects of their experience.

Nanananda now completes that analysis.

The discourse distinguishes three fundamental attitudes: the untaught ordinary person, the noble disciple still training, and the liberated arahant. The Buddha's own freedom is also described, with special emphasis on his complete understanding of craving and suffering.

The objects under discussion range from the elements and living beings to exalted states, the entirety of experience and even Nibbana itself.

What makes the distinction so interesting is that all three people can recognise the same ordinary object.

Imagine a beautiful house.

The ordinary person sees it.

The trainee sees it.

The arahant sees it.

The awakened person has not suddenly lost the ability to recognise architecture.

So what's different?

### The ordinary person

The first person looks at the house.

And almost immediately, the conceptual machinery begins.

"That's a magnificent house."

"I'd love to live there."

"People would admire me."

"Imagine my life if I owned that."

"I would finally feel successful."

At some point, a house has become much more than a structure.

It has become a possible identity.

The mind has converted a visible object into a promise concerning *me*\.

And what follows?

Desire.

Comparison.

Possessiveness.

Anxiety.

The entire psychological universe of acquisition.

The person isn't simply recognising the house.

They're imagining their existence in relation to it.

### The noble trainee

The second person sees the house.

The desire may arise.

Perhaps envy appears.

Perhaps there's an impulse to imagine owning it.

But this person is training.

They recognise that delighting in the object can become the basis of clinging.

They investigate the reaction.

They practise restraint.

They cultivate wisdom.

And here is the crucial distinction.

**The trainee is not liberated merely because they understand the explanation.**

The underlying tendencies have not all been eliminated.

They may still be carried away.

That's why the training remains necessary.

Nanananda particularly emphasises this difference between someone who has begun understanding and someone whose comprehension is complete.

Now consider the practical consequences.

You might understand intellectually that prestige cannot provide unconditional security.

But when somebody prestigious praises you, an enormous wave of pleasure still arises.

You may understand that your identity is conditioned.

But when somebody humiliates you, the whole system of self-defence still comes roaring back.

The knowledge is real.

But it hasn't yet penetrated all the tendencies through which the machinery operates.

This is not necessarily hypocrisy.

It's the distinction between **seeing a mechanism and having fully uprooted it**\.

### The arahant

Now the third person sees the house.

They recognise it.

They can describe it.

They can enter it.

They can explain how it's constructed.

They may even appreciate its usefulness.

But the activity of appropriation no longer takes place in the same way.

The house is not converted into an independent object whose possession will complete an independent owner.

And the arahant isn't constantly issuing instructions to themselves\:

"Don't identify."

"Don't desire."

"Remain mindful."

"Remember that this isn't mine."

That belongs to the training stage.

For the arahant, Nanananda argues, the relevant ignorance has been uprooted.

The compulsion is absent.

**The trainee is learning not to feed the fire. The arahant has exhausted the fuel.**

This distinction is absolutely decisive.

Because otherwise we imagine enlightenment as a permanent psychological effort.

An eternity of holding an extraordinarily sophisticated form of attention.

Every moment requiring vigilance.

Every sensation requiring inspection.

Every thought requiring a counter-thought.

A spiritual security guard working twenty-four hours a day.

But wouldn't that be rather exhausting?

If liberation required a permanent activity of suppressing identification, the activity itself would become another dependency.

Nanananda's point is that the liberated person no longer needs to maintain the specific struggle against the defilements that have been eradicated.

The task has been completed.

And this leads to something deeply uncomfortable.

## 2\. The Buddha does not delight even in Nibbana

Here comes the first genuine philosophical detonation of the chapter.

The *Mulapariyaya Sutta* includes Nibbana among its objects of analysis.

And the Buddha is described as not delighting even in Nibbana.

Excuse me?

The entire Buddhist path is directed towards Nibbana.

It is presented as the supreme peace.

The ending of craving.

The ultimate liberation.

And now you're saying the Buddha doesn't delight in it?

What the heck?

Nanananda thinks this is precisely why the discourse is so important.

**If you make Nibbana into an object that must be possessed, you have misunderstood what liberation means.**

Consider the ordinary spiritual project.

"I want enlightenment."

Fine.

Then\:

"I want the experience of enlightenment."

Still understandable.

Then\:

"I want to become somebody who possesses enlightenment."

Now something has changed.

You've turned liberation into property.

The mind imagines a future owner.

The owner acquires an extraordinary state.

And the state becomes the ultimate guarantee of the owner's existence.

The entire construction is being organised through the very activity liberation is supposed to end.

That's the contradiction.

You've made non-attachment into something to attach to.

The donkey has been promised an eternal carrot.

And now it's running towards the most magnificent carrot in the entire history of agriculture.

### The difference between realising peace and possessing peace

Imagine someone carrying an enormous stone.

Their back hurts.

Their arms are trembling.

Their entire life has become organised around the burden.

Then somebody shows them how to put it down.

They release it.

The burden is gone.

Now ask\:

"Do you possess the absence of the stone?"

What?

"No, seriously. Have you acquired a new object called Stone-Absence?"

No.

The burden has ceased.

The person is no longer carrying it.

The release is intelligible without requiring the creation of another object that now belongs to the person.

Nanananda argues that Nibbana is understood through the extinguishing of craving rather than as a substantial entity which subsequently performs the extinguishing.

He explicitly criticises explanations suggesting that one first reaches Nibbana as a separate thing and then has defilements removed by it.

His argument is not that Nibbana is merely a pleasant psychological feeling.

Nor does it mean the early Buddhist scriptures never call Nibbana unconditioned.

He is challenging the tendency to transform the *unconditioned* into another independently possessable object.

And this puts pressure on certain later Theravada interpretations that describe Nibbana as an ultimate existent reality.

Those interpretations are not automatically refuted by his linguistic argument; this is a genuine exegetical disagreement.

But Nanananda's challenge is formidable.

If liberation is the ending of craving, **what would it mean to crave liberation as a possession after craving has ended?**

Nothing coherent.

That is why the Buddha doesn't delight in Nibbana in the relevant sense.

Not because Nibbana is undesirable.

Because the possessive delight which sustains suffering is absent.

And notice how beautifully this clarifies the insight from Chapter I.

You can direct the mind towards Nibbana.

You can contemplate the possibility of release.

You can cultivate the path.

But the contemplation is successful insofar as it helps undo grasping, not insofar as it creates a more elaborate object for grasping.

**Even the goal must not become another prison.**


## 3\. The grammar of the universe has apparently made you immortal

Nanananda now makes an unusual argument.

Ordinary language encourages us to think of processes as things.

Consider a river.

You say, "The river flows."

Perfectly normal.

But then ask: what is this river, independently of the water flowing through its channel?

You can identify banks, currents, a geographical location and a recognisable continuity.

But you cannot produce some additional, self-existing river-substance concealed behind the activity of flowing.

Likewise, we say, "It rains."

Yet there's no independent entity called *It* standing above the clouds and performing the act of raining.

The sentence doesn't require a cosmic rain-manager.

Nanananda argues that our tendency to turn actions into independently existing actors can distort even our understanding of Nibbana.

You hear the word *cessation*\.

Immediately you imagine a thing called Cessation.

Something must possess it.

Somebody must enter it.

Perhaps it exists somewhere beyond the universe.

Perhaps there's an unconditioned consciousness that experiences it forever.

And suddenly the mind has constructed a metaphysical entity out of a description of release.

**The noun has hypnotised you into looking for a substance.**

This is Nanananda's interpretive argument, not a claim that English grammar literally produces egoism in everyone who speaks it.

Language is useful.

It lets us talk about rivers, rain, persons, consciousness and liberation.

The problem begins when we assume the structure of language must reveal the ultimate structure of reality.

A statement can be conventionally intelligible without committing us to a permanent entity behind every noun.

And this applies most devastatingly to the word *I*\.

"I am thinking."

"I am feeling."

"I am conscious."

The grammar provides a subject.

But does the usefulness of that grammatical subject prove the existence of an independent metaphysical subject standing behind thinking, feeling and consciousness?

No.

That requires an argument the grammar itself cannot supply.

And here's the irony.

You can realise that this is a mistake and still become attached to being **the person who has realised it**\.

Which brings us to the most dangerous kind of self-construction.

### The self that survives its own demolition

Imagine an extraordinarily sophisticated philosopher.

They understand the five aggregates.

They reject the permanent soul.

They analyse consciousness.

They recognise the conditionality of perception.

They can explain dependent arising in exhaustive detail.

Then somebody challenges their interpretation.

Immediately\:

"How dare this ignorant sod question my understanding?"

Their conceptual self has been dismantled.

But their identification with being knowledgeable remains spectacularly intact.

They are now defending a self-image built out of the doctrine of selflessness.

Nanananda's concern is that concepts can become the very supports through which craving, conceit and views continue operating.

Even true concepts.

Even Buddhist concepts.

Even the concept of Nibbana.

And this is why the difference between training and liberation matters.

The trainee must remain alert to the danger of conceptual appropriation.

The arahant no longer possesses the underlying tendency in the same way.

**Not because the arahant has discovered the ultimate correct self-description, but because the compulsive demand for such a description has ended.**

And there's a marvellous canonical detail here.

The *Mulapariyaya Sutta* ends unusually.

The monks listening to the discourse do not rejoice in what they've heard.

Nanananda suggests that perhaps they were uncomfortable because the Buddha had undermined the very tendency to appropriate even the highest spiritual goal.

That's his interpretation. The traditional commentary offers a different explanation, connected with the difficulty of the discourse.

But you can appreciate the psychological possibility.

Imagine a monk who has devoted his life to attaining Nibbana.

And the Buddha effectively says\:

"By the way, don't turn Nibbana into something that belongs to you."

The monk is sitting there thinking\:

"FOR GOODNESS' SAKE. THAT WAS THE ONE THING I THOUGHT I WAS ALLOWED TO HAVE."

Quite a difficult sermon to applaud.


## 4\. The demon king imprisoned by his own thoughts

Now Nanananda introduces one of the strangest and most useful stories in Sermon 13.

The gods fight the demons.

The gods win.

They capture Vepacitti, king of the demons.

And they bind him with five bonds.

So far, ordinary mythological warfare.

But these are extremely unusual restraints.

When Vepacitti thinks favourably of the gods and their realm, the bonds disappear.

He enjoys heavenly pleasures.

When he decides the demons are superior and wishes to return to their realm, the bonds reappear.

The bondage responds to his thoughts.

But the Buddha then says that the bondage produced by *me-thinking* is subtler still.

Nanananda uses this story from the *Yavakalapi Sutta* to show how conceptual identification can bind a being without any visible chain.

Consider the implications.

The demon's imprisonment is not simply a physical fact.

Its continuing significance is connected with his mental orientation.

And Nanananda argues that the bondage of ordinary existence is even more deeply implicated in our assumptions about identity.

"I am."

"I am this."

"I shall exist."

"I shall not exist."

"I shall become something."

"I shall attain a formless state."

"I shall become permanently conscious."

"I shall finally escape consciousness."

Look at that list.

Even the supposed solutions remain organised around the destiny of an imagined *I*\.

The metaphysical content changes.

But the activity of self-construction continues.

### The most sophisticated trap

Suppose somebody becomes afraid of death.

They adopt a belief in an eternal soul.

Now they feel reassured.

But the reassurance depends upon preserving the imagined soul.

Another person rejects the soul and embraces annihilation.

They imagine death as the absolute ending of their existence.

But their position is still organised around what happens to the supposed owner.

The first wants to preserve it.

The second wants to eliminate it.

Nanananda is asking both to investigate what exactly has been posited in the first place.

And this is why the teaching is so cutting.

**You cannot escape identification merely by selecting a more sophisticated identity.**

An ordinary identity can become a spiritual identity.

A spiritual identity can become a formless identity.

A formless identity can become the identity of someone who has transcended every identity.

The content keeps changing.

The appropriation persists.

And that's the subtler bondage.

### Why Nibbana is called unfalsifying

Nanananda now turns to a pair of verses in the *Dvayatanupassana Sutta*\.

Their central insight is that whatever the mind imagines as a substantial thing becomes subject to alteration.

The object cannot deliver the unconditional permanence that grasping demands.

The body changes.

Relationships change.

Experiences change.

Beliefs change.

Even extraordinarily refined meditative states can cease.

And the more absolutely you identify with any of these, the more their transformation becomes a threat to the identity.

Nibbana, however, is described as unfalsifying.

Nanananda understands this in terms of its freedom from the construction of a thing that must subsequently change.

That is a striking interpretation.

Ordinarily, you try to attain security through something.

A permanent body.

A permanent state of consciousness.

A permanent experience of bliss.

An eternal personal essence.

But each proposal creates another candidate for possession.

The mind imagines an object that will finally stop changing.

Then identifies with the imagined object.

The machinery continues.

Nanananda proposes a more radical possibility\:

**What if genuine security does not arise from finding an unchangeable possession, but from ending the compulsive dependence on possessions altogether?**

That does not settle every scholarly disagreement about the ontological status of Nibbana.

It does identify the practical significance of his argument.

The unshakeability of liberation is not the invulnerability of a newly acquired personal substance.

It is the ending of the grasping through which conditioned change becomes bondage.


## 5\. The palace that shook its owner

Nanananda now produces an absolutely beautiful example.

Sakka, king of the gods, visits the Buddha.

The Buddha teaches him that nothing should become a basis for dogmatic appropriation.

Sakka approves.

He leaves.

Later, the great disciple Mahamoggallana decides to find out whether Sakka actually understood what he heard.

So he visits the heavenly realm.

Sakka welcomes him.

But he's terribly busy.

Divine responsibilities.

Important celestial matters.

Apparently, being king of the gods involves an enormous amount of administration.

And there's something else.

Sakka has recently had a magnificent palace constructed.

He would very much like to show it off.

Naturally.

A heavenly king has acquired some extraordinary real estate and would appreciate appropriate recognition.

So he takes Mahamoggallana on a tour.

The building is exquisite.

The celestial luxury is spectacular.

Sakka is absolutely delighted.

And Mahamoggallana apparently decides the king could benefit from a practical demonstration.

He uses his psychic power to make the palace shake.

And Sakka is shaken with it.

The palace trembles.

The king trembles.

His magnificent security has become a source of terror.

And suddenly he remembers the Buddha's instruction.

Nanananda reads this as an illustration of what happens when someone doesn't merely possess a palace, but becomes psychologically *invested in* the palace.

This is a very precise distinction.

Sakka can physically enter the palace.

He can appreciate it.

He can use it.

The problem is that he has also entered into the palace psychologically.

His identity has become implicated in its existence.

So when the palace shakes, the owner shakes.

### The extraordinary freedom of entering a house without entering a house

Nanananda now asks us to imagine children building a little hut.

They gather sticks and leaves.

Construct a shelter.

Declare it a house.

Perhaps they invite their mother inside.

The mother crouches down and enters.

She plays along.

But she doesn't seriously believe she's entering a magnificent permanent residence.

She understands the convention.

She can participate without being deceived.

Nanananda suggests that an arahant can employ worldly concepts in a comparable way.

The liberated person enters a house without entering into the concept of the house through craving, conceit and views.

And that's magnificent.

Because it means liberation isn't necessarily about withdrawing from ordinary experience.

It's about no longer being psychologically captured by the meanings imposed upon it.

You can recognise an achievement without turning it into the essence of your identity.

You can occupy a role without imagining that the role establishes your ultimate existence.

You can use possessions without requiring them to make you complete.

You can speak about yourself without treating every sentence beginning with *I* as a metaphysical declaration.

### Can an arahant say “I”?

Yes.

Nanananda invokes a canonical verse explaining that an arahant can still use ordinary expressions such as speaking in the first person.

The conventional language remains available.

What is absent is the conceit that would turn it into a substantial assertion of selfhood.

This is tremendously important.

Because otherwise people imagine enlightenment requires some peculiar linguistic performance.

"I am going to lunch."

"NO\! An enlightened person cannot say I\!"

"Very well. This collection of aggregates is proceeding towards the kitchen."

Excellent.

You've made yourself sound like a malfunctioning philosophical refrigerator.

And the ego can still be perfectly intact.

What matters isn't the elimination of the pronoun.

It's the elimination of the delusion of independent ownership.

Nanananda makes this point explicitly: an arahant may continue using first-person language without the underlying conceit of self.

### The decisive insight of Sermon 13

The problem is not that the world contains houses, palaces, bodies, thoughts and useful concepts.

The problem is the way those objects become existential possessions.

**You are shaken by what you have entered into through identification.**

The world changes.

And because you have constructed your identity in dependence upon it, you feel that your very existence is under attack.

The Buddha's release is not to become an indestructible palace.

It is to stop requiring a palace to establish what you are.

But an enormous question remains.

If the subtle conceit *I am* underlies our imagined existence, what does it mean to say that the liberated person is no longer subject to birth and death?

The body still ages.

It still dies.

So is Buddhism making an impossible claim?

Or is there a precise distinction between the death of an organism and the cessation of the becoming that ignorance sustains?

Nanananda's Sermon 14 confronts this question directly.


# Sermon 14 - The man who stopped being born while his body was still alive

## 6\. The Buddha makes a statement that sounds completely impossible

Now Nanananda turns to the *Dhatuvibhanga Sutta*, MN 140, the Buddha's teaching to the ascetic Pukkusati.

Near its conclusion, the Buddha describes the liberated sage as someone in whom the compulsive constructions of *I am*, *I will be*, *I will not be*, and the various imagined forms of future existence have ceased.

Then comes an extraordinary declaration.

The sage is no longer born, no longer ages, no longer dies, and has no longing.

What?

Hold on.

An arahant still has a body.

The body still ages.

And eventually it dies.

So what exactly is the Buddha claiming?

Has the monk obtained an extraordinary exemption from biology?

Is his bloodstream no longer subject to time?

Has enlightenment somehow persuaded his knees to stop deteriorating?

No.

Nanananda argues that the passage concerns the cessation of the *appropriated existence* sustained by ignorance and craving.

This requires a very careful distinction.

A living arahant has not abolished the physical processes of aging.

Nor has becoming enlightened made the organism medically immortal.

But the arahant has ended the craving and clinging responsible, within the Buddhist account of dependent arising, for renewed becoming and rebirth.

Nanananda goes further, interpreting the transcendence of birth and death as something realised in the present experience of liberation.

That is a strong interpretive claim, not merely a restatement of biological facts.

And it's worth taking seriously because it exposes a profound misunderstanding of what the mind ordinarily fears.

### The thing that must survive

Imagine you've devoted twenty years to becoming a great philosopher.

Your identity has become organised around that project.

You're knowledgeable.

Respected.

You know the texts.

You can dismantle an opponent's argument with extraordinary precision.

Then you begin losing your memory.

Suddenly, a dreadful possibility emerges.

*What happens to me if I can no longer think like this?*

The underlying fear isn't simply that certain faculties may decline.

It's that the identity you've constructed around those faculties appears threatened.

You have equated yourself with a particular configuration of capacities.

And now change looks like personal destruction.

The same process appears in ordinary aging.

You notice physical decline.

Perhaps your appearance changes.

Perhaps your strength diminishes.

The changes themselves may involve real limitations, loss and pain.

But the mind can add something\:

"This is happening to ME."

"This means I am becoming less."

"I am losing what makes me who I am."

And now the body isn't merely changing.

A supposedly independent owner is being progressively destroyed.

Nanananda's analysis challenges the assumption that such an owner can actually be established.

**The body changes. But the additional story of an independent self whose essence is being destroyed is something the mind contributes.**

This doesn't make aging harmless.

It doesn't remove grief from ordinary life by philosophical decree.

And it certainly doesn't imply that people who fear death are spiritually defective.

The point is that suffering can include identification with an entity that the Buddhist analysis does not recognise as independently existing.

And ending that identification changes the meaning of the entire process.

## 7\. The tree that ages differently depending on who wants it

Nanananda introduces a wonderfully simple illustration.

Imagine a tree.

One person earns money by selling its young buds.

Another collects its leaves.

A third sells its flowers.

A fourth wants its fruit.

Now ask\:

"When does this tree become old?"

The bud-seller says it has passed its useful stage once the buds develop into leaves.

The leaf-seller may regard the next stage as decline.

The florist considers the flowering stage most valuable.

The fruit-seller is waiting for something later.

The physical transformation proceeds continuously.

But the judgment about what counts as flourishing and decline depends partly upon the standpoint from which the tree is valued.

That is an elegant illustration of how grasping influences our experienced world.

**The same process can appear as growth to one person and decline to another.**

But we needn't overstate the implication.

Biological aging is not merely an opinion.

Trees and people undergo measurable physiological changes.

Nanananda's point concerns the interpretive and possessive significance attached to those changes.

When your identity depends upon a particular stage, its passing becomes an existential threat.

And suddenly the natural movement of conditions is experienced as something deeply personal.

Now imagine somebody who no longer constructs an independent identity around those stages.

The organism still changes.

But the change is no longer understood as the destruction of a permanent owner.

In that sense, the language of liberation from aging and death acquires a contemplative significance in the present.

The Buddhist claim also includes the ending of future rebirth, and we shouldn't silently reduce it to a psychological metaphor.

But Nanananda wants us to see that the release is realised **while the arahant is alive**, not simply promised at the body's eventual death.

### The rope returns

Remember the rope from Chapter III.

Two strands are being twisted in opposing directions.

Someone grips them in the middle.

The tension becomes concentrated around the grasp.

The person feels resistance and believes the resulting structure must be defended.

But when the grip is released, the particular tension dependent upon that grasp ceases.

Nanananda returns to this image to explain the relationship between grasping and becoming.

He adds a remarkable observation: impermanence, considered simply as change, need not constitute a personal injury.

The problem arises when something changing has become an object of possessive identification.

Now this is a cutting distinction.

The world changes.

That's not an insult.

Pleasant experiences end.

That's not necessarily an injustice.

Bodies age.

That's not proof that the universe has made a personal exception to its rules just to torment you.

But once the changing process is appropriated as *me* or *mine*, its transformation can become the basis of an immense psychological struggle.

You're trying to persuade impermanence to stop applying to something you've decided must define you.

And impermanence isn't interested in negotiating.

It hasn't even read your proposal.

The answer Nanananda offers isn't to discover a magical substance immune to change.

It's to understand how the grasp generates the bondage.

And that prepares his next extraordinary image.


## 8\. The island that isn't an island

A man stands in the middle of a flood.

The water is rising.

The current is fierce.

He needs somewhere safe.

So he asks the Buddha to identify an island where the floods of aging and death cannot reach.

Now imagine the Buddha responds with directions.

"Right. Head north for forty miles."

"Take the second turning beyond the celestial river."

"Continue past the Brahma headquarters."

"Look for a small island with a sign reading NIBBANA - NO REFUNDS."

That would make liberation into another cosmic destination.

But the canonical reply, as Nanananda emphasises, identifies the island with the ending of possession and grasping.

**The island is the freedom of no longer taking anything as an ultimate possession.**

Nanananda discusses this passage from the *Kappa-manavapuccha*, arguing that its description of Nibbana as an island should not be interpreted as a geographical destination.

And here's the extraordinary thing.

The person wants something permanent to stand upon.

But the teaching points towards relinquishing the very activity through which the need for an ultimate foothold is perpetuated.

The flood is not defeated by acquiring a bigger possession.

The bondage is ended through the cessation of grasping.

Which sounds beautiful.

But now a very serious objection emerges.

**If everything we know is conditioned, how can there be an unconditioned ending?**

Wouldn't Nibbana itself have to arise through conditions?

And if it arises through conditions, wouldn't it necessarily be impermanent?

This is one of the deepest philosophical problems in Buddhism.

Nanananda addresses it directly.

## 9\. The unborn, the unmade, and the biggest ontological temptation in Buddhism

The Buddha declares in the *Udana* that there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade and unconditioned.

Without that, escape from what is born and conditioned would not be discernible.

That's a remarkable statement.

And it has generated centuries of interpretation.

One reading takes it as an affirmation of an independently real unconditioned element.

Another emphasises the cessation of conditioned becoming.

Nanananda strongly favours the latter approach.

He argues that the significance of the passage concerns the possibility of liberation from the conditioned process **here and now**\.

He criticises interpretations that transform the unborn into an independently existing metaphysical object somewhere beyond the world.

Now listen carefully.

This doesn't mean he's merely saying\:

"Everything is conditioned, except that feeling relaxed is nice."

That would be a dreadful reduction.

He's discussing the complete cessation of ignorance, craving and the conditions responsible for renewed becoming.

Something far more radical than temporary emotional composure.

But he objects to picturing Nibbana as a permanent object which the practitioner travels to and acquires.

His argument is that the unborn and unconditioned refer to liberation from the processes of birth, becoming and fabrication, not the discovery of another thing within those processes.

### The doctrinal disagreement

Here we need to distinguish Nanananda's position from a prominent classical Theravada interpretation.

In Abhidhamma-oriented Theravada, Nibbana is commonly understood as an unconditioned ultimate reality. It isn't produced by the path, although it is realised through the path.

That is not necessarily the crude claim that a little object called Nibbana exists at a particular location.

The classical account is considerably more sophisticated than that.

Nanananda nevertheless objects that substantialising Nibbana risks reproducing the conceptual habits the Buddha's teaching is meant to overcome.

**The dispute is not simply over whether Nibbana is real. It's over what sort of reality, if any, can coherently be attributed to the ending of grasping.**

This matters because one can reject metaphysical substantialisation without reducing liberation to an ordinary conditioned mental state.

And one can defend the unconditioned status of Nibbana without necessarily positing an eternal conscious self.

These positions require careful arguments.

Analayo's lecture materials preserve Nanananda's reading alongside alternative translations of the unborn passage and references to the broader history of the *Udana* collection. They provide textual context, not a demonstration that every later Theravada interpretation has been definitively disproved.

Now comes the practical payoff.

Why set the mind towards the unconditioned?

Because the contemplation directs attention towards the possibility of ending the conditions that sustain bondage.

You're not trying to generate a permanent emotional state.

You're cultivating an understanding through which the compulsion to grasp at conditioned states can cease.

The path is conditioned.

The wisdom develops.

But the freedom it realises is not simply another product to accumulate.

**The goal isn't to manufacture a permanent possession. It's to end the need for one.**

And now Nanananda explains how to apply that insight to the most intimate object of possession of all.

Your body.


## 10\. The body isn't your private collection of cosmic elements

The *Dhatuvibhanga Sutta* examines the person through six elements: earth, water, fire, air, space and consciousness.

Nanananda insists that this is not primarily a speculative scientific analysis.

It's a way of exposing identification.

Consider the solid components of your body.

Bones.

Teeth.

Tissues.

They belong to the same broad category of material qualities recognised outside the body.

The warmth in the body is not an entirely different metaphysical substance from warmth elsewhere.

Nor does bodily movement possess an independent personal essence simply because it occurs within your organism.

And the spaces within the body are not metaphysically separate from the more general category of space.

Nanananda argues that the Buddha's analysis helps dissolve the supposedly absolute distinction between internal and external materiality.

Now the point is not that bodies are physically indistinguishable from piles of rocks.

A living organism possesses extraordinarily complex biological organisation.

Nor is it that all living and nonliving systems are identical.

The point concerns **ownership**\.

You call something *my body*\.

And immediately it acquires a special existential status.

Its changing conditions become threats to the owner you imagine behind it.

But when the body is investigated as conditioned materiality, the claim of absolute ownership becomes difficult to sustain.

The body has causes.

It depends on nourishment, temperature, environment and innumerable other conditions.

It changes independently of your wishes.

You can influence it.

Care for it.

Train it.

But you cannot establish unlimited sovereign control over it.

And if you cannot exercise such control, what exactly does the claim of permanent metaphysical ownership mean?

### The mirror strikes again

Nanananda returns to the mirror.

You look at the reflection.

You recognise your body.

Then declare\:

"This is me."

But what has occurred?

A visual appearance.

Recognition.

An interpretation.

And identification.

The reflection doesn't contain a separate metaphysical owner.

The observer supplies that interpretation.

Nanananda argues that craving, conceit and views reinforce the distinction between an imagined internal owner and the external things standing in relation to it.

This is where the teaching becomes more subtle than an ordinary claim that the body is made of material elements.

The body is not merely being analysed into parts.

**The activity through which the body becomes *mine* is being investigated.**

And what happens when that activity ceases?

The body can remain.

Its practical functioning can continue.

But the possessive identification has been uprooted.

This is why an arahant can still experience bodily sensations without those sensations necessarily producing the same bondage.

And the analysis doesn't stop with materiality.

Nanananda takes it into the increasingly subtle experiences of meditation.


## 11\. The meditator who became the infinite

Imagine somebody enters a profound meditative absorption.

Ordinary bodily perception becomes faint.

The familiar world recedes.

An immense sense of boundless space appears.

Now the meditator thinks\:

"This is extraordinary."

Then\:

"This must be ultimate reality."

Then\:

"I have realised infinite space."

Then\:

**"I AM THE ONE WHO HAS REALISED INFINITE SPACE."**

And there it is.

The owner.

The metaphysical little sod has survived the disappearance of ordinary bodily perception.

He's now sitting inside an experience of cosmic vastness, congratulating himself on his transcendence of the body.

Nanananda examines the progression towards formless meditative attainments and argues that even the perception of infinite space remains conditioned and therefore cannot, simply by itself, establish Nibbana.

This is an extremely important distinction.

An experience can lack ordinary bodily boundaries without being unconditioned.

A state can appear limitless without being eternal.

Consciousness can become extraordinarily subtle without the underlying tendencies of identification having ceased.

And the meditator may become more attached to the attainment precisely because it seems so extraordinary.

Now consider what happens when the state ends.

Ordinary bodily awareness returns.

The meditator becomes disappointed.

They want the state back.

The experience which seemed to demonstrate transcendence has become another possession whose loss produces suffering.

You've traded a small house for an infinite cosmic palace.

But you're still Sakka.

And somebody can still shake the palace.

### The real practice: non-identification even with the highest attainment

Nanananda introduces an important teaching from the *Sappurisa Sutta*, MN 113.

Even the attainment of neither-perception-nor-non-perception - among the subtlest meditative states described in early Buddhism - must not become a basis for identification.

The practitioner is encouraged to cultivate non-identification even there.

That is extraordinarily radical.

Because it means the criterion of liberation isn't simply the magnificence of an experience.

It is the ending of the activity which makes experiences into possessions.

Suppose you're experiencing profound stillness.

The mind doesn't need to announce\:

"This is my ultimate nature."

Suppose bodily boundaries seem to dissolve.

The mind doesn't need to conclude\:

"I have become the universe."

Suppose awareness appears extraordinarily expansive.

The mind doesn't need to establish\:

"I am an eternal consciousness."

These might be interesting experiences.

But the interpretations are additional.

And their metaphysical validity cannot be established merely by the force of the experience.

Nanananda's argument is that wisdom understands the conditioned status of even the most exalted attainments.

And consequently, the practitioner need not appropriate them.

**The subtler the experience, the more sophisticated the self that may attempt to possess it.**

A rather inconvenient discovery for the spiritual tourism industry.

### The real ending of Sermon 14

Nanananda now turns to the Buddha's extraordinary instruction to Bahiya.

An ascetic encounters the Buddha while the Buddha is walking for alms.

He urgently requests a teaching.

The Buddha initially declines because the time is unsuitable.

The ascetic persists.

And the Buddha finally gives one of the briefest and most devastating instructions in the entire early Buddhist canon.

The essential point is that seeing, hearing, sensing and knowing should not become occasions for the additional construction of an independent possessor.

Nanananda introduces the instruction at the end of Sermon 14 and devotes Sermon 15 to explaining it.

And this is where the entire series takes a dramatic turn.

Because the Buddha is no longer merely explaining the construction of identification.

He's showing how the movement can be interrupted at its most intimate point.

Right where experience occurs.

Before the mind has completed its usual act of psychological appropriation.

The next question is unavoidable.

**How can perception occur without creating the person who supposedly perceives?**

That's the central problem of Sermon 15.


# Sermon 15 - The Buddha who destroyed an entire universe with one instruction

## 12\. The naked ascetic who demanded enlightenment in the street

The Buddha is walking through Savatthi on his alms round.

A wandering ascetic named Bahiya hears that the Buddha is nearby.

And Bahiya is absolutely desperate to meet him.

Not vaguely interested.

Not casually curious.

He has an urgent conviction that he needs the teaching.

So he hurries after the Buddha.

Finds him.

Bows.

And asks for instruction.

The Buddha initially refuses.

The timing is unsuitable.

He's on his alms round.

Bahiya insists.

The Buddha refuses again.

Bahiya insists a third time.

And now the Buddha finally delivers an instruction so concise, and apparently so simple, that it's almost offensive.

The teaching is usually summarised in English as\:

**In seeing, only what is seen. In hearing, only what is heard. In sensing, only what is sensed. In knowing, only what is known.**

And the discourse then states that when this is genuinely accomplished, identification in relation to those experiences ceases; the practitioner is no longer established *here*, *there*, or between the two. The conclusion is the ending of suffering.

According to the *Bahiya Sutta*, Ud 1.10, Bahiya realises arahantship upon hearing the instruction.

Nanananda introduced the encounter at the end of Sermon 14. Sermon 15 is devoted to its implications.

Now hold on.

**That's it?**

We've spent fourteen sermons investigating consciousness, dependent arising, the construction of the world, the imagined self, conceptual proliferation, non-manifestative consciousness, the metaphysics of the unconditioned-

And the Buddha just tells somebody to see what's seen?

A fairly modest instruction for an apparently infinite philosophical problem.

But the simplicity is deceptive.

Because this is not merely a recommendation to stop thinking.

It's a precise intervention in the mechanism we've been investigating throughout the series.

And if you understand what it is targeting, the entire structure suddenly becomes intelligible.


## 13\. The moment a sound becomes an insult to your existence

Imagine you're sitting quietly.

Somebody says something offensive.

First, sound is heard.

Then its meaning is recognised.

Perhaps unpleasant feeling arises.

Now ordinary proliferation begins.

"That person is insulting me."

"They think I'm inferior."

"They have no right to treat me like this."

"I must defend myself."

And suddenly an entire personal world has emerged.

There's a victim.

An aggressor.

A violated identity.

A history of previous grievances.

An imagined future in which the insult must be corrected.

The sound has become an existential event.

But Nanananda asks us to investigate a more basic question.

**At what point did the auditory experience become something that belonged to an independent owner?**

It is not necessary to deny the insult occurred.

Nor is it necessary to pretend offensive language has no social consequences.

You may still need to respond.

But consider the additional movement through which the experience becomes proof of the existence and vulnerability of an independent *me*\.

That is the operation under investigation.

And Bahiya's instruction is aimed precisely at preventing this additional appropriation.

### The crucial difference between perception and identification

Suppose you see an extraordinarily beautiful sunset.

The colours are vivid.

The landscape is magnificent.

The experience is pleasurable.

So far, nothing especially problematic.

But then\:

"I want this to last."

"I wish I could always experience this."

"Perhaps I should photograph it."

"Maybe I should live somewhere like this."

"I want my life to contain more of these moments."

Again, not every thought is harmful.

Taking a photograph is not a spiritual offence.

Planning a journey is not necessarily an expression of delusion.

The important question is whether the experience has become the basis of compulsive possession.

Has the mind begun treating the sunset as something which must secure its future satisfaction?

Has it constructed a personal identity around the experience?

Has it started measuring the value of existence according to whether such experiences continue?

**What began as seeing has become a project of becoming.**

And Nanananda believes the Buddha's instruction shows how that additional movement can cease.

Not by destroying vision.

Not by making the sunset disappear.

Not by manufacturing a blank state of consciousness.

But by understanding experience without converting it into a possessive standpoint.

### Why “bare awareness” isn't quite enough

Nanananda initially explains the instruction in terms of stopping short at bare awareness.

But if we reduce this to a simple attentional technique, we miss its deepest significance.

Imagine you're looking at a tree.

You deliberately avoid thinking the word *tree*\.

You simply observe colours and shapes.

Wonderful.

But then you think\:

"I am the pure observer of unlabelled experience."

And the self has returned.

Now it's an extremely sophisticated self.

It no longer identifies with the tree.

It identifies with **being the person who observes the tree without identification**\.

The mechanism is still operating.

This is why the teaching cannot be reduced to the suppression of verbal thought.

Nanananda links it directly with the earlier *Mulapariyaya Sutta*\: the point is to cease the egoistic imagining through which experience becomes something one identifies with, possesses or inhabits.

The object doesn't have to disappear.

The owner doesn't have to be replaced with a subtler owner.

**The activity through which ownership is imposed must be understood and ended.**

And now we arrive at the central revelation of the chapter.


## 14\. How a process becomes somebody

Let's examine the entire mechanism without metaphysical decoration.

A visual experience occurs.

Recognition occurs.

Feeling occurs.

Intention may arise.

These are conditioned processes.

Now the mind adds a relationship of possession.

**I see.**

Still conventionally useful language.

But then it adds\:

**I am the one who sees.**

Then perhaps\:

**Seeing belongs to me.**

And finally\:

**There must be an independently existing observer behind the seeing.**

This last step is the metaphysical leap.

The existence of an experience has become the supposed evidence for an independent experiencer.

But the experiencer isn't found as an additional object outside the very processes being examined.

Instead, Nanananda argues, the sense of an owner is itself established through craving, conceit and views.

He describes this as identification: the mind becomes entangled with something through the very act of appropriating it.

### The gem that possesses its owner

Nanananda returns to the image of somebody acquiring a precious gem.

Ordinarily, we say the person possesses the gem.

And conventionally that's true.

But now suppose the gem becomes central to their identity.

They worry about theft.

They worry about damage.

They worry about its value.

They imagine losing it.

Every change in its circumstances becomes a source of anxiety.

What has actually happened?

The person possesses the gem.

But the gem also exercises psychological power over the person.

**The owner has become possessed by the possession.**

And Nanananda makes precisely this point while explaining identification in Sermon 15.

Now apply that to the body.

You regard the body as yours.

And because it is yours, its changing condition becomes a source of existential anxiety.

Apply it to opinions.

You possess a philosophical view.

But once your identity depends upon the view, the view possesses you.

Apply it to spiritual attainment.

You experience profound concentration.

You regard the attainment as yours.

Now the possibility of losing the attainment becomes threatening.

The apparent owner has become dependent upon the object owned.

And that dependency is precisely what liberation is meant to end.

### The self is not an extra object hiding in consciousness

This is where the teaching becomes particularly relevant to nondual philosophy.

One might reason\:

"Fine. The body isn't the self."

"Thoughts aren't the self."

"Feelings aren't the self."

"But surely consciousness itself must be the true self."

After all, isn't consciousness that which knows everything else?

Nanananda's answer is that conditioned consciousness participates in dependent arising and can itself become a basis for grasping.

You cannot simply take the word *consciousness*, remove the ordinary sense of identity, and declare that you've established an eternal metaphysical subject.

That would require an additional argument.

The Buddhist investigation is directed towards the cessation of identification, including identification with consciousness.

This doesn't mean that every philosophical account of self-revealing awareness is incoherent.

It means that such an account is not automatically entailed by the Buddhist analysis.

And in these sermons, Nanananda's operative distinction is between consciousness entangled through grasping and the freedom he associates with non-establishment.

Not between a lower personal consciousness and an eternal Siva-consciousness discovered underneath it.

**The teaching isn't trying to discover who the owner really is. It's asking whether the supposed independent owner was ever established.**

And that brings us to the final part of Bahiya's instruction.

Because the Buddha doesn't merely say that experience can occur without appropriation.

He describes a startling consequence.

The person is no longer established here or there.

What the heck does that mean?


## 15\. The man who disappeared without going anywhere

Imagine an ordinary statement\:

"I am here."

It seems straightforward.

There is an *I*\.

And there is a location.

The two form a relationship.

Now imagine\:

"I shall be there."

Suddenly the *I* possesses a future destination.

The mind can orient itself towards becoming.

"I was there."

Now the *I* possesses a past.

And an entire temporal narrative can form around that supposedly enduring subject.

Nanananda argues that this structure becomes deeply misleading when the independently existing *I* is merely assumed rather than established.

When the activity of identification ceases, the associated personal standpoints lose their hold.

He interprets the *here*, *there* and *between* of Bahiya's instruction as expressions of the conceptual world organised around the conceit of existence.

Now don't misunderstand the claim.

An arahant hasn't lost the ability to locate their body.

They can find their monastery.

Remember where they left their bowl.

Walk from one village to another.

And avoid stepping into a ditch.

The point is not spatial disorientation.

It's freedom from the appropriation through which a location becomes the standpoint of an imagined independently existing self.

### The cinema again

You enter a cinema.

The film begins.

You become absorbed.

The characters appear.

The setting becomes convincing.

The story acquires emotional force.

And you begin participating in its world.

Now the lights come on.

The illusion loses its grip.

Where did the characters go?

Nowhere.

They were never independently present in the way the fiction depicted them.

The changing of conditions makes the deception apparent.

Likewise, Nanananda argues that awakening doesn't require transporting an independently existing self out of the world.

It requires seeing through the delusion by which that self and its world become established.

Now the comparison has limits.

The physical world isn't literally a projected motion picture.

And the Buddha's teaching is not merely that life resembles entertainment.

But the analogy illustrates an important distinction.

**An appearance can cease exercising delusive power without having to be replaced by a second, more ultimate appearance.**

The mind need not construct another identity to escape the first.

The cessation of appropriation is the relevant freedom.

And this is why Nanananda understands Bahiya's instruction as a direct way of ending suffering rather than a meditation technique that merely produces temporary psychological calm.

### The deserted village

He introduces another canonical image.

Imagine entering a village.

The buildings are there.

The paths are there.

The houses remain.

But the inhabitants have departed.

You can still recognise the village.

Yet it is empty of the people you expected to find.

The Buddha compares the six sense bases to an abandoned village, empty of a self or anything belonging to a self.

Nanananda connects this with Bahiya's instruction.

The eye functions.

The ear functions.

The body experiences sensations.

The mind recognises phenomena.

But when these are examined, the independent owner assumed to inhabit them cannot be found.

Not because the relevant sensory processes are meaningless.

But because the supposed permanent inhabitant was an additional construction.

The village was never the person you imagined living inside it.

And now ask what happens when this is genuinely understood.

You don't need to keep searching for the missing inhabitant.

You don't need to demolish the village.

You don't need to invent a new, invisible inhabitant called Pure Consciousness.

The investigation has revealed that the premise was mistaken.

**There is experience, but no independently established owner of experience.**

That is the cut.


## 16\. Morality, concentration and wisdom in one blimming sentence

Nanananda now makes a very ambitious claim.

He argues that Bahiya's brief instruction contains the essential movement of the entire threefold Buddhist training.

Ethical discipline.

Concentration.

Wisdom.

How?

### Ethical discipline

When an object appears, the mind need not immediately grasp its attractive features or begin elaborating upon them.

This supports sense restraint.

You see something desirable.

There can be recognition without immediately following every acquisitive impulse.

You encounter something unpleasant.

There can be recognition without automatically indulging hatred.

The purpose isn't to become incapable of perceiving attractive or unattractive qualities.

It is to cultivate freedom from compulsive reaction.

### Concentration

When the mind ceases elaborating an experience through unnecessary discursive activity, the conditions for collectedness improve.

You are no longer continuously dragged away by the stories arising around every encounter.

Attention can become steadier.

But don't confuse this with trying to forcibly eliminate every thought.

The point is the weakening of distracting compulsions, not an absolute prohibition on cognitive functioning.

### Wisdom

The deepest part is understanding that the experience does not establish an independent owner.

Seeing is not proof of an eternal seer.

Thinking is not proof of an eternal thinker.

Consciousness is not proof of an eternal consciousness-substance.

And the mind need not construct an identity around what occurs.

Nanananda explicitly relates these three dimensions to Bahiya's instruction, arguing that its extraordinary brevity contains the essential training leading to liberation.

This is interpretive synthesis, not a claim that the canonical sutta contains a complete technical explanation of every meditation practice.

But the synthesis is powerful.

Because it explains why the instruction can function as more than a reminder to be attentive.

The practice simultaneously involves restraining grasping, stabilising attention and understanding the absence of independent ownership.

And that brings us to another crucial question.

If the instruction is so simple, why doesn't everybody become liberated the moment they hear it?

### Why Bahiya understood immediately

The tradition presents Bahiya as exceptionally ready for insight.

Nanananda understands the instruction as arriving at the decisive moment when the relevant wisdom could penetrate completely.

That is not the same as saying any person can become an arahant merely by repeating the sentence three times.

The Buddha's teaching describes a cultivated path.

Ethical conduct.

Mental development.

Discernment.

Progressive weakening and abandonment of defilements.

Bahiya's story illustrates an extraordinary moment of realisation, not the redundancy of the entire path.

And this gives the series an important practical lesson.

**An explanation can be simple even when the understanding it expresses requires profound cultivation.**

A musician may finally understand a difficult movement through one brief instruction.

A mathematician may suddenly grasp a proof after years of study.

The final insight can be extraordinarily concise.

But the preparedness matters.

Nanananda's point is that the Buddha's instruction reached Bahiya at the level where it could overturn the remaining identification.

And the story now takes a startling turn.


## 17\. Bahiya reaches liberation, then gets killed by a cow

Yes.

Immediately after this astonishing awakening, Bahiya dies.

According to the *Udana*, a cow with a young calf gores him.

The man has just realised the ending of suffering.

And almost immediately, his physical life ends.

The monks later discover his body.

The Buddha instructs them to arrange his cremation and honour his remains.

They ask about his destiny.

And the Buddha tells them Bahiya attained final Nibbana.

Nanananda discusses this sequence carefully because it leads to a major disagreement about the interpretation of the Buddha's concluding verse.

Think about the narrative.

Bahiya didn't acquire physical immortality.

He didn't obtain supernatural protection against animals.

His body remained vulnerable.

The cow was not persuaded by the metaphysics of non-self.

The organism died.

So what exactly had liberation accomplished?

Within the Buddhist account, craving and ignorance had been uprooted.

The conditions for renewed becoming were exhausted.

And at the final passing away, there was no further rebirth.

This is importantly different from the claim that nothing significant happened because the body died anyway.

The Buddhist claim concerns the complete ending of the cycle of suffering and rebirth.

But notice how beautifully the story prevents a common misunderstanding.

**Nibbana is not an improved survival strategy for an eternal person.**

Bahiya's liberation does not depend upon the preservation of his organism.

And his death does not reverse the liberation.

Now the Buddha utters a verse involving the four material elements, the sun, moon, stars and the absence of darkness.

It is an extraordinarily mysterious image.

And Nanananda thinks the traditional interpretation gets its significance wrong.

## 18\. Where the sun and moon don't shine

The conventional commentarial explanation treats the verse as a description of the unconditioned Nibbana element associated with final liberation.

The physical elements do not exist there.

Nor do the sun, moon or stars.

Yet the verse also denies darkness.

Nanananda finds this explanation unsatisfactory.

He argues that the verse isn't describing the geographical destination to which Bahiya travelled after death.

Rather, he interprets it through the experience of liberation and what he calls non-manifestative consciousness.

He returns to the cinema analogy.

In a dark room, a projected image becomes vivid.

The audience is absorbed.

But when a much stronger light floods the room, the projected image loses its power.

Nanananda argues that the luminosity of wisdom similarly supersedes the ordinary apparent solidity of sensory experience.

This is not literally a claim that sunlight disappears when someone awakens.

It's an interpretation of the relationship between wisdom and delusive appearance.

And the metaphor is extremely evocative.

You have been watching the world through a narrow arrangement of conditioned perception.

The appearances seem independently substantial.

The observer seems independently established.

The distinction between self and world appears absolute.

Then wisdom reveals the conditionality of the whole arrangement.

The apparent solidity of the constructed world is penetrated.

The illusion no longer commands the mind in the same way.

### But here's where Analayo's textual caution matters

Remember Chapter III.

The description of non-manifestative consciousness as universally luminous has a disputed textual history.

Analayo's comparative study of the relevant passages raises serious questions about some received readings.

So Nanananda's use of this image cannot be treated as definitive proof of a particular luminous consciousness beyond all conditions.

Nor should his reading of Bahiya's concluding verse be presented as the only legitimate interpretation.

The Sermon 15 study materials preserve Nanananda's analysis alongside John D. Ireland's alternative translation and other canonical comparisons. The texts support a serious exegetical discussion, not a simple declaration that the traditional view has been refuted. \[buddhistinquiry.org; buddhistinquiry.org\]

And we should also avoid another mistake.

Even if Nanananda describes an extraordinary luminosity of wisdom, it does not follow that a permanent metaphysical self has been discovered.

His argument remains directed towards the cessation of appropriation.

The wisdom doesn't make the ego immortal.

It removes the delusion through which an independently existing ego was assumed.

And now the question becomes immensely practical.

How do you cultivate the right kind of understanding rather than merely a convincing spiritual experience?

Nanananda ends Sermon 15 with a surprisingly precise answer.


## 19\. The rainbow you miss by a fraction of an inch

Imagine looking at a tiny drop of dew.

Sunlight passes through it.

Under the right conditions, the colours of the spectrum become visible.

But your position must be correct.

You can stare at the drop for hours from the wrong angle.

Nothing.

Move slightly.

And suddenly the colours appear.

Nanananda uses this image to explain the importance of right view.

You can meditate for years.

Practise discipline.

Develop concentration.

Study the teachings.

And still misunderstand what the entire enterprise is intended to accomplish.

Perhaps you think liberation means permanently suppressing thought.

Perhaps you think it means acquiring eternal consciousness.

Perhaps you think it means eliminating every ordinary sensation.

Perhaps you think it means creating the perfect emotional state.

Your practices may be impressive.

But they are being directed towards the wrong conception of the goal.

You are attempting to reach Nibbana by manufacturing another possession.

The angle is wrong.

### How right view changes the whole project

Nanananda draws attention to an apparent difficulty in Buddhist practice.

The threefold training is commonly presented as ethical discipline, concentration and wisdom.

But the Noble Eightfold Path begins with right view.

Does wisdom come first or last?

His answer is that the path factors and the three trainings are interconnected.

He compares them to strands of a rope.

You climb by grasping the rope as a coherent structure.

You don't need to obsess about which strand must be touched first at every moment.

But right view has a guiding function: it ensures the practice is directed towards genuine release rather than an imagined spiritual acquisition.

And here's why this matters for our entire ten-chapter reading.

You can pursue meditation with the same psychological structure through which you pursue money, admiration or pleasure.

"I need more concentration."

"I need more insight."

"I need more spiritual experiences."

"I need confirmation that I'm progressing."

"I need to become enlightened."

And the practice can become a factory manufacturing increasingly sophisticated spiritual ambitions.

But when the goal is correctly understood as the ending of grasping, the direction changes.

You're not trying to possess the perfect state.

You're examining the processes through which states become objects of possession.

You're not trying to create an indestructible observer.

You're investigating the assumption that an independent observer must exist.

You're not trying to permanently eliminate ordinary experience.

You're cultivating the wisdom that ends delusive appropriation.

**Right view makes the path an undoing rather than another programme of acquisition.**

And this brings us straight back to your insight at the beginning of the series.

Setting the mind towards Nibbana works because the very understanding of what Nibbana means can begin loosening the movements through which the mind remains bound.

The conditioned contemplation is not itself the unconditioned goal.

But it is oriented towards the removal of the relevant conditions.

The path progressively undermines the structure of grasping rather than perfecting it.

That's the beautiful coherence of the teaching.


## 20\. The experiment: What exactly is this “me”?

Take a simple experience.

Perhaps you're sitting quietly.

A thought appears.

"I should be doing something productive."

Now notice what has happened.

There is a thought.

But the thought implies a person.

A person who ought to act.

A person whose worth may depend upon productivity.

And suddenly, without any deliberate investigation, an entire identity has been invoked.

Perhaps anxiety follows.

You remember unfinished tasks.

You imagine future failure.

You become dissatisfied with the present.

Now the original thought has become a personal demand.

But investigate.

**What exactly does the thought prove about an independently existing owner?**

Nothing.

It establishes that thinking has occurred.

It may communicate useful information about practical responsibilities.

But it does not establish a permanent metaphysical entity whose essential worth depends upon obeying it.

Notice the distinction.

You might still decide to work.

There may be good reasons.

But the work need not be undertaken because an independent self must continually establish its legitimacy through accomplishment.

You can respond intelligently without being enslaved by the identity the thought invites you to assume.

Now try the opposite.

A pleasant state appears.

Stillness.

Ease.

Perhaps an expansive sense of awareness.

Immediately the thought arises\:

"This is beautiful."

Then\:

"I want this to continue."

Then\:

"Perhaps this is genuine spiritual progress."

Then\:

"I am someone who can experience this."

And now watch the movement of appropriation.

The experience has become a possession.

The possession has become evidence for an identity.

The identity has acquired something to lose.

The very pleasure which seemed to promise freedom may now become a basis for anxiety.

Again, you don't need to destroy the pleasure.

You don't have to reject the experience.

You can recognise its conditioned character.

And see the additional movement through which the mind tries to make it secure an imagined owner.

**That movement is what we're learning to understand.**

This is not a guaranteed method for producing sudden liberation.

Nor does observing a thought make you an arahant.

But it is an intelligible application of Nanananda's argument.

The issue is not merely that thoughts arise.

It's that the mind constructs a possessor out of the processes it experiences.

And the possessor immediately becomes another condition for craving.


# The final cut - There was never a separate CEO of experience

Let's gather the three sermons into one argument.

**Sermon 13 asks what distinguishes an ordinary person's experience from an arahant's.**

The answer is not that the arahant has stopped recognising objects.

It's that the activity of egoistic appropriation has ceased.

The liberated person can use ordinary concepts without becoming psychologically imprisoned by them.

Even Nibbana itself is not converted into an object of possessive delight.

**Sermon 14 asks what happens when the most subtle forms of identification are uprooted.**

Nanananda argues that the sage is liberated from the becoming sustained by craving, conceit and views.

He investigates the supposed distinction between internal and external elements.

He examines the danger of appropriating exalted meditative attainments.

And he introduces the principle of non-identification: the ending of the tendency to become psychologically bound to what is experienced.

**Sermon 15 shows how that principle reaches into the moment of perception itself.**

The Buddha's instruction to Bahiya is not simply to eliminate thoughts or concentrate upon sensory data.

It points towards a way of experiencing in which seeing, hearing, sensing and knowing do not become grounds for constructing an independent possessor.

The activity of identification ceases.

And with the ending of that activity, the entire project of securing the imagined owner loses its foundation.

Now here's the extraordinary thing.

We began the chapter asking\:

**How does a process become someone called me?**

And the answer has turned out to be remarkably exact.

A conditioned experience occurs.

The mind recognises it.

Feeling and intention participate.

Craving makes it desirable or threatening.

Conceit establishes a personal relationship.

Views reinforce the apparent independence of the supposed owner and the objects owned.

The mind begins treating the resulting structure as a substantial reality.

And from that point onward, the apparent person has an entire future to protect.

An identity to improve.

A reputation to defend.

A body to preserve.

Experiences to acquire.

Threats to escape.

And eventually, even a spiritual liberation to possess.

The machinery is extraordinarily productive.

But the owner for whom the entire enterprise is supposedly operating cannot be independently established.

**The CEO is a construction of the factory.**

And once that is understood, something radical becomes possible.

The factory doesn't need to produce a superior CEO.

It doesn't need to manufacture a cosmic consciousness and appoint it as permanent management.

It doesn't need to destroy every activity taking place within its walls.

It needs to end the delusion through which its own processes have become the possessions of an imagined independent owner.

That's the conceptual core of Nanananda's account of liberation.

But now we have reached an even more interesting question.

Because we have spent fifteen sermons investigating the construction and ending of identification.

And there's an extraordinary possibility implicit in all of this.

**If there is no independent possessor of experience, can experience continue without anybody possessing it?**

Not merely in theory.

Not merely as a philosophical proposition.

But as the actual condition of a living liberated person.

Can there be seeing without the conceit of a seer?

Feeling without the possessive demand to secure a feeling-owner?

Action without the identity of an independent actor?

And how does the Buddhist account explain such freedom without lapsing into unconsciousness, passivity or the fantasy of an eternal witness?

This is the problem Chapter VI must address.

And it brings us closer to the deepest point of the entire teaching\:

**Liberation is not the acquisition of a perfect experiencer. It is freedom from the bondage of making experience belong to one.**
