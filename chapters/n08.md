# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter VIII of X · Sermons 22-24 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, with Bhikkhu Analayo's comparative materials

## What actually happens to someone who is liberated and dies?

**The answer: The causes of renewed becoming have ceased. But asking where the liberated person goes may be the wrong question, because the independent entity you are trying to locate was never established in the first place.**

And the Buddha is going to make this considerably more uncomfortable.

He doesn't merely refuse to give you the liberated person's destination.

He questions your understanding of a *person*\.

Then he questions your understanding of *existence*\.

Then he questions what you mean by *the world*\.

And eventually he questions the very framework of experience through which you've been asking all these questions.

You came here wanting to know whether an enlightened monk survives death.

And the Buddha is about to interrogate the intellectual machinery that produced the question.

It's rather like asking a mechanic where your car has gone, only for him to explain that you've mistaken the operation of an engine for the existence of a second invisible vehicle.

You're furious.

You just wanted the address.

But there is something extraordinary about Nanananda's approach.

He doesn't believe this is abstract philosophical cleverness.

He thinks the misunderstanding is directly responsible for suffering.

**The mind invents a permanent owner of experience, becomes terrified about that owner's destiny, and then attempts to solve the terror without questioning the invention.**

That's the trap.

And it explains why the Buddha's refusal to answer certain questions can itself be a liberating teaching.

But there is a strict distinction we must preserve.

The early Buddhist texts assert that a fully liberated person will not undergo renewed rebirth. They do **not** thereby establish that an eternal soul has been annihilated. And they do **not** require us to posit an immortal consciousness continuing in another realm.

Those are different claims.

Nanananda will defend that distinction in increasingly radical ways.

Analayo's comparisons with the Chinese Agamas will help us distinguish the shared canonical arguments from Nanananda's stronger philosophical interpretations.

And we begin not with a person dying, but with somebody terrified by the suggestion that no permanent person can be found.


# Sermon 22 - The monk who accidentally turned Buddhism into annihilationism

## 1\. The children have destroyed their sandcastles. Who exactly has died?

Chapter VII ended with the Buddha's image of children playing with sandcastles.

They build elaborate structures.

They decorate them.

They become attached to them.

They fight over them.

But eventually their interest ends.

They scatter the sand.

And go home.

Nanananda opens Sermon 22 by returning to the implication.

The children didn't have to transport their sandcastles into a permanent sandcastle heaven.

Nor did they have to annihilate an independently existing Sandcastle Essence.

The structures depended upon arrangements of sand and the meanings invested in those arrangements.

When their fascination ended, the structures lost their possessive significance.

Now Nanananda asks us to consider the five aggregates: bodily form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness.

These are the processes around which the ordinary person constructs the identity of *me*\.

But what makes somebody a *being* in the sense relevant to bondage?

The Buddha gives Radha a startling explanation.

**A being is someone caught up through craving and attachment to the aggregates.**

The Chinese parallel, SA 122, preserves the same central point: attachment and entanglement in the aggregates underlie this designation.

Now think about the consequences.

The ordinary person isn't merely experiencing the aggregates.

They are attached to them.

They take them as the basis of an identity.

And that identity becomes the foundation of the whole project of existence.

*My body must continue.*

*My consciousness must continue.*

*My achievements must endure.*

*Something that is really me must survive.*

The entire system becomes an exercise in protecting the imagined owner.

And Nanananda is asking something radical.

If the concept of a *being* is bound up with this attachment, what happens when the attachment ceases?

Not necessarily the disappearance of the physical body.

Not necessarily the immediate cessation of every sensory process.

The arahant remains alive and conventionally identifiable.

But the compulsive appropriation through which the aggregates become an independent *me* has ended.

**What disappears is not an eternal individual substance. It's the bondage through which one was imagined and defended.**

And now the trouble begins.

Because most people hear that and immediately panic.

"WHAT DO YOU MEAN THERE'S NO BEING?"

"ARE YOU SAYING I DON'T EXIST?"

"ARE YOU SAYING ENLIGHTENMENT MEANS I GET DESTROYED?"

The Buddha's reply is about to be extraordinarily precise.

## 2\. The Buddha is accused of murdering the soul

Nanananda turns to the *Alagaddupama Sutta*, MN 22.

The Buddha describes a liberated monk who has laid down the burden of identification and ended the fetters of existence.

Even gods cannot discover an object upon which that liberated consciousness is established in the old possessive way.

And then comes a remarkable accusation.

Certain religious thinkers claim that the Buddha teaches the destruction of a truly existing being.

They call him an annihilationist.

The Buddha rejects the charge.

He explains that his teaching concerns suffering and its cessation, not the extermination of an eternal individual. Analayo's notes compare the Pali passage with the Chinese parallel MA 200, which likewise preserves both the untraceability theme and the rejection of annihilationism.

Now consider the two positions.

The critic assumes there is a permanent self.

Then hears the Buddha deny that such a self can be established through the aggregates.

Then concludes that the Buddha must be attempting to destroy that self.

But the inference doesn't follow.

If somebody tells you there's no invisible man living inside your refrigerator, they're not proposing the murder of the invisible man.

They're questioning the evidence for his existence.

You cannot annihilate a metaphysical entity merely by refusing to accept that it was there.

And that's the exact logical distinction Nanananda thinks people keep missing.

**Not finding an independent self is not the same as destroying an independent self.**

The first concerns the validity of an assumption.

The second presupposes the very entity under dispute.

The annihilationist has accepted the eternalist's fundamental premise and simply changed the ending.

One says the soul lasts forever.

The other says the soul is destroyed.

The Buddha questions the independently existing soul itself.

And that's why his position cannot be captured by merely choosing between the two.

### But surely the Buddha himself existed?

Of course, conventionally.

He was a historical teacher.

He had a body.

He walked.

Ate.

Spoke.

Taught disciples.

Experienced aging.

Eventually died.

There is no need to deny those ordinary facts.

But ordinary identification and ultimate metaphysical establishment are different questions.

When the Buddha says the liberated one is untraceable in the relevant sense, he isn't claiming people can no longer recognise his face.

He's denying the possibility of establishing him as an independent substance through the aggregates or through a separate consciousness possessing them.

Nanananda presses this distinction very strongly.

He criticises a commentarial explanation which, in his reading, turns the inability to trace an arahant's consciousness into a mystery about some surviving consciousness that the gods cannot locate.

He thinks that preserves the very assumption the discourse is intended to undermine.

But this is an interpretive dispute.

The commentary's reference to different kinds of consciousness does not automatically entail belief in an eternal postmortem soul.

Nanananda's philosophical criticism is that the explanation risks keeping alive an inappropriate search for something to trace.

The strongest shared canonical point is narrower\:

**Liberation cannot be understood simply by locating a permanent consciousness-object on which an independently existing person is based.**

And this distinction becomes devastating in the next story.

## 3\. Yamaka, the monk who thought he had figured out death

Meet Yamaka.

A Buddhist monk.

Presumably familiar with the teachings.

And possessed of an interpretation so confident that the other monks can't persuade him to abandon it.

Yamaka has concluded that an arahant, at death, is annihilated.

No more body.

No more aggregates.

No more person.

Total destruction.

He thinks this is what the Buddha teaches.

His fellow monks disagree.

They try explaining the problem.

Yamaka refuses to budge.

So they ask Sariputta to intervene.

Now here's why this story is extraordinary.

Sariputta doesn't begin with a complicated metaphysical lecture about a transcendent realm.

He asks Yamaka to examine his assumptions.

Is bodily form the Tathagata?

No.

Feeling?

No.

Perception?

No.

Mental formations?

No.

Consciousness?

No.

Is the Tathagata simply identical with all five aggregates together?

No.

Is he an independently existing entity separate from them?

That isn't established either.

So what exactly is Yamaka claiming gets annihilated?

Sariputta has exposed the assumption concealed within the statement.

Yamaka is describing the destruction of an independent entity whose existence he has not demonstrated.

This is the *Yamaka Sutta*, SN 22.85. Analayo's materials reproduce the corresponding Chinese parallel SA 104, which preserves the essential questioning and Yamaka's eventual change of view.

Now imagine Yamaka's predicament.

He thinks he's being wonderfully consistent.

"No soul. No permanent self. So obviously the arahant is annihilated."

And Sariputta effectively replies\:

"Mate, you've just smuggled a soul into the sentence so you can destroy it."

An extraordinary achievement.

You've become an annihilationist by retaining the metaphysical furniture of eternalism.

The independently existing self is still sitting in the room.

You've simply announced that somebody will burn the room down.

But Buddhism is questioning whether that particular piece of furniture was ever there.

### The answer Yamaka finally gives

Sariputta asks Yamaka how he would now describe the death of an arahant.

Yamaka no longer speaks of the destruction of an independently existing being.

He describes the aggregates as impermanent and subject to cessation.

That's a completely different answer.

It identifies what arises and ceases without adding a metaphysical owner.

And Nanananda considers the distinction decisive.

Notice what has changed.

The original answer was\:

"A person is annihilated."

The corrected answer concerns the cessation of conditioned processes.

The latter does not entail an immortal soul.

Nor does it describe the destruction of one.

And this is precisely why Nanananda insists that the Buddha's teaching cannot be reduced to either eternal survival or annihilation.

The analysis concerns dependent arising and cessation.

But Sariputta isn't finished.

He now introduces one of the most disturbing similes in the entire series.


## 4\. The assassin who became your closest friend

Imagine a wealthy man.

He has property.

Money.

Influence.

Servants.

A comfortable life.

Naturally, he wants protection.

Someone appears.

A charming stranger.

Polite.

Diligent.

Reliable.

The fellow offers to serve him.

The wealthy man is impressed.

He takes him into his household.

The new servant behaves magnificently.

He rises early.

Works hard.

Anticipates his master's needs.

Speaks gently.

Behaves with absolute devotion.

The wealthy man comes to regard him as a trusted companion.

Perhaps almost a son.

There's just one slight inconvenience.

**The servant is an assassin.**

He has been planning the wealthy man's murder from the beginning.

He waits patiently.

Continues behaving impeccably.

Gains ever greater trust.

And eventually, when the master is unguarded, he kills him.

Sariputta asks Yamaka when the man became the assassin.

Was it only at the moment of the killing?

No.

His murderous intention was present throughout.

The wealthy man failed to recognise what he had welcomed into his household.

Nanananda presents the story as an analogy for the ordinary person's relationship to the five aggregates. The Chinese parallel SA 104 likewise preserves the infiltrating enemy and murder narrative.

Now listen carefully.

This is not an invitation to hate the body.

Nor to treat feelings as morally evil.

Nor to declare consciousness an enemy in every possible sense.

The point is the danger of **mistaking vulnerable conditioned processes for a permanent source of security**\.

The body can be pleasant.

Useful.

Beautiful.

Strong.

Your memories can be meaningful.

Your knowledge can be valuable.

Your consciousness can contain extraordinary experiences.

But none of these is capable of guaranteeing permanent security.

And when you identify with them absolutely, their inevitable alteration becomes an existential threat.

The assassin's danger lies partly in the victim's ignorance of what has been trusted.

Likewise, clinging to changing aggregates as *my permanent self* exposes the mind to suffering.

### The most dangerous friend is the one you mistake for yourself

Now take the analogy deeper.

You don't merely welcome the aggregates into your household.

You conclude that **you are the household**\.

The body changes.

You feel personally diminished.

An experience ends.

You feel that something essential has been taken from you.

An opinion is challenged.

Your existence seems threatened.

A memory fades.

You fear losing the person you fundamentally are.

All because conditioned phenomena have been elevated into an independent identity.

And Sariputta's point is that understanding them as impermanent, vulnerable and not-self transforms that relationship.

They need not be possessed as an ultimate identity.

Which means the processes can cease without the annihilation of an eternal owner.

And here's the extraordinary ending of the story.

Yamaka understands.

His view collapses.

And in the Pali account he declares that his mind has been liberated through non-clinging.

The Chinese parallel likewise describes liberation arising as Sariputta teaches.

Think about that.

The monk came into the conversation defending a speculative position about what happens after death.

He leaves with liberation.

Not because Sariputta supplied a better theory of the afterlife.

But because the discussion exposed and removed a misunderstanding at the foundation of grasping.

**A question about death has become the occasion for understanding life.**

And this is why Nanananda thinks the Buddha's unanswered questions are not pointless.

Their analysis can penetrate directly to the roots of suffering.

### But why are people so frightened by this?

Because the ordinary mind wants an owner.

It wants something definite to survive.

And if survival cannot be secured, it may prefer the certainty of annihilation.

At least then it knows what happens.

But Nanananda is offering neither reassurance.

He is asking the mind to investigate the very mechanism that makes reassurance necessary.

And this can be deeply unsettling.

He cites the Buddha's prediction that monks in future generations may be unwilling to investigate teachings concerned with profound emptiness.

Nanananda understands this reluctance as partly arising from fear of the implications of non-self.

The mind hears\:

"There is no independent owner."

And panics\:

"BUT WHO WILL RECEIVE MY ENLIGHTENMENT?"

Exactly.

That's the question we're learning to stop taking for granted.

And now Nanananda makes a surprising move.

He leaves the fate of the liberated person and asks about the fate of something even larger.

**The entire world.**

Not merely\:

"Does the Buddha survive death?"

But\:

"Does the world survive?"

"Is it eternal?"

"Is it finite?"

"Can it end?"

And most importantly\:

**What exactly are you calling the world?**

That is Sermon 23.


# Sermon 23 - The man who travelled for a century and forgot to leave his own body

## 5\. The world's end is not where the world stops appearing on a map

Nanananda begins Sermon 23 with an extraordinary reversal.

The previous sermon asked whether a liberated person can be found as an independent entity.

Now he asks what happens when the same analysis is directed towards the world.

And his starting point is beautifully practical.

**The world you know is a world known through the senses.**

You see.

Hear.

Smell.

Taste.

Feel.

Think.

And through these processes, the familiar world becomes available.

Objects are distinguished.

Distances are measured.

Locations are recognised.

Time is understood.

And you begin constructing propositions about what the world fundamentally is.

Perhaps it's eternal.

Perhaps it's temporary.

Perhaps it's finite.

Perhaps it's infinite.

But Nanananda asks us to notice something.

Every one of those propositions is formulated through a particular structure of experience.

And that structure has conditions.

You haven't stepped outside experience to examine the universe from an impossible, unconditioned standpoint.

You've used the senses and mind to construct an account of the world.

Now this does **not** establish that no physical world exists independently of your individual consciousness.

Nanananda explicitly cautions that his argument is not a denial of materiality.

He is investigating the *world as perceived, conceived and measured*, and the bondage connected with that world.

And this changes the question.

If the world as experienced depends upon a network of conditions, what does it mean to reach its end?

Surely we should first understand the network.

Instead, people keep asking how far they must travel.

And Nanananda now introduces the most absurd expedition in ancient Buddhist literature.

## 6\. Rohitassa: the fastest man in the universe, travelling in the wrong direction

Meet Rohitassa.

In a previous life, he was an ascetic with extraordinary powers.

He could move through the air at tremendous speed.

His stride was so vast that he could cross immense distances almost effortlessly.

One day he developed an ambition.

He would travel to the end of the world.

And not merely the edge of the known inhabited world.

He wanted to reach a place beyond birth, aging and death.

The ultimate destination.

So he set off.

And kept going.

And going.

And going.

For a hundred years.

Apart from eating, drinking, sleeping and attending to bodily necessities, he devoted his life to the journey.

Imagine having supernatural locomotion and using it to conduct the world's longest unsuccessful visa run.

Entire kingdoms pass beneath you.

Mountains.

Oceans.

Continents.

The horizon keeps receding.

And Rohitassa keeps thinking\:

**"SURELY THE EDGE IS JUST A LITTLE FARTHER."**

Eventually, he dies.

Without finding it.

Later, as a deity, he approaches the Buddha and asks whether the end of the world can be reached through travel.

The Buddha says no.

But then adds the astonishing qualification.

Without reaching the end of the world, suffering cannot be ended.

And the world, its arising, its cessation and the path to cessation are to be understood in relation to this very body endowed with perception and mind.

This is the *Rohitassa Sutta*, found at SN 2.26 and AN 4.45, which Nanananda makes central to Sermon 23.

Now stop.

How can both statements be true?

You cannot reach the world's end by travelling.

But you cannot end suffering without reaching the world's end.

The Buddha seems to have designed the most infuriating travel itinerary imaginable.

Destination mandatory.

Travel impossible.

No refunds.

But the apparent contradiction disappears when the expression *world* is examined.

### The world he took with him

Rohitassa imagines that the relevant problem is distance.

Somewhere beyond the last mountain, the last galaxy, the final limit of space, there must be a territory where death has no power.

But Nanananda argues that Rohitassa was carrying the conditions of his experienced world along throughout the journey.

His body.

His perception.

His consciousness.

His craving.

His identification with becoming.

These were not left behind when he travelled.

He merely changed geographical locations.

**He was trying to escape the conditions of suffering by transporting those conditions somewhere else.**

And that's why the entire project was misconceived.

Imagine being deeply unhappy because you identify your worth with other people's approval.

You decide to move to another country.

Perhaps this will finally make you happy.

You arrive.

The environment changes.

The language changes.

The people change.

But your need for approval remains.

Now you begin worrying about what the new people think of you.

The old mechanism has successfully acquired a new international address.

The journey may have been valuable.

But the underlying problem has not been solved.

Rohitassa's cosmic journey is this mistake carried to its metaphysical extreme.

He seeks a place free from death while carrying the conditioned basis of his fear of death.

And the Buddha's instruction points in another direction.

**Investigate the conditions of the world as experienced, and discover the cessation of the bondage connected with them.**

Not a new location.

A radically different understanding.

### Analayo's Chinese parallel changes the emphasis

Here the comparative evidence is especially useful.

The Chinese *Samyukta-agama* parallel, SA 1307, preserves Rohitassa's hundred-year journey and the Buddha's response.

But it adds an important explicit explanation.

It identifies the world with the five aggregates subject to grasping, identifies craving as the origin of the world, identifies its ending with the cessation of craving, and identifies the Noble Eightfold Path as the way to that cessation.

That's a remarkably direct connection with the Four Noble Truths.

Notice why this matters.

Nanananda emphasises the six sense spheres through which a world is experienced.

The Chinese parallel makes the relationship to the aggregates and craving unusually explicit.

These are related analyses, but they are not identical formulations.

And the Chinese version provides an important safeguard against an overly idealist interpretation.

**The Buddha is not merely offering a theory about how perception constructs a virtual reality. He is teaching the ending of suffering through the cessation of craving.**

That's the point.

Rohitassa doesn't need a more powerful form of transportation.

He needs to understand what he's trying to escape.

And now Nanananda turns to two philosophers who are making almost the same mistake.

## 7\. Two omniscient gurus walk into an argument

Two brahmins approach the Buddha.

They have a disagreement.

One renowned teacher claims to possess infinite knowledge of an infinite world.

Another claims finite knowledge of a finite world.

Both appear confident.

Both claim extraordinary insight.

And the brahmins want to know which one is right.

Now imagine the Buddha convening an enormous cosmological conference.

Perhaps he develops a model of space-time.

Measures the universe.

Determines the limits of infinity.

Publishes the definitive chart.

Instead, he leaves the dispute aside and teaches the Dhamma.

Nanananda takes this from AN 9.37, the *Lokayatikabrahmana Sutta*\.

Why?

Because the question concerns speculative claims about the world which do not directly resolve the conditions of suffering.

And Nanananda points out how easy it is to get trapped in circular argument.

The first guru claims infinite knowledge of an infinite world.

His opponent can object that an infinite world has not been fully known.

The second claims finite knowledge of a finite world.

His opponent can object that the apparent finitude may merely reflect the limits of his knowledge.

Round and round they go.

Each accuses the other of confusing the world's limits with the limits of cognition.

A philosophical tennis match with no final whistle.

Now this doesn't prove that scientific cosmology is meaningless.

Whether physical space is finite or infinite is a legitimate scientific question.

And contemporary mathematics has rigorous ways to distinguish different meanings of infinity.

Nanananda's critique concerns the **soteriological usefulness and hidden assumptions** of such speculative positions, not an empirical refutation of cosmological investigation.

But his philosophical challenge is sharp.

Suppose you knew the exact size of the observable universe.

Would that knowledge, by itself, eradicate your craving?

Suppose you established with certainty that physical space is infinite.

Would that remove the conceit of *I am*?

Would it free you from hatred?

Would it end the process through which pleasure becomes dependence?

Not automatically.

And that's the distinction.

A proposition may be true.

Even extraordinarily important.

Without being sufficient for liberation.

The Buddha is redirecting attention from the ambition to possess a total description of the cosmos towards the investigation of suffering and its causes.

But why does Nanananda think the ordinary concept of the world is so misleading?

He gives us a wonderful image.


## 8\. The magic kettle that produces an infinite universe

Imagine an exhibition.

In the middle of the room is an extraordinary device.

A kettle pours water continuously into a basin.

The water never stops.

One spectator is fascinated.

"Eventually the kettle must become empty."

Another disagrees.

"Eventually the basin must overflow."

They sit down to watch.

Minutes pass.

Hours pass.

Still pouring.

The kettle doesn't empty.

The basin doesn't overflow.

The first spectator becomes desperate.

"IT HAS TO RUN OUT."

The second becomes equally desperate.

"THE BASIN HAS TO FILL."

They begin developing theories.

Perhaps the kettle possesses an infinite reservoir.

Perhaps the basin has a hidden capacity beyond ordinary geometry.

Perhaps the laws of reality have been suspended.

An entire metaphysical industry emerges.

Meanwhile, behind the apparatus, a hidden pipe returns the water from the basin to the kettle.

The system is circulating.

The spectators have mistaken an ongoing process for a situation involving two independent quantities, one of which must eventually reach a final limit.

This is Nanananda's authentic magic-kettle simile.

Now the philosophical point is remarkable.

The observers have constructed two apparently obvious alternatives.

The kettle must empty.

Or the basin must overflow.

But the alternatives depend upon an incomplete understanding of the system.

Once the circulation is understood, the whole debate changes.

The problem wasn't that one philosopher possessed the correct answer and the other the wrong answer.

**Both were asking questions based on an inadequate model of what was happening.**

And Nanananda applies this to the experienced world.

We habitually notice the apparent arising and persistence of things.

The mind constructs an impression of compact, enduring objects.

Then imagines these objects moving through time and space as independent substances.

And finally asks whether the totality must be eternal, non-eternal, finite or infinite.

But dependent arising reveals a more fundamental structure of conditionality.

The world as experienced isn't simply a collection of permanent objects waiting to be counted.

Its appearance and intelligibility depend upon changing conditions.

And the ordinary mind can become trapped in speculative questions that conceal those conditions.

### The danger of turning this into fake physics

Nanananda sometimes extends his cycle analogy to cosmological expansion and contraction.

That belongs to his wider philosophical illustration.

It is not scientific evidence that the physical universe operates like a kettle connected to a hidden pipe.

Nor does the recurrence of phenomena establish that the cosmos literally lacks a beginning in every scientifically possible sense.

The important argument concerns conditioned experience and the conceptual assumptions through which it becomes a basis for grasping.

**A useful analogy for dependent arising should not be promoted into a verified cosmological mechanism.**

But within its proper scope, the kettle is devastating.

Because it forces you to examine the structure of your question.

And this produces the next move.

What if the Buddha's promise to reach the end of the world concerns the cessation of the very processes through which a world becomes a field of personal experience?

How would such an ending be realised?

And does it mean becoming unconscious?


## 9\. The meditator who reaches the world's end without moving

Nanananda now returns to the extraordinary meditative attainments examined in Chapters VI and VII.

A discourse in AN 9.37 describes progressively refined forms of meditation.

The practitioner moves beyond ordinary sensual pleasure.

Enters the absorptions.

Transcends increasingly subtle forms of perception.

Eventually reaches the attainment involving the cessation of perception and feeling.

And crucially, the discourse connects this with the destruction of the defilements through wisdom.

Nanananda interprets this as an account of reaching the end of the world **without travelling through physical space**\.

Now here's the important distinction.

A person can enter an extraordinary concentration.

Ordinary perception may cease temporarily.

But that fact alone does not necessarily establish final liberation.

In classical Theravada, the cessation attainment is not simply identical with Nibbana, and it is not restricted exclusively to arahants; qualified non-returners may also attain it.

The canonical passage places special importance on wisdom and the destruction of the defilements.

Nanananda emphasises the experience of cessation as revealing the ending of the ordinary world of sense-contact.

But we must not silently equate every extraordinary state of diminished or absent perception with Nibbana.

Anaesthesia is not enlightenment.

Sleep is not arahantship.

And the ability to make ordinary sensations disappear does not prove that craving has been uprooted.

**The decisive question is whether the conditions of bondage have ended, not merely whether perceptions are temporarily absent.**

But now Nanananda makes a much more radical philosophical claim.

He argues that the ordinary world of experience is measured out through the six sense spheres.

And that when these cease in the relevant liberating realisation, the conceptual framework through which that world is constructed no longer applies.

Now the implications become extraordinary.

If the ordinary categories of things depend upon conditions of perception and contact, what exactly does it mean to ask whether something remains when those conditions have ceased?

That is where the Buddha's unanswered questions reveal their deepest significance.

## 10\. Sariputta refuses every answer, including the clever one

Two senior disciples are discussing the cessation of the six bases of sense-contact.

Mahakotthita asks Sariputta whether, after their complete cessation, something remains.

Sariputta tells him not to put the question that way.

Fine.

So he asks whether nothing remains.

Again, Sariputta refuses.

What about both?

No.

Neither?

Also no.

Now this is even more frustrating than the earlier question about the Tathagata's death.

Because the question has been reformulated.

We're no longer directly asking where a person went.

We're asking whether **anything** remains after the cessation of the six sense-contact spheres.

And Sariputta still refuses all four alternatives.

Why?

Because, he explains, the questions amount to conceptual proliferation concerning what is no longer within the relevant range of conceptual proliferation.

This is AN 4.173, the *Mahakotthita Sutta*\. The Chinese parallel SA 249 preserves the fourfold refusal, although its questioner is Ananda rather than Mahakotthita.

Now pay attention.

This is not simply saying\:

"Everything is mysterious, therefore stop asking questions."

The explanation is more precise.

The four alternatives employ categories whose applicability is tied to the conditions of the experienced and conceptualised world.

And Nanananda argues that these are precisely the conditions being brought to cessation.

So using the same conceptual machinery to demand an account of what remains becomes self-defeating.

### The computer that demanded its own answer after being switched off

Imagine a computer running an elaborate virtual environment.

Inside the simulation are objects.

Characters.

Locations.

A map.

Rules governing movement.

You can ask meaningful questions within that environment.

Where is the castle?

How far is the mountain?

Which character owns the treasure?

Now suppose the simulation stops running.

And somebody demands\:

"RIGHT. WHAT ARE THE COORDINATES OF THE CASTLE AFTER THE SIMULATION HAS CEASED?"

The question needs examination.

If the castle has a representation stored elsewhere, there may be a meaningful technical answer about data.

If a real physical castle exists independently of the simulation, that's another matter.

But if the question is specifically about coordinates *inside a simulation that is no longer running*, the demand may be using a framework outside the conditions under which it applies.

That's the analogy.

Not because the physical universe is literally a computer simulation.

Not because Buddhism proves that everything is made of information.

But because categories may depend upon a framework of conditions.

**The cessation of the conditions that make a question intelligible can render its usual alternatives inappropriate.**

And that's what Nanananda thinks Sariputta is explaining.

Now the crucial caution.

The canonical passage establishes that Sariputta rejects the four speculative alternatives and relates that rejection to the cessation of proliferation.

Nanananda goes further by identifying this with his broader account of the cessation of the six sense spheres and the non-manifestative consciousness discussed earlier.

That complete synthesis is his philosophical interpretation.

It is not simply an uncontested quotation from the early discourses.

But his insight is significant.

Perhaps the Buddha isn't refusing to tell you which metaphysical box contains the liberated person.

Perhaps he is explaining why the boxes were built from assumptions which no longer apply.

### The source itself complicates the conventional story

Analayo's Chinese parallel SA 249 is especially interesting because it preserves the fourfold refusal but identifies Ananda as the questioner.

And its continuation explicitly associates the cessation of the six contact spheres with release from conceptual falsification and Nibbana.

That supports the importance of the relationship between contact, proliferation and liberation, while showing that the conversation's transmitted details are not identical across recensions.

The precise textual differences matter.

But there's no need to turn them into a chanting exercise or a lesson in ancient grammar.

The argument is what matters.

**When the machinery of possessive conceptualisation stops, its categories cannot simply be treated as eternally applicable tools for describing the result.**

And now Nanananda makes a final claim.

Perhaps the entire universe of opposing standpoints depends upon a deeper circularity.

The dependence of consciousness upon name-and-form.

And name-and-form upon consciousness.

Like two bundles of reeds supporting each other.

When one falls, the other loses its support.

Nanananda closes Sermon 23 by returning to that reciprocal relationship and connecting its cessation with the ending of the world as dependently experienced.

And here comes the next question.

If the sense-contact structure is what sustains the ordinary world of proliferation, why does the mind keep experiencing that world as something perfectly stable and independently existing?

Why does it continuously rebuild the distinction between an owner and things owned?

And how can the Buddha understand every ordinary experience while being completely free of the standpoint that makes the ordinary person enslaved to it?

That's Sermon 24.

And this time, Mara himself is about to turn up and announce that the entire sensory universe belongs to him.


# Sermon 24 - Mara owns the entire universe, and the Buddha tells him to get stuffed

## 11\. The Buddha's opponent has an extremely ambitious property portfolio

Sermon 24 begins where Rohitassa's journey ended.

You cannot escape the world by travelling because the world relevant to liberation is bound up with the conditions through which it is experienced.

Nanananda now asks a more sinister question.

What exactly makes this conditioned world a realm of bondage?

And who is its apparent ruler?

Enter Mara.

In the Buddhist discourses, Mara is associated with death, temptation, delusion and the obstacles to liberation.

Sometimes he appears as a supernatural antagonist.

Sometimes the language of Mara points towards the conditions through which beings remain vulnerable to death and bondage.

Nanananda is particularly interested in the second dimension.

He draws upon a discourse in which the Buddha tells Radha to regard the five aggregates as belonging to Mara's domain.

Why?

Because form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness are all vulnerable to change and cessation.

When they are appropriated as an independent self, the person becomes subject to the suffering connected with that vulnerability.

The Chinese parallel SA 120 similarly associates bodily form and the aggregates with Mara.

Now imagine the ordinary person attempting to escape death.

They want the body to survive.

Or consciousness to survive.

Or an eternal soul to survive.

Or some subtle form of existence that remains beyond the reach of ordinary bodily mortality.

And Nanananda's response is devastating.

**You are trying to escape death by permanently preserving what you've already identified with the realm of death.**

It's like attempting to escape a burning building while insisting on carrying the flames with you.

Or fleeing a dangerous criminal while packing him into your suitcase because he's a cherished family possession.

The problem isn't that you've failed to travel far enough.

The problem is what you're carrying.

And now Mara makes this point rather enthusiastically himself.

## 12\. Mara turns up disguised as a farmer and announces universal ownership

Picture the Buddha teaching his monks.

Mara wants to interrupt.

So he disguises himself as a farmer.

He carries agricultural equipment.

His feet are muddy.

His appearance is suitably rustic.

And he approaches the Buddha with a wonderfully mundane question.

Has the Buddha seen his oxen?

The Buddha recognises him.

And Mara drops the disguise.

He begins boasting.

The eye belongs to him.

Visible forms belong to him.

Eye-contact and the corresponding sphere of consciousness belong to him.

The same applies to hearing, smelling, tasting, touching and thinking.

Then Mara demands to know where the Buddha intends to go to escape his domain.

It's an extraordinary scene from SN 4.19.

The cosmic landlord has arrived.

"YOUR EYES ARE MINE."

"YOUR EARS ARE MINE."

"YOUR THOUGHTS ARE MINE."

"YOUR ENTIRE SENSORY UNIVERSE IS COVERED UNDER THE TERMS OF MY LEASE."

And now he wants to know where the Buddha imagines he can go.

Because from Mara's perspective, everything available through ordinary sensory experience falls within his territory.

The Chinese parallel SA 246 preserves the confrontation, putting particular emphasis on Mara's claim over the spheres of contact.

### The Buddha's answer

The Buddha acknowledges the domain Mara is describing.

But then identifies a freedom beyond his reach.

Where the relevant sense-contact conditions no longer operate, Mara cannot establish his power.

Nanananda interprets this as a reference to the cessation of the six sense spheres and the ending of the ordinary world of conceptual proliferation.

Now we must be precise.

This does not mean that a living arahant is permanently incapable of seeing, hearing or thinking.

We've already established the canonical descriptions of living liberation.

Nor should the word *where* be treated automatically as a geographical location.

That's exactly the mistake Nanananda has been criticising.

He understands the passage as pointing towards a realisation of cessation beyond the ordinary sense-contact framework.

But he also makes the stronger claim that this cessation is Nibbana itself.

That relationship requires careful interpretation.

A temporary meditative cessation of ordinary perception is not automatically equivalent to the permanent eradication of greed, hatred and delusion.

And this is where Analayo's Chinese parallel is particularly informative.

SA 246 frames Mara's dominion primarily through the **spheres of contact** and describes freedom where those are absent.

The Pali passage spells out more explicitly the sense faculties, their objects and contact.

That shared emphasis on contact supports the doctrinal importance of the sensory process, while cautioning against turning one formulation into an uncontested account of every aspect of Nibbana.

Now here's the philosophical consequence.

**Mara's power depends upon conditions.**

And if the conditions of bondage cease, there is nothing for that power to operate upon in the relevant way.

Not because the Buddha has obtained a more powerful supernatural property.

Not because he has become the immortal landlord of a superior dimension.

But because the conditions sustaining Mara's influence have been ended.

That's a much more radical victory.

### Why Mara can't repossess what nobody is grasping

Imagine a debt collector arriving.

He has a folder.

A detailed account.

An enormous outstanding balance.

He announces that he's come to seize the debtor's property.

But suppose the relevant debt has genuinely been discharged.

There is no outstanding obligation.

The document no longer grants the power he claims.

He can stand outside shouting for as long as he likes.

The relationship upon which his authority depended has ceased.

Nanananda's argument has a similar structure.

The liberated mind is no longer bound through the craving and identification that sustain becoming.

The senses aren't permanently switched off during ordinary living activity.

But the defilements through which experience becomes renewed bondage have been eradicated.

And Nanananda additionally interprets the extraordinary cessation of sense-contact as a direct realisation beyond Mara's reach.

The key is understanding which claim we're making.

**Freedom from bondage is not simply the possession of a stronger defence against bondage. It's the ending of the relevant conditions.**

But if those conditions are bound up with consciousness, perception and contact, how do they actually arise?

Now Nanananda goes to the foundation.


## 13\. Consciousness doesn't get to be the eternal VIP at its own party

Consider the ordinary assumption.

There is consciousness.

It encounters a world.

The world changes.

Consciousness observes those changes.

And therefore, perhaps consciousness is the stable reality beneath everything else.

It's an attractive argument.

Especially after profound meditation.

The body can seem to disappear.

Thoughts can become quiet.

The ordinary sense of space may change.

Yet something is still known.

And the mind immediately announces\:

"AH. SO THE KNOWING MUST BE ETERNAL."

Not so fast.

Nanananda turns to the *Dvaya Sutta*, SN 35.93, which explains consciousness as dependent upon a pair of conditions.

Visual consciousness depends upon the eye and visible forms.

Auditory consciousness depends upon the ear and sounds.

And so on.

Even mental consciousness is discussed in dependence upon mind and mental phenomena.

The discourse emphasises that these supporting conditions are impermanent, and that the consciousness arising through them is likewise conditioned.

The Chinese parallel SA 214 preserves the corresponding analysis of consciousness dependent upon paired conditions.

Now here's why this is devastating.

The ordinary mind has been imagining consciousness as the permanent observer of changing things.

But the Buddha's analysis refuses to grant ordinary consciousness that independent status.

The seeing is conditioned.

The hearing is conditioned.

The thinking is conditioned.

And the objects, faculties and contacts involved are likewise subject to change.

**The observer isn't granted an exemption from dependent arising merely because observation occurs.**

But don't jump from that to an equally unsupported conclusion.

This doesn't prove that every philosophical account of consciousness is false.

Nor does it independently establish a complete contemporary neuroscience of conscious experience.

The point within the early Buddhist teaching is that the relevant forms of consciousness arise dependently and are not suitable foundations for conceiving an eternal independent self.

And that's enough to create a serious philosophical problem.

If the very act of experiencing depends upon changing conditions, what happens when you treat the experience as the foundation of an unchanging owner?

You've made a conditional process responsible for an unconditional guarantee.

The old mistake has returned.

Only now the self is hiding inside the word *awareness*\.

### The movement from contact to identity

Take something ordinary.

You see a person.

Visual consciousness occurs.

Recognition follows.

Perhaps attraction arises.

You begin imagining a relationship.

The relationship becomes desirable.

Then you imagine yourself possessing it.

Then you imagine how its loss would affect you.

Suddenly an entire personal drama has emerged.

A visual encounter has become a possible future identity.

Now imagine the same process occurring with an extraordinary meditative state.

An expansive experience of consciousness arises.

You recognise it.

Pleasure follows.

You interpret it as metaphysically significant.

You imagine yourself as the one who has realised ultimate consciousness.

You begin defending the interpretation.

And once again, conditioned experience has been converted into an identity.

**The sophistication of the object does not establish freedom from the activity of appropriation.**

That's why Nanananda continually returns to the conditionality of consciousness.

The ordinary mind wants to find an absolutely secure witness.

But the early Buddhist analysis challenges the independence of the very consciousness being nominated for the job.

And now the question becomes sharper.

If the senses and consciousness are conditioned, why does ordinary experience appear so continuous?

Why do we see permanent things rather than the ceaseless change through which they arise?

This is where Nanananda returns to his great cinematic analogy.

But with an important textual complication.


## 14\. The Buddha's cinema that might actually have been a painting-or a bird

Nanananda invokes a discourse in which the Buddha compares the mind's complexity with an elaborate picture.

The point is the extraordinary variety of mental activity.

Our thoughts and perceptions are immensely diverse.

But Nanananda develops the analogy into a modern cinema.

Individual frames pass rapidly before the spectator.

The successive images produce the appearance of continuous movement.

The audience becomes absorbed.

And the apparent world of the film seems to possess a stability that depends upon the conditions of projection and perception.

Nanananda uses this as an illustration of the way the mind overlooks the ceaseless change underlying ordinary experience.

But here's where Analayo's comparative material is genuinely delightful.

The Pali reference in SN 22.100 concerns a variegated picture.

The corresponding Chinese parallel, SA 267, instead refers to a colourful bird.

The two witnesses preserve the central theme of variety and mind, but the illustration differs.

Which means we shouldn't pretend the Buddha was literally describing a motion picture.

A movie is Nanananda's modern explanatory analogy.

Not the historical Buddha giving a lecture on cinema projection two millennia before the technology existed.

**The source concerns the mind's extraordinary variability. The cinematic treatment is Nanananda's interpretive extension.**

And that distinction matters.

Because a useful analogy can illuminate a doctrine without providing independent evidence for a particular theory of perception.

### The ultimate film critic

Now imagine somebody watching a film.

A character appears.

The character is threatened.

The audience reacts.

A dramatic event occurs.

The audience becomes excited.

Then somebody announces\:

"Actually, the character is being produced through a sequence of images."

Well, yes.

But that doesn't necessarily stop the spectator enjoying the story.

The insight concerns the difference between the appearance and the assumptions we make about the appearance.

Nanananda thinks ordinary experience involves a comparable mistake.

The mind treats changing processes as compact, independently existing things.

Then it makes those things into objects of craving.

And the craving helps sustain an apparently stable personal world.

Now the issue isn't whether the external world is literally an illusion projected from individual consciousness.

That's a much stronger claim.

The practical Buddhist point concerns the assumption of permanence and independent ownership imposed upon conditioned experience.

And Nanananda introduces a very precise antidote.

**The contemplation of impermanence.**

Not merely repeating that everything changes.

Actually investigating the conditioned character of what appears.


## 15\. The two ways to look at a beautiful object

Imagine seeing something beautiful.

An extraordinary piece of art.

A beautiful person.

A magnificent landscape.

A rare object you've wanted for years.

The mind recognises it.

Then desire arises.

You begin imagining possession.

Perhaps you're already considering how to keep it.

How to experience it again.

How to prevent its loss.

A world of future obligations emerges around the object.

Now consider another way of looking.

You recognise the beauty.

You appreciate the experience.

But you also understand its conditioned character.

The appearance depends upon circumstances.

Your response depends upon circumstances.

The pleasure is changing.

The object itself is vulnerable to alteration.

And none of this can provide an unconditional guarantee of happiness.

The mind doesn't have to declare the object worthless.

It doesn't have to hate beauty.

It needn't suppress pleasure.

**It simply understands that pleasure cannot be made into permanent security.**

Nanananda contrasts grasping at the signs of experience with an investigating attitude grounded in impermanence.

The first contributes to craving, conceit and conceptual proliferation.

The second can help weaken the tendency to identify with appearances and culminate in increasingly profound insight.

He supports the importance of impermanence with a passage whose Chinese parallel, SA 270, likewise connects its cultivation with the abandonment of craving and conceit.

And this is why insight can have a liberating function.

It doesn't merely introduce a new belief.

It changes the way the mind relates to experience.

Now there's a subtle distinction.

A person can know something is impermanent and still desperately want to possess it.

You know a holiday will end.

You know youth will fade.

You know a relationship may change.

The intellectual proposition is entirely familiar.

But the underlying demand remains.

The mind still hopes that *this particular thing* will somehow escape the rule.

So the mere thought *everything changes* doesn't establish liberation.

The insight must penetrate the compulsive relationship through which an object becomes a possession.

And this produces the next question.

If the Buddha understands the entire world as conditioned and incapable of supplying permanent possession, does he stop knowing things?

Does he become indifferent?

Or can he know the world more completely while refusing to appropriate it?

Nanananda thinks the answer is contained in an extraordinarily difficult discourse.

And he ends Sermon 24 by bringing us directly into it.


## 16\. The Buddha knows everything relevant-but refuses to stand on any of it

The *Kalakarama Sutta*, AN 4.24.

This is where the chapter becomes genuinely explosive.

The Buddha declares that whatever can be seen, heard, sensed, cognised, sought or considered within the world is known to him.

But then comes the crucial distinction.

He has understood it without becoming established upon it through the usual possessive activity.

He does not take the seen, heard, sensed or known as grounds for imagining an independent object-owner.

And this creates a strange problem.

Suppose somebody asks whether he knows what the world knows.

He cannot truthfully say he knows nothing.

But neither does his knowledge produce the possessive standpoint ordinarily associated with claiming to know.

Nanananda understands the discourse as an extraordinarily radical account of **knowledge without appropriation**\.

Now imagine a scholar.

They spend years studying something.

Eventually they understand it.

Then they announce\:

"I KNOW."

Then they become invested in being the person who knows.

Then somebody challenges their explanation.

And suddenly the entire project becomes a battle over the scholar's identity.

The knowledge has become a possession.

And the possession has begun possessing the scholar.

That's the ordinary problem.

But the Buddha's understanding, as Nanananda reads it, does not involve this additional appropriation.

He can know without requiring knowledge to establish an independent knower.

He can understand an object without making that object part of his personal identity.

He can employ concepts without becoming enslaved to the standpoint those concepts ordinarily support.

**The deeper knowledge doesn't produce a stronger grip. It makes the grip unnecessary.**

And this brings the whole series back to the *Mulapariyaya Sutta* from Chapters IV and V.

There, the ordinary person recognises an object and then constructs relationships of possession and identity around it.

The liberated person understands the object without the same additional conceiving.

The Buddha's knowing does not culminate in\:

"This belongs to me."

"I am this."

"I exist because I know."

"My understanding makes me an independent superior being."

The activity of appropriation has ceased.

### The most extraordinary philosophical reversal

Ordinarily, we imagine the ultimate knower as somebody who possesses the most comprehensive collection of knowledge.

A person who knows everything.

The supreme cosmic intelligence.

An infinite consciousness with perfect access to the universe.

But Nanananda's emphasis is different.

The Buddha's liberating distinction isn't merely an enormous quantity of information.

It's the absence of the ignorance and grasping through which knowledge becomes an object of possession.

Now this does not settle the separate canonical and historical debates about the scope of the Buddha's knowledge or omniscience.

In the classical commentary, the *Kalakarama Sutta* is connected with the Buddha's special knowledge and his freedom from defilements.

Nanananda emphasises how precisely the same higher understanding prevents the Buddha from taking a standpoint.

These are different explanatory emphases.

And Analayo's materials preserve the alternative translation by Bhikkhu Bodhi and Nanamoli's rendering of the related *Mulapariyaya Sutta*\. \[buddhistinquiry.org; buddhistinquiry.org\]

The distinction is important.

You shouldn't simply conclude that the Buddha is ignorant of the world because he doesn't become attached to it.

Nor that he refuses to state true propositions.

Nor that all propositions are equally true.

The teaching is not an invitation to intellectual irresponsibility.

**It is the distinction between understanding something and constructing an identity around understanding it.**

And now look how beautifully the argument has developed.

We began asking where a liberated person goes after death.

Nanananda questioned the independent person.

Then the independent world.

Then the conditions of contact.

Then the assumed permanence of consciousness.

And finally the activity through which knowledge becomes a possession.

What remains is an extraordinary possibility.

**A mind can understand the world without being bound to the world through the act of understanding.**

And Nanananda is now preparing to take that possibility to its limit.

Because if the world is such a vast field of constructed appearances, and the mind continually mistakes those appearances for independently existing realities, then the next question is obvious.

**What exactly is the trick?**

And how does the Buddha see through it without simply replacing one illusion with another?

That's where Chapter IX will begin.

With Nanananda's remarkable treatment of the world as a magic show.


# The final cut - Death is not the problem you thought it was

Let's return to the question that began the chapter.

**What happens to someone who is liberated and dies?**

Within the Buddhist account, the aggregates of the living organism cease at death.

And because ignorance and craving have been eradicated, no renewed becoming or rebirth follows.

But the teaching doesn't require us to imagine an eternal independent person being transported somewhere.

Nor does it allow us to describe the annihilation of a permanent owner which was never established.

And Nanananda argues that the ordinary questions about survival and annihilation are frequently misguided because they presuppose an independent self.

That is the central doctrinal answer.

Now consider what Sermons 22-24 have added.

**Sermon 22 showed why annihilationism isn't the answer.**

Yamaka imagined that an arahant must be destroyed at death.

Sariputta exposed the assumption concealed within that claim.

The five aggregates are conditioned.

They are not an independently existing self.

And the end of their continuation does not entail the annihilation of an eternal entity.

The assassin disguised as a trusted servant revealed the danger of making the aggregates into permanent possessions.

The real problem was the ignorance through which the victim trusted what could not provide ultimate security.

**Sermon 23 showed why searching for an eternal destination isn't the answer.**

Rohitassa travelled for a hundred years.

He possessed extraordinary powers.

But the end of the world remained unreachable.

Why?

Because the problem wasn't physical distance.

The problem concerned the conditions through which a world becomes experienced and grasped.

The magic kettle showed how opposing theories can arise from misunderstanding a circular process.

And Sariputta's refusal of the four speculative alternatives concerning the cessation of sense-contact showed why some questions are inappropriate to the very cessation being investigated.

**Sermon 24 showed what actually binds us to the world.**

Mara claimed dominion over the senses and contact.

The Buddha pointed towards a freedom beyond that dominion.

Nanananda examined the conditionality of consciousness and the mistake of treating changing sensory processes as independent permanent realities.

Then he brought us to the *Kalakarama Sutta*\.

And there the deepest distinction emerged.

The Buddha knows the world.

But he doesn't become established upon it through craving, conceit and views.

**Knowledge need not become ownership.**

Now here is the point I think makes the entire chapter worth hearing.

The ordinary mind is frightened of death because it has constructed an owner of experience whose existence must somehow be secured.

You imagine\:

*I am the person who sees.*

*I am the person who knows.*

*I am the person who remembers.*

*I am the person who must continue.*

And then the prospect of death becomes terrifying because those very processes are vulnerable to cessation.

So you search for something that can survive.

Perhaps a soul.

Perhaps an eternal witness.

Perhaps a cosmic consciousness.

Perhaps a heavenly destination.

Or perhaps, in despair, you embrace the opposite conviction.

Nothing survives.

Everything is annihilated.

But in either case, the same conceptual problem may remain.

You are still attempting to determine the destiny of an independent owner whose existence you have not demonstrated.

And Nanananda is asking you to undertake a more radical investigation.

**What happens if the need to construct that owner itself comes to an end?**

Not through suppressing thought.

Not by pretending the physical body doesn't exist.

Not by refusing ordinary responsibilities.

And not by converting Nibbana into a blank unconscious state.

But through understanding the dependent processes of experience so thoroughly that the ignorance sustaining appropriation is uprooted.

Then something extraordinary becomes possible.

You no longer need the world to guarantee the permanent survival of an imagined independent self.

You no longer need pleasure to establish the value of your existence.

You no longer need knowledge to confirm your superiority.

You no longer need the afterlife to provide an eternal territory in which the self can finally be secure.

The question itself has lost its compelling force.

And that is the reversal Nanananda wants us to understand.

**The answer to death isn't necessarily the discovery of something that can never die. It is the ending of the bondage through which death becomes a threat to an imagined permanent owner.**

The traditional Buddhist teaching makes the further claim that this liberation ends renewed rebirth.

That claim is not independently established simply by analysing ordinary psychological experience.

But within the doctrinal system, its causal structure is clear.

Ignorance and craving sustain becoming.

The path uproots them.

Their cessation ends the possibility of renewed bondage.

And now we can return to the Buddha's apparently frustrating silence.

Why does he refuse to tell us where the liberated person goes?

Because answering the question in the ordinary way may preserve precisely the misunderstanding that liberation removes.

You keep asking\:

**"But what happens to ME?"**

And the teaching keeps directing you towards the more fundamental question\:

**"What is this ME you are trying to preserve?"**

Not to humiliate you.

Not to make existence seem meaningless.

And not to provide the world's most elaborate philosophical non-answer.

But because understanding the question may be the key to ending the suffering that made you ask it.

You began the series wanting to know what Nibbana is.

Then you wanted to know why you couldn't see it.

Then how consciousness constructs experience.

Then what keeps the machinery operating.

Then how that machinery produces someone called *me*\.

Then whether experience can continue without an independent owner.

Then why release can be supreme happiness without being an intensified pleasurable feeling.

And now you've discovered that even the fear of death can involve the same fundamental assumption.

**There must be an independent owner whose fate needs to be secured.**

But what if liberation doesn't secure the owner?

What if it ends the bondage of ownership?

Then the entire structure changes.

The fire doesn't need a destination.

The whirlpool doesn't need an immortal centre.

The sandcastle doesn't need an eternal kingdom.

And the mind doesn't need to build a final metaphysical possession called *my Nibbana*\.

The compulsion to possess can cease.

That is the freedom Nanananda has been trying to make intelligible.

But now a final question remains.

If ordinary consciousness constructs such a convincing world of apparently permanent things, how does the trick actually operate?

What gives appearances their solidity?

Why does the mind keep falling for the performance?

And how can wisdom expose the illusion without requiring the physical world to disappear?

That is the doorway into Chapter IX.

**The Buddha knows it's a magic show. The rest of us are still arguing with the magician.**


**End of Chapter VIII - Sermons 22-24 of 33.**
