# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter VII of X · Sermons 19-21 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, with Analayo's comparative materials

## If craving ends, does happiness disappear too?

**The answer: What disappears is the dependence upon pleasurable experience for happiness.**

But Nanananda is going to make a much more radical claim.

The Buddha's supreme happiness is not simply the greatest amount of pleasurable feeling.

It is the ending of the very conditions through which feeling becomes a source of bondage.

Which means something rather extraordinary.

You could experience the most exquisite pleasure imaginable, and it would still be inferior to Nibbana.

Not because pleasure is inherently evil.

Not because a liberated person is forbidden from enjoying anything.

But because the most extraordinary pleasure remains vulnerable to change.

And if your happiness depends upon possessing it, you have purchased suffering along with the pleasure.

You haven't escaped the problem.

You've simply acquired a more magnificent version.

**Nibbana isn't the ultimate pleasurable possession. It's freedom from the need to possess pleasure.**

And here's the question that makes this chapter genuinely difficult.

Suppose every feeling ceases.

No pleasure.

No pain.

No neutral sensation.

Nothing whatsoever is being felt.

How the heck can that be called happiness?

Surely happiness must be something somebody experiences.

Otherwise why would anyone want it?

Nanananda's answer is going to take us through the death of the Buddha, the ending of karmic debts, the fire that goes nowhere, and finally the collapse of the very assumption that there must be a person to possess liberation.

This is the actual progression of Sermons 19-21.

And we begin with a problem inherited directly from Chapter VI.


# Sermon 19 - How can you know that death has lost its power before you die?

## 1\. The arahant is free. So why is there still a body?

We've established something very important.

An arahant has destroyed greed, hatred and delusion.

But the living organism continues.

The senses function.

There can still be bodily pleasure and pain.

The person can become hungry.

The body ages.

Eventually it dies.

Now here's a philosophical problem.

Buddhist texts distinguish Nibbana with a remainder from final Nibbana without a remainder.

Does that mean the living arahant is only partially liberated?

Is death required to complete the achievement?

Is enlightenment effectively a two-stage subscription?

**Nibbana Basic\:** No greed, hatred or delusion, but your knees still hurt.

**Nibbana Premium\:** Available upon termination of your biological contract.

And presumably the premium plan includes access to whatever supernatural benefits the marketing department has invented.

Nanananda says this is the wrong model.

His central argument in Sermon 19 is that the realisation of liberation is already complete during life. The remaining body is not evidence that some further portion of the defilements must be removed at death.

This is subtle.

When he speaks of two aspects of Nibbana, he doesn't want us imagining two different kinds of liberation.

One for the living.

Another mysteriously awarded after death.

He wants us to understand the relationship between a complete realisation and the continuing conditioned organism.

And to do that, he brings forward one of the most important images in the entire series.

## 2\. The oil lamp that knows its fuel is exhausted

Imagine an oil lamp.

It burns because there is oil and a wick.

The flame depends upon those conditions.

When the supplies are exhausted and no replacement is provided, the burning ceases.

Now suppose you're watching the lamp.

You understand exactly what sustains it.

You see that no further oil is being supplied.

You understand that the remaining fuel will be exhausted.

Do you need to travel into the future to know what will happen?

No.

You understand the relevant conditions.

And their consequences.

Nanananda draws on the *Dhatuvibhanga Sutta*, MN 140, where the Buddha relates the exhaustion of a lamp's fuel to the cooling of feelings at the end of a liberated person's life. \[Sermon 19 - Nibbana; buddhistinquiry.org\]

Now this is the key.

The arahant does not need to die, travel into some mysterious realm, and send a postcard back to confirm liberation.

The knowledge of freedom from renewed becoming has already arisen.

Within the Buddhist framework, the causes of future rebirth have been eradicated.

The continuation of the present organism does not invalidate that knowledge.

And this is what Nanananda finds so astonishing.

**The liberated person knows the end of the process through understanding its conditions.**

Not by predicting an arbitrary future event.

Not by having blind faith in a supernatural promise.

But through knowing the exhaustion of the causes of bondage.

That is the claim.

And it has enormous implications.

### The person who is still trying to become immortal

Ordinarily, fear of death makes us look for something that can survive it.

A soul.

An eternal consciousness.

A heavenly body.

A permanent essence.

Some independently existing *me* that will somehow escape the organism's fate.

The proposed solutions vary.

But the psychological structure is remarkably similar.

**Find something I can identify with that death cannot destroy.**

Now Nanananda turns the question around.

What if the very identification you are attempting to preserve is part of the process that sustains bondage?

What if liberation doesn't require protecting an eternal owner?

What if it requires ending the conditions through which an owner is imagined and grasped?

Then deathlessness would mean something quite different from immortality.

Not\:

*I have discovered the eternal substance that I really am.*

But\:

*The conditions responsible for continued bondage have ceased.*

And within the Buddhist teaching, that includes the ending of renewed birth.

The living arahant doesn't become biologically immortal.

The body still dies.

But the complete freedom from craving is already realised.

This is Nanananda's great distinction.

And it leads directly to our central question.

**If the liberated person can still feel pleasure and pain, in what sense have feelings cooled?**


## 3\. The pleasure that doesn't require pleasure

Nanananda introduces an expression we can translate simply as **happiness without feeling**\.

He relates it to the extraordinary concentration of arahant fruition discussed in the previous chapters.

In that attainment, he argues, the usual processes of feeling have ceased, yet the realisation is described as supreme peace.

The living arahant can subsequently resume ordinary sensory functioning without losing the irreversible liberation already attained.

Now the objection is obvious.

How can the absence of feeling be happiness?

Suppose you enjoy a delicious meal.

You feel pleasure.

That makes sense.

Suppose you hear exquisite music.

You experience delight.

Perfectly intelligible.

Suppose you embrace someone you love.

There's warmth, affection, bodily sensation.

Again, we understand why the experience is desirable.

But now remove feeling.

What remains that could possibly deserve the name *happiness*?

And here is the crucial distinction.

**Happiness does not always mean the presence of a pleasurable sensation.**

Consider relief.

You have been carrying an enormously heavy burden.

Your arms are aching.

Your shoulders are exhausted.

You finally put it down.

There may be pleasant bodily feelings.

But the central significance of the event is not necessarily the production of an extraordinary pleasurable sensation.

It is that the burden is no longer being carried.

A constraint has ended.

A source of suffering has ceased.

Now imagine the removal of something much deeper than a physical burden.

The exhaustion of craving itself.

No compulsive demand that present experience become something else.

No desperate need for an independent self to secure its future.

No hatred requiring the world to submit to your preferences.

No delusion organising experience around an imaginary permanent owner.

And no further becoming sustained by these causes.

The Buddhist tradition calls this supreme peace.

Not because it is the most intense pleasant feeling.

But because the very structure of bondage has ended.

### But surely the absence of pain isn't automatically happiness?

Exactly.

And this is where we need to be more precise than the usual spiritual clichés.

Being unconscious is not necessarily Nibbana.

General anaesthesia is not arahantship.

A person entering dreamless sleep hasn't necessarily eradicated greed, hatred and delusion.

And simply removing sensory stimulation does not establish liberation.

The absence of feeling is insufficient by itself.

Nanananda's claim concerns the **liberating realisation of cessation**, not merely a blank gap in experience.

That is an interpretive claim about the special attainment he associates with arahantship.

The early Buddhist tradition has other ways of explaining happiness beyond feeling, and identifying the relevant passages exclusively with fruition concentration is not uncontested.

But his essential argument is cutting.

**The value of liberation is not measured by the quantity of pleasant sensation it produces.**

And that means the entire ordinary project of happiness may have been misdirected.

We have spent our lives trying to accumulate desirable feelings.

Maybe we've been attempting to solve the wrong problem.


## 4\. The terrible mistake of thinking happiness is something you must continually produce

Imagine a man with an extraordinary machine.

Every time he presses a button, it produces an intensely pleasurable sensation.

He presses it.

Wonderful.

Again.

More pleasure.

Again.

Even better.

Eventually he concludes\:

"I have solved the problem of human existence."

He has constructed a machine capable of generating limitless enjoyment.

But now suppose the machine breaks.

Immediately he becomes desperate.

His entire happiness depends upon something that must continue functioning.

He repairs it.

Protects it.

Worries about the power supply.

Fears theft.

Becomes anxious about the possibility of a permanent breakdown.

And the extraordinary thing is that the more absolutely his happiness depends upon the machine, the more vulnerable he becomes.

The machine may generate pleasure.

But it does not generate unconditional security.

**Pleasure and freedom from bondage are different achievements.**

Now don't jump to the opposite extreme.

Pleasure is not inherently bad.

Food is not inherently evil.

Friendship is not a spiritual failure.

The Buddha doesn't require the living arahant to become incapable of appreciating the world.

But when pleasant feeling becomes the foundation of your imagined security, every pleasant event carries the possibility of future distress.

You want it.

You obtain it.

You fear losing it.

You lose it.

You want it again.

And the mind begins organising its existence around the repetition of an unstable condition.

This is the mechanism of bondage.

So what if happiness were defined differently?

Not by the production of something you must retain.

But by the ending of what continually demands retention.

That is the direction Nanananda is taking us.

And now he asks an even more serious question.

**How does the arahant know that the freedom already realised will not be destroyed by death?**


## 5\. The Buddha dies without travelling to a better address

Nanananda turns to the Buddha's final passing.

The body is failing.

The Buddha is approaching death.

And the monk Anuruddha describes the scene.

The Buddha remains composed.

The pain does not overthrow the liberation of mind.

The body eventually ceases.

Nanananda considers this a decisive illustration of the difference between physical death and the freedom already realised before it.

Now imagine the ordinary interpretation.

The Buddha has lived for decades after awakening.

But perhaps the real prize is still waiting.

At death, he will finally enter the ultimate realm.

An eternal refuge.

A permanent state somewhere beyond the ordinary cosmos.

Something that was not fully available while the body remained alive.

Nanananda objects.

He argues that this reverses the meaning of liberation.

The Buddha doesn't die in order to acquire a new state of peace.

He dies **having already realised peace**\.

The freedom precedes the body's final cessation.

The body doesn't have to die before the central work is complete.

And that gives his interpretation an extraordinary sharpness.

Death cannot confiscate the unshakeable liberation because that liberation is not a possession dependent upon the body's continued existence.

It is the ending of the conditions through which renewed becoming would arise.

### The difference between the text and Nanananda's reading

The underlying canonical verses describe the Buddha's final passing and composure.

Nanananda argues that a commentarial explanation wrongly makes the Buddha's death a movement *towards* peace.

He instead reads the passage as describing the Buddha's mind already established in peace before death.

This is a substantive exegetical disagreement.

Analayo's Sermon 19 materials place the canonical passage alongside translations by Maurice Walshe and Bhikkhu Bodhi, and Chinese parallels to the Buddha's final passing. Those comparisons establish the complexity of the textual evidence, though they don't by themselves settle every aspect of Nanananda's interpretation.

And we should be particularly cautious about claims concerning the internal meditative experience of the Buddha at the moment of death.

These are transmitted religious accounts.

They are not independent medical or scientific observations.

But their philosophical function is clear.

**The liberated person need not wait for death to acquire freedom from death's power over becoming.**

And that leads to a beautiful distinction between faith and knowledge.


## 6\. The general who knows when he is relying on faith

Nanananda introduces the military general Siha.

Siha is a devoted supporter of the Buddha.

He asks about the benefits of generosity.

The Buddha identifies several benefits visible in ordinary life.

A generous person becomes dear to others.

People of integrity associate with them.

They acquire a good reputation.

They can enter assemblies with confidence.

Siha understands these benefits from his own experience.

Then the Buddha identifies another benefit.

A generous person may enjoy a fortunate rebirth.

And Siha makes a remarkably clear distinction.

He knows the ordinary benefits.

But the claim concerning future rebirth is something he accepts on trust in the Buddha.

Nanananda uses the contrast to distinguish confidence based on direct knowledge from confidence concerning something not yet personally verified.

Now here's why this matters.

The Buddha's path is not intended to remain forever a collection of propositions believed because an enlightened teacher said them.

It directs the practitioner towards realisation.

In Nanananda's interpretation, the arahant has understood the ending of the causes of rebirth.

They no longer need to rely on faith concerning the cessation of their own becoming.

They know the relevant causes have ended.

And that is how Nanananda explains the extraordinary certainty traditionally attributed to an arahant.

But here's the important distinction for us.

**The text's assertion of a liberated person's direct knowledge is not an independently verified proof of rebirth or its cessation.**

A modern reader can understand the internal logic of the teaching without pretending the empirical and metaphysical questions have been settled for everybody.

The Buddhist argument is that ignorance and craving sustain rebirth.

Arahantship eradicates them.

Therefore the conditions for renewed becoming are exhausted.

That is the doctrinal inference, presented within the tradition as realised knowledge.

And Nanananda thinks it transforms our entire relationship to death.

Not because we now possess an eternal immortal self.

But because the need to secure such a self has ended.

### The arahant who has finished the training

Nanananda then brings forward a curious Dhammapada verse.

On its surface, it sounds like the Buddha is praising somebody faithless, ungrateful, destructive and associated with thoroughly unappealing things.

What an extraordinary spiritual role model.

But the verse uses deliberately ambiguous expressions.

Its deeper interpretation concerns a person who has passed beyond dependence on faith through direct knowledge, severed the links sustaining rebirth, and exhausted desire.

Nanananda uses this wordplay to describe the arahant as someone whose training has been completed.

We don't need to dissect the words to appreciate the point.

The arahant isn't someone who has become incapable of learning ordinary things.

They can still acquire practical information.

But the specific liberating work of the Noble Path has been completed.

No further abandonment of the defilements remains necessary.

And now notice what this means for happiness.

Ordinarily, you imagine your future happiness depends upon becoming somebody better.

You must learn more.

Acquire more.

Improve more.

Purify more.

Attain more.

But in the completed path, the project of becoming liberated has fulfilled its purpose.

The mind doesn't need to maintain an eternal ambition to become free.

**The possibility of no longer needing to become anything is itself central to the peace.**

And now Nanananda introduces a startling image.

A floral arrangement.

One that will eventually bring us back to the very first question of this series.


## 7\. The floral arrangement that dissolves when the glue is removed

Imagine somebody constructing an elaborate design from flowers.

The petals are arranged.

The colours correspond.

Threads hold parts together.

Glue keeps the structure in place.

Knots secure the decorations.

The completed arrangement looks like one substantial thing.

But the apparent unity depends upon the conditions holding it together.

Now suppose you remove the glue.

Untie the knots.

Disentangle the threads.

The arrangement loses the structure that made it appear to be a single independent object.

Nanananda uses this image to describe conditioned becoming. In his analogy, craving supplies the glue, views produce tangles, and conceit makes knots.

And here comes his most radical interpretive move.

He suggests that the relationship between the conditioned and the unconditioned can be understood through the undoing of those very supports.

You don't need to transport the floral arrangement into an unconditioned universe.

The structure collapses when the conditions sustaining it cease.

Now we must be precise.

This is **Nanananda's distinctive account** of the unconditioned, not a universally accepted Theravada metaphysics.

Classical Theravada generally treats Nibbana as an unconditioned reality, not as a conditioned object transformed into another state through rearrangement.

Nanananda's criticism is that substantialising Nibbana risks recreating an object for the mind to grasp.

He wants the emphasis on cessation.

And from the standpoint of practice, the analogy is extremely powerful.

You're trying to secure a self which has been constructed through craving, conceit and views.

You spend your life tightening the knots.

Protecting the arrangement.

Strengthening the glue.

And every attempt to secure it makes the structure more important.

Then the Buddha points towards the possibility of understanding the conditions and relinquishing them.

**The answer isn't a stronger knot. It's the ending of the need to keep tying them.**

But then what about final Nibbana?

Does the arrangement travel somewhere after its supports collapse?

Nanananda now confronts that fantasy through two wonderfully absurd creatures.

## 8\. The fish who cannot imagine dry land

Nanananda describes two popular explanatory parables about Nibbana.

In one, a tortoise leaves the water and experiences dry land.

The fish ask what land is like.

Is it wet?

Does it flow?

Can you swim through it?

The tortoise keeps answering no.

The fish are bewildered.

The conclusion drawn by teachers who employ the parable is that fish cannot understand the radically different reality of land.

Likewise, ordinary consciousness supposedly cannot understand the transcendent realm of Nibbana.

A similar story uses a frog and a water-dwelling creature.

It's an appealing analogy.

But Nanananda objects.

Because it quietly assumes what the Buddhist teaching is supposed to investigate.

**There must be some separate territory called Nibbana into which the liberated person travels.**

We've merely made the territory difficult to describe.

The underlying idea of a destination remains intact.

Nanananda argues that this does not adequately explain the Buddha's teaching on dependent arising, cessation and not-self.

Now here's the point.

If you tell someone that Nibbana is an infinitely wonderful place which cannot be described through ordinary experience, what happens?

The mind begins imagining it.

Perhaps endless light.

Perfect peace.

A consciousness beyond space and time.

The ultimate freedom from every limitation.

And then the mind decides\:

**"I WANT TO GO THERE."**

The old activity has reappeared.

A future owner.

A desirable possession.

A destination.

And an entire project of acquisition.

Nanananda's criticism is that the parable can reinforce the very assumptions the teaching seeks to dismantle.

This doesn't settle every philosophical question concerning the reality of the unconditioned.

But it exposes a practical danger.

**You can turn the ending of craving into the ultimate thing to crave.**

### The arahant who left no ashes

Sermon 19 also returns to the canonical story of Dabba Mallaputta, an arahant associated with extraordinary meditative powers.

According to the *Udana*, at the end of his life Dabba rises into the air, enters a fire-element attainment, and his body is consumed without leaving ashes.

Nanananda takes the narrative as an illustration of complete extinguishment: no residual object is left through which one might imagine the liberated person travelling to another destination.

This is a traditional miraculous account. It should not be presented as independently verified physiology.

And the philosophical conclusion doesn't require believing that monks can literally incinerate themselves in midair.

The essential point is the fire.

Once burning has ceased, asking where the fire went misunderstands what burning was.

And once again, Nanananda isn't claiming the liberation of an arahant is ordinary annihilation.

He is questioning the presupposition that there was an independently existing self which must either survive or be destroyed.

**Extinguishment is not the transportation of a fiery substance.**

And that gives Sermon 19 its final conclusion.

The Buddha's achievement is not that he has found a better address after death.

It is that the conditions sustaining suffering and renewed becoming have been brought to an end.

The liberation is realised while alive.

The body eventually dies.

And no further becoming occurs.

But now listen to the question this creates.

If the ultimate goal is cessation rather than the acquisition of an eternal experience, why does the Buddha keep praising it as the highest happiness?

Nanananda has been preparing that question.

And in Sermon 20, he finally states the answer with extraordinary force.


# Sermon 20 - The happiness that bankrupts the entire pleasure industry

## 9\. What if your understanding of happiness is completely upside down?

Sermon 20 begins with a remarkable identification.

Nanananda brings together supreme knowledge, unshakeable liberation, happiness and freedom from debt.

These aren't presented as four unrelated spiritual achievements.

They describe aspects of the completed release from the bonds of becoming.

And the most startling phrase is **happiness without feeling**\.

Nanananda takes this to refer to the supreme peace of cessation, which he connects with the attainment of arahant fruition. The verses he cites from the *Anguttara Nikaya* describe liberation as unshakeable, unsurpassed in happiness and free from debt. Analayo's notes compare Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation and a Chinese *Madhyama-agama* parallel, MA 125, which similarly associates Nibbana with freedom from affliction and unshakeable peace. \[Sermon 20 - Nibbana; buddhistinquiry.org\]

Now think carefully about this.

Ordinarily, we define happiness in terms of experiences.

Good experiences make us happy.

Bad experiences make us unhappy.

And the entire task of life appears to involve maximising the former while minimising the latter.

More pleasure.

Less pain.

More exciting relationships.

More satisfying achievements.

More beautiful experiences.

More extraordinary states of consciousness.

An endless optimisation problem.

But Nanananda is asking whether that framework misses a deeper dimension of suffering.

Because every pleasurable experience depends upon conditions.

And the mind can become bound through its demand that those conditions continue.

So imagine you've successfully maximised pleasure.

You've obtained extraordinary health.

Magnificent food.

Wonderful relationships.

An exquisite environment.

Profound meditative bliss.

Everything is perfect.

Now ask one question.

**Can you guarantee that none of it will change?**

No.

And if your well-being requires that guarantee, your achievement has not solved the underlying problem.

You've made yourself dependent upon an extraordinarily elaborate collection of unstable conditions.

Congratulations.

You have assembled the greatest pleasure portfolio in human history.

Unfortunately, the entire thing is exposed to market risk.

And the chief risk is not merely that something may disappear.

It's that you believe your happiness must disappear with it.

### The happiness that doesn't have to be maintained

Now consider another possibility.

The mind no longer compulsively demands pleasant experiences.

It can enjoy them.

But it does not require their continuation.

It can encounter unpleasant experience.

But it doesn't construct an independent owner whose existence must be justified or defended through resistance.

The underlying greed, hatred and delusion have ended.

Within the Buddhist framework, the causes of renewed becoming have been exhausted.

And the mind is no longer engaged in the perpetual struggle to secure itself through what it experiences.

Now what is the value of that freedom?

It isn't measured by the intensity of a particular feeling.

It is the freedom from the structural vulnerability created by craving.

**The mind no longer has to purchase peace from a world incapable of guaranteeing it.**

This is why Nanananda thinks the Buddha can describe cessation as supreme happiness.

The happiness is not an exceptionally large sensation.

It concerns the ending of the bondage that makes sensations into the conditions of one's existential security.

But we should avoid an easy mistake.

The Buddhist distinction between pleasurable feeling and liberating happiness does not mean every experience of relief, relaxation or emotional quiet is Nibbana.

Nor does it mean unconsciousness is somehow supreme bliss.

Nanananda is speaking about the realisation of the cessation of craving.

He links it with a distinctive meditative attainment.

Other Buddhist interpreters distinguish Nibbana as unconditioned from the conditioned consciousness that realises it.

That disagreement matters.

But the common point is sufficiently sharp\:

**The highest happiness is not defined by possessing the greatest possible quantity of pleasurable feeling.**

And that produces an extraordinary inversion of ordinary values.

What the world thinks is happiness may remain bound to suffering.

And what the world fears as cessation may be precisely the ending of suffering.


## 10\. The karmic debt collector has been put out of business

Nanananda now develops the image of debt.

Imagine owing money.

A great deal of money.

The interest accumulates.

Every month you make a payment.

But the outstanding balance barely changes.

You earn more.

You pay more.

More interest appears.

The debt becomes the central fact around which your existence is organised.

You work to pay it.

Worry about it.

Plan around it.

Fear what will happen if you cannot meet your obligations.

Now imagine the entire debt is finally extinguished.

Not deferred.

Not refinanced.

Not transferred into a longer mortgage.

**Gone.**

There may be pleasure associated with receiving the news.

But the decisive significance is that the debt no longer exists.

You no longer need to organise your life around repayment.

A whole structure of compulsion has ended.

Nanananda compares the continuing cycle of karmic action and rebirth to a debt that accumulates further obligations. In arahantship, the seeds sustaining renewed becoming are exhausted; no fresh rebirth-producing debt is incurred.

Now notice the distinction.

Ordinary pleasure may be like receiving a bonus while still owing an enormous amount of money.

Wonderful.

But the debt remains.

Even extraordinary pleasure doesn't necessarily free you from the mechanism that continually generates further demands.

Nibbana is compared to the ending of the debt itself.

And that is why it can be superior to pleasure without being an intensified pleasant feeling.

### The most important detail: nobody needs to keep paying

Suppose you've finally cleared a debt.

Now imagine you continue making repayments because you've become so accustomed to paying them.

Every month\:

"Right. Time to send another thousand pounds to the lender."

"But the debt is gone."

"Yes, but I'm a highly disciplined individual."

"There's nothing left to pay."

"That sounds suspiciously like an excuse for spiritual laziness."

Mate.

You've completed the task.

The activity no longer has a purpose.

This is the significance of the arahant being someone who has finished the training.

The path is necessary while its work remains incomplete.

But the completed liberation doesn't require an endless activity of becoming liberated.

And Nanananda's description of supreme debtlessness is especially powerful because it explains why cessation is not simply another acquisition.

**The value lies in what no longer has to be done.**

The compulsive project has ended.

The further becoming it sustained has been exhausted.

Now the question becomes rather terrifying.

If you no longer have to continue existing through the structures of craving, what happens when the body dies?

Does the liberated person survive?

Or disappear?

And why does the Buddha refuse to give the answer everybody wants?


## 11\. The fire that cannot be found on Google Maps

Nanananda returns to the story of Dabba Mallaputta and the image of a fire that has gone out.

A fire burns.

It consumes fuel.

When the fuel is exhausted, it ceases.

And now somebody asks\:

"Where did it go?"

East?

West?

North?

South?

Into the atmosphere?

Into a subtler dimension?

Does it currently exist in a realm of Pure Fire where all the extinguished flames live forever?

What the heck are we talking about?

The question treats burning as an independent object which must have changed location.

But the fire was a conditioned process.

Once the relevant conditions ceased, the burning ended.

Nanananda argues that asking where the fire went fundamentally misunderstands the process. He takes this as the key to the Buddha's refusal to locate a liberated person after death.

Now here's the genuinely difficult part.

You cannot simply use the analogy to prove ordinary annihilation.

Why?

Because annihilation ordinarily presupposes that there was a substantial entity whose existence has now been destroyed.

But the Buddhist analysis has already challenged the assumption of an independently existing self.

So the options aren't\:

An eternal self survives.

Or an eternal self is annihilated.

There is a third possibility.

The conditioned process through which a self was imagined has ceased to be sustained.

The categories of eternal survival and annihilation may therefore be inappropriate to the thing under investigation.

**The fire wasn't a hidden object travelling through its own flames.**

And the liberated person isn't established as an eternal soul travelling through the aggregates.

But Nanananda presses this even further.

He criticises explanations that imagine the liberated person entering some mysterious, indescribable realm after death.

In his view, those explanations preserve the idea of a destination even while insisting it cannot be described.

The old assumption survives.

There must be somewhere for the self to go.

And the mind becomes attached to that imagined destination.

It's Nibbana with a metaphysical forwarding address.

### Does that prove the classical Theravada interpretation wrong?

Not automatically.

We need to be careful here.

Classical Theravada can describe Nibbana as an unconditioned ultimate reality without treating it as a spatial location.

A sophisticated Abhidhamma account does not have to imagine arahants travelling to a heavenly apartment complex.

Nanananda nevertheless thinks that certain substantialist interpretations obscure the early discourses' emphasis on cessation and non-appropriation.

His criticism is philosophically serious.

But it doesn't establish that every account of an unconditioned reality is secretly eternalist.

That's the actual disagreement.

And the distinction matters because we'll eventually need to decide what, if anything, can legitimately be said about Nibbana beyond the ending of suffering.

But Nanananda's next question is much sharper.

**If a liberated person doesn't possess an independent eternal self, what exactly continues from one life to another before liberation?**

Now we've reached the mechanism of rebirth.


## 12\. The flame carried by the wind

The wandering ascetic Vacchagotta approaches the Buddha.

He wants to know about rebirth.

Suppose a fire is blown by the wind from one place to another.

What sustains the flame during its movement?

The Buddha replies using the image of the wind as its support.

Vacchagotta then asks the corresponding question about a being who has died and is connected with another birth.

What supplies the continuity?

The Buddha identifies craving as the crucial supporting condition.

Nanananda discusses this exchange from SN 44.9 in detail.

Now this is remarkable.

The explanation does not require an independently existing soul physically travelling through space.

Instead, the continuity of becoming depends upon conditions.

The flame is not an eternal little object flying from one piece of fuel to another.

Its apparent continuity depends upon an ongoing process of combustion and support.

Likewise, the Buddhist account locates the condition of renewed becoming in craving rather than in a permanent soul.

And that makes the cessation of craving enormously significant.

If craving sustains renewed becoming, then uprooting craving ends the relevant process.

The fire is no longer being supplied.

But Nanananda now enters a genuine textual disagreement.

### The rebirth passage that doesn't say exactly the same thing in every version

The Pali textual tradition contains an ambiguity about the condition of the being between death and rebirth.

One reading suggests the being has already reached another body.

Another suggests the being has not yet been reborn.

Nanananda prefers the first.

He believes it better fits the flame analogy and undermines the idea of an intermediate existence between death and rebirth.

However, Analayo's comparative materials include Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation following the second reading.

And the Chinese parallel, SA 957, describes a being travelling by means of a **mind-made body**\.

That is significant evidence against simply asserting that the source unambiguously excludes an intermediate condition.

So here's the honest conclusion.

The canonical materials connect rebirth with craving.

They do not present a single, uncontested account of the detailed mechanics across every textual witness.

Nanananda argues against intermediate existence.

Other early textual evidence complicates that conclusion.

And this should make us cautious about pretending that one difficult sentence definitively solves the mechanics of rebirth.

But the central philosophical point remains.

**Within the Buddhist account, craving is not merely a source of psychological dissatisfaction. It is implicated in the continuation of becoming itself.**

And this explains why Nibbana's happiness is much more radical than the relief of ordinary anxiety.

It claims the ending of the process that sustains suffering across lives.

That is a major religious and metaphysical claim, not something established merely by observing everyday reactions.

But it belongs to the original teaching.

And it cannot be removed without fundamentally changing its scope.


## 13\. Four answers. Every single one wrong.

Now Nanananda brings us to a famous philosophical dilemma.

What happens to the liberated Buddha after death?

Surely one of four answers must be correct.

He exists.

He does not exist.

He both exists and does not exist.

He neither exists nor does not exist.

Those are the four alternatives traditionally associated with the tetralemma.

And the Buddha declines all four.

Imagine a philosopher hearing this.

"Fine. I understand rejecting the first two."

"But surely you must accept one of the remaining alternatives."

"No."

"Then you must mean the fourth."

"No."

"Then you mean something beyond all four."

And now the philosopher is trying to make the rejection of the questionnaire into yet another answer on the questionnaire.

Extraordinary.

The metaphysical equivalent of being told that a legal form is invalid and desperately attempting to tick the box marked *None of the Above*\.

Nanananda argues that the Buddha's refusal is not simply coy mysticism.

Nor is it an admission of ignorance.

The questions presuppose something about the identity of the liberated person that the Buddha's analysis does not establish.

### The loaded question

Imagine somebody asks\:

"Have you stopped secretly stealing money from your family?"

You're forced to answer yes or no.

But suppose you've never stolen money from your family.

If you answer yes, it sounds as though you previously stole.

If you answer no, it sounds as though you're still stealing.

The question is defective.

The problem isn't that the answer is too mystical.

The problem is that the question smuggles in an assumption.

Nanananda uses precisely this sort of legal trap to explain the Buddha's refusal of the speculative alternatives.

Now apply it to the liberated person.

"Does the eternal individual survive after death?"

But what if the supposed independent individual was never established?

"Is that individual annihilated?"

Same problem.

"Does the individual both survive and not survive?"

Still assumes the relevant individual.

"Does the individual neither survive nor not survive?"

You have made the language more elaborate without examining the premise.

The old assumption remains concealed beneath increasingly sophisticated logic.

So the Buddha refuses to play.

And his actual response is directed towards something else.

**Craving has been cut off. The fetters have been severed. The conceit of independent selfhood has been understood. Suffering has ended.**

That's the answer Nanananda thinks the entire tradition has been pointing towards.

And now the question becomes unavoidable.

If the Buddha has rejected the framework used to discuss a liberated person's destiny, how should we understand the very concept of a liberated person?

That is Sermon 21.



# Sermon 21 - The liberated person who cannot be found even while standing in front of you

## 14\. The Buddha's answer is not hidden in a fifth box

Sermon 21 begins by returning to Vacchagotta.

And Nanananda makes an important clarification.

The Buddha isn't choosing the most mysterious of four possible answers.

He isn't saying\:

"Actually, I have a secret fifth position which transcends logic."

He is rejecting the assumptions from which the questions arise.

The problem is not that the liberated person has an exceptionally complicated destination.

The problem is that the questioner assumes an independently existing person whose destination must be determined.

Nanananda argues that this is why the Buddha brings the discussion back to dependent arising rather than answering on the questioner's terms.

And now he introduces a genuinely extraordinary canonical claim.

The Buddha's freedom from being identified through the aggregates is already relevant **while he is alive**\.

Think about that.

You're standing directly in front of the Buddha.

You can see his body.

Hear his voice.

Watch him walk.

See him eat.

But the Buddha says that the liberated one cannot be reckoned in terms of bodily form, feeling, perception, mental formations or consciousness as an independently existing self.

And Nanananda asks\:

If that independent person cannot be established even now, what exactly are you trying to locate after death?

### The uprooted palm tree

Imagine a palm tree.

It's standing upright.

From a distance it looks perfectly healthy.

But its roots have been severed.

The trunk remains.

The leaves remain.

The physical appearance persists.

Yet the tree no longer possesses the conditions required for continued growth.

Nanananda uses this canonical simile to explain the situation of the living arahant.

The five aggregates continue to function conventionally.

But the roots of renewed becoming have been destroyed.

Now this is philosophically subtle.

He isn't claiming that the living arahant's biological body has literally stopped depending upon conditions.

The body still needs food, water and air.

And the sensory faculties continue functioning.

The roots in the analogy concern the conditions sustaining the relevant cycle of craving and becoming.

That's what has been severed.

Now imagine a philosopher walking past the tree.

"Ah\! A fine healthy palm\!"

Well, yes, it looks like a palm.

But the conditions of its continued growth have already changed.

Likewise, the ordinary observer sees the Buddha and assumes the presence of an independently existing owner of the aggregates.

Nanananda argues that the arahant's freedom consists precisely in the ending of that identification.

**The organism remains. The bondage that sustained becoming has ceased.**

And this gives him a striking answer to the four speculative alternatives.

The questions presuppose a substantial entity whose destiny can be determined.

But the teaching has been investigating how such an entity is imagined in the first place.

So asking where it went after death may be fundamentally misconceived.

And that brings us back to the most beautiful image in Nanananda's entire series.


## 15\. The whirlpool that mistook itself for an ocean separate from the ocean

Imagine an enormous ocean.

Water moves.

Currents form.

Some currents encounter others.

Under the appropriate conditions, a whirlpool appears.

It has a recognisable shape.

A centre.

A direction.

A pattern of movement.

And you can point to it.

"There is a whirlpool."

Perfectly meaningful.

Now suppose the whirlpool somehow acquires philosophical ambitions.

It begins announcing\:

**"I AM A SEPARATE, INDEPENDENT WHIRLPOOL."**

"I must preserve my existence."

"I must acquire more water."

"I must defend my boundaries."

"I must survive the destruction of the surrounding ocean."

The poor sod has begun treating a conditioned pattern as a permanent independent entity.

And the entire project is absurd.

The whirlpool consists of the activity of water under particular conditions.

It doesn't contain an independently existing whirlpool-substance.

Now imagine the currents change.

The whirlpool subsides.

And somebody asks\:

"Where did it go?"

Did it travel north?

Did it merge into the ocean?

Did it become an eternal ocean-consciousness?

Did it discover that its true self was the Entire Ocean all along?

Nanananda argues that every one of these formulations can become misleading.

**The whirlpool was never a separate substance that needed to travel somewhere or merge with something else.**

It was a dependent pattern.

Its cessation is not the relocation of an independent being.

This is his central analogy for the relationship between the ordinary cycle of becoming and liberation.

### Why this is not secretly Vedanta

Now this is an especially important distinction.

When people hear that the whirlpool was never separate from the ocean, they may immediately conclude\:

"Ah\! So the individual soul merges into universal consciousness."

Or\:

"The apparent person discovers that they were always the infinite Self."

Or\:

"Nanananda has finally proven the metaphysics of Trika."

But that isn't what his argument establishes.

Nanananda explicitly warns that saying the vortex has *merged* with the ocean is only a manner of speaking.

The metaphor is intended to expose the absence of an independent whirlpool-entity.

It is not a demonstration that there exists an eternal conscious ocean-substance into which individual consciousness returns.

And that is where his Buddhist interpretation differs fundamentally from the universal self-revealing consciousness of Pratyabhijna.

The traditions may both challenge the apparent independence of ordinary individual identity.

But their metaphysical conclusions are not interchangeable.

**Non-separateness in an analogy does not automatically establish an eternal universal Self.**

The crucial Buddhist claim concerns dependent arising and the ending of the ignorance that sustains appropriation.

And Nanananda now makes the image even more radical.

### The whirlpool is an act of resistance

He describes the whirlpool as a disturbance arising through the interaction of currents.

Something is moving.

A contrary movement creates conflict.

The water becomes caught in a circular pattern.

And the resulting vortex acquires the appearance of an independent centre.

Nanananda compares this with the mind's attempt to secure something impossible through grasping.

The mind wants conditioned things to provide unconditional security.

It wants impermanent experiences to deliver permanent satisfaction.

It wants changing processes to establish an unchanging owner.

The project cannot succeed on its own terms.

And yet the effort continues.

**The struggle to obtain something impossible becomes the very mechanism that sustains the struggle.**

Now apply that to the search for happiness.

You experience something pleasurable.

It passes.

You want it again.

The desire creates dissatisfaction.

You obtain another pleasurable experience.

It passes.

You become afraid of losing the next one.

More desire.

More striving.

More becoming.

The mind is whirling.

And the extraordinary thing is that it believes the solution lies in whirling more successfully.

"Perhaps I haven't achieved enough."

"Perhaps I need a more extraordinary experience."

"Perhaps the next state of consciousness will finally solve the problem."

But Nanananda's argument suggests something else.

**Perhaps the freedom you're seeking isn't a superior movement of the vortex. It's the cessation of the vortex itself.**

And that brings us directly to the chapter's question.

Why should anyone want Nibbana?

Because the ending of the compulsive struggle is not the loss of a treasure you were successfully accumulating.

It's freedom from the mechanism that continually made you feel something was missing.

The vortex stops.

The impossible project is abandoned.

The conflict ceases.

And that is why Nanananda finds the comparison with the great ocean so illuminating.

But he now introduces a further complication.


## 16\. The ocean doesn't get more enlightened every time a monk dies

The Buddha compares the Dhamma to the great ocean.

Among the ocean's remarkable qualities is that enormous rivers can flow into it without producing an obvious increase in its level.

Similarly, the scriptures say that even when many monks attain final Nibbana, there is no corresponding increase or decrease in the Nibbana element.

Now imagine somebody interpreting this literally.

"So Nibbana must be an enormous container."

"And all the liberated beings enter it."

"But the container doesn't fill up."

"Therefore Nibbana is an infinite-capacity warehouse for enlightened souls."

Wonderful.

The cosmic storage facility has been discovered.

Nanananda thinks this is an enormous mistake.

The ocean analogy isn't intended to establish a metaphysical storage space.

He relates it instead to the cessation of the vortex.

When the whirlpool ceases, the ocean hasn't acquired an additional independent substance.

The vortex didn't arrive as a separate object.

Its characteristic movement ended.

Similarly, Nanananda argues, the cessation of craving and becoming doesn't entail the relocation of an independent liberated entity into a cosmic repository.

And here's where Analayo's comparative material becomes particularly important.

The ocean comparison appears in the Pali *Paharada Sutta* and other textual witnesses.

But the corresponding fifth quality is **absent from the Chinese Madhyama-agama parallel, MA 35**\.

That doesn't prove the Pali version is late or false.

But it does mean we should be cautious about treating every detail of its analogy as the unquestionable foundation of a complete metaphysical system.

Nanananda's interpretation remains philosophically interesting.

But the simile is not a proof of an eternal ocean, a universal consciousness or a literal place of postmortem residence.

The deeper issue is the cessation of a dependently sustained process.

And this brings us to something profoundly uncomfortable.

**If the whirlpool was never an independent entity, how far can we meaningfully speak of the person who has attained liberation?**

Nanananda's answer is going to unsettle even his own listeners.


## 17\. The monk who tried to save the Tathagata from non-existence

Now we meet the monk Anuradha.

He's living in solitude.

Some wandering ascetics approach.

And they ask about the Buddha's state after death.

They insist upon the familiar four alternatives.

He exists.

He doesn't exist.

Both.

Neither.

Anuradha knows the Buddha has rejected those categories.

So he tells them the liberated one must be described outside the four alternatives.

Which sounds very sophisticated.

Very nondual.

Very philosophically advanced.

Unfortunately, it's still wrong.

The wandering ascetics call him incompetent and leave.

Anuradha is worried.

Has he misrepresented the Buddha?

So he approaches his teacher.

And the Buddha proceeds to dismantle the assumptions underlying the entire question.

This is the *Anuradha Sutta*, SN 22.86, which forms a central part of Sermon 21.

### The interrogation

The Buddha begins with the five aggregates.

Body.

Feeling.

Perception.

Mental formations.

Consciousness.

Are these permanent?

No.

Are they subject to change?

Yes.

Can they appropriately be regarded as an independently existing self?

No.

Then the Buddha asks another question.

Is the Tathagata identical to any one of these aggregates?

No.

Is he located inside them?

No.

Is he an independent entity separate from them?

No.

Can he be established as somebody who exists without them?

No.

And then comes the devastating conclusion.

If an independently existing Tathagata cannot be established here and now, how can Anuradha legitimately claim that such a being must be described by some mysterious fifth alternative after death?

**The problem isn't that the liberated individual has escaped the four categories. The problem is that the independent individual presupposed by the question cannot be established.**

Now that's enormously important.

Anuradha was trying to defend the Buddha from the apparently annihilationist implications of denying survival.

But he did so by postulating some special manner of existence beyond the available categories.

He hadn't fully questioned the assumed entity.

He had simply placed it beyond description.

The self has gone into the metaphysical witness-protection programme.

And the Buddha isn't satisfied.

### The philosophical difference

There is a crucial distinction between saying\:

*The Buddha exists in a manner too mysterious for language.*

And saying\:

*The assumption of an independently existing Buddha is not established by analysis of the aggregates.*

The first can preserve a hidden metaphysical entity.

The second questions the entity itself.

Nanananda strongly favours the second.

But his interpretation is radical, and we need to distinguish it from the ordinary conventional reality of persons.

The historical Buddha was a living human being.

He taught.

Walked.

Ate.

Spoke.

Had relationships with disciples.

The analysis does not deny those conventional facts.

It denies that they establish an independent, permanent self existing over and above the dependently arisen processes.

**The Buddha is conventionally identifiable without being metaphysically reducible to an eternal owner of the aggregates.**

That's the distinction.

And Nanananda makes it explicit when discussing the problem of identifying the Tathagata even during life.

But here's the real payoff.

If the independent owner cannot be established, then the question of that owner's destiny after death is not solved by inventing an eternal substance.

Nor by insisting that the substance is annihilated.

It is solved by understanding the processes through which the supposed owner is imagined and grasped.

And the Buddha's actual teaching returns to a much more precise problem.

Suffering.

And its ending.

Not the eternal fate of a metaphysical object.

Now Nanananda gives us one final analogy.

The most beautiful and perhaps most devastating of all.


## 18\. The children who destroyed their sandcastles without becoming miserable

Imagine some children playing on a beach.

They're constructing sandcastles.

Beautiful little towers.

Moats.

Walls.

Roads.

Perhaps entire kingdoms.

They become completely absorbed.

"This one is mine."

"That one is yours."

"My castle is bigger."

"You can't touch my castle."

A tiny empire of ownership has emerged.

And the emotional stakes are enormous.

Somebody accidentally knocks over a tower.

Catastrophe.

A child cries.

Another becomes angry.

An argument begins.

Diplomatic relations between the Kingdom of the Left Sandcastle and the Empire of the Right Sandcastle have completely collapsed.

And all of this is happening around arrangements of sand.

Now here's the important point.

The castles are real enough as structures of sand.

The children have genuinely arranged the material.

They can recognise different towers.

They can enjoy the activity.

But their emotional investment has made those structures into possessions whose continued existence matters enormously.

Then something changes.

The children grow tired of the game.

Their interest fades.

They no longer care about preserving the castles.

And now they begin knocking them down.

Not out of despair.

Not because life has become meaningless.

Not because somebody has forced them to abandon the precious objects.

They simply no longer experience the castles as things that must be possessed.

They scatter the sand.

And go on with their lives.

This is the Buddha's simile in the *Radha Samyutta*, which Nanananda uses as the climax of Sermon 21.

Now listen to the mechanism.

At first, the castles were precious.

The children defended them.

Their pleasure depended upon their preservation.

But eventually the attachment ceased.

And the destruction of the castles was no longer experienced as a catastrophe.

The material didn't have to become metaphysically nonexistent.

The children didn't need to transform themselves into eternal observers of the sand.

**The relationship of ownership had changed.**

And Nanananda argues that this points towards the way wisdom brings an end to grasping at the aggregates.

The body.

Feeling.

Perception.

Mental formations.

Consciousness.

These are the processes around which the mind has constructed its identity.

The mind says\:

"This is me."

"This is mine."

"This must continue."

"This must not change."

And an entire cycle of becoming is organised around protecting the arrangement.

But when the true nature of the processes is understood, the demand to possess them can cease.

The castle was never an eternal kingdom.

And the child doesn't need an eternal kingdom to be free.

### Why the Buddha says a being is someone caught

Nanananda draws attention to another teaching given to Radha.

The Buddha explains that what makes someone a *being*, in the relevant sense, is their attachment to the aggregates.

The living process becomes a being *caught up* in what it desires and identifies with.

This is not an ordinary dictionary definition of a biological organism.

It is a soteriological reinterpretation of what it means to be bound.

**The being is characterised by being caught.**

And liberation is the ending of that attachment.

The extraordinary significance is that one need not discover and destroy an independently existing soul.

One must understand the conditions through which grasping sustains becoming.

Now think how this transforms the idea of spiritual practice.

Ordinarily, you want to become a better person.

A more peaceful person.

A more knowledgeable person.

An extraordinarily spiritual person.

Perhaps eventually an enlightened person.

But the path doesn't end by producing the most magnificent identity imaginable.

It ends by uprooting the compulsion to identify.

The sandcastle doesn't need to become infinitely large.

The child doesn't need to build the ultimate castle.

The game itself can end.

And this is where Nanananda closes the sermon.

The Buddha's answer to Radha identifies Nibbana with **the destruction of craving**\.

Not the acquisition of an eternal consciousness.

Not the permanent possession of an infinitely blissful feeling.

Not the discovery of a metaphysical residence beyond death.

The ending of craving.

And we have finally returned to the beginning of the chapter.


# The final cut - The supreme happiness is not something you get

Let's gather these three sermons into one argument.

**Sermon 19 asks whether liberation must wait until death to be complete.**

Nanananda says no.

The arahant has already realised the ending of the defilements during life.

The body can continue.

The senses can operate.

Pleasant and painful feelings can occur.

But the underlying conditions sustaining renewed bondage have been eradicated.

Death does not deliver the missing portion of enlightenment.

The liberation is already complete.

And that creates the extraordinary possibility of happiness not dependent upon the possession of pleasant feeling.

**Sermon 20 asks what makes Nibbana the supreme happiness.**

Nanananda identifies it with unshakeable freedom.

The ending of the karmic debt.

Freedom from the necessity of perpetuating the cycle of becoming.

And the cessation of craving itself.

The value of the achievement is not measured by the intensity of a pleasurable sensation.

It lies in the ending of the compulsive dependence through which sensations become the conditions of bondage.

Then he turns to death.

And shows that the Buddhist account does not need an eternal soul travelling from one life to another.

Craving is the supporting condition.

Without it, the process of renewed becoming ceases.

And the fate of a supposedly independent liberated self cannot be determined through the ordinary alternatives of eternal survival and annihilation.

**Sermon 21 asks whether the liberated person can be established as an independent entity even during life.**

Nanananda says no.

The aggregates continue conventionally.

But they do not establish a permanent owner.

The whirlpool can appear as a distinctive feature of the ocean without possessing an independent essence.

And when the whirlpool ceases, no separate substance needs to migrate somewhere else.

The children can play with sandcastles.

Then understand that the castles need not be defended as existential possessions.

The game can end.

And the activity of ownership no longer has to continue.

Now here's the extraordinary conclusion.

**Nibbana is not the ultimate success of the project of becoming happy. It is the ending of the compulsion through which the project continually reproduces unhappiness.**

Think about how radical that is.

Ordinarily, the mind wants something.

It imagines the possession.

It constructs a future.

It anticipates the pleasure.

It becomes dissatisfied with its present condition.

It pursues the object.

It obtains it.

And then the object changes.

Or disappears.

Or fails to provide the unconditional security the mind expected.

So the mind starts again.

A new object.

A new project.

A new identity.

A new imagined completion.

The entire machinery is organised around the promise that something not yet possessed will finally make the owner complete.

But Nanananda argues that the apparent owner is itself a product of the processes being investigated.

And the promise of completion can never be fulfilled through acquiring another conditioned possession.

The problem isn't that you've failed to find a sufficiently magnificent object.

**The problem is the assumption that possession can provide the freedom you're looking for.**

And this is why the contemplation of Nibbana can be so powerful.

It turns attention away from the compulsive search for another object and towards understanding the process of grasping itself.

The more deeply the process is understood, the less compelling its demands need become.

Not because a superior self has finally mastered the inferior self.

Not because you've discovered an eternal substance that survives the destruction of the body.

Not because you've entered the ultimate pleasure dimension.

But because the causes of bondage are being understood and, ultimately, uprooted.

Now we can answer the chapter's opening question.

**If craving ends, does happiness disappear too?**

No-within the Buddhist account, what ceases is the bondage that makes happiness depend upon craving.

And Nanananda's most radical claim is that the complete cessation of those conditions deserves to be called supreme happiness even though it cannot be reduced to a pleasant feeling.

There is a profound difference between\:

*I am happy because this experience is happening.*

And\:

*My freedom does not depend upon securing any particular experience.*

The first can be wonderful.

But it is conditional.

The second is what Nanananda wants us to recognise as the deeper possibility of Nibbana.

And now the final irony.

You began the path wanting Nibbana.

Naturally.

It sounded like something extraordinary.

The ultimate happiness.

The supreme peace.

The end of suffering.

You imagined acquiring it.

Then you investigated craving.

You discovered how the mind constructs possessions.

You discovered how those possessions become identities.

You discovered how those identities generate further demands.

You discovered how the entire cycle continues through ignorance and grasping.

And now you understand something extraordinary.

**The liberation you were seeking is the ending of the very activity through which you were trying to possess liberation.**

The donkey stops chasing the carrot.

The debtor no longer owes.

The fire stops receiving fuel.

The whirlpool subsides.

The children leave their sandcastles behind.

And nobody needs to construct another identity around having become free.

That's the extraordinary simplicity.

But we've reached a final philosophical danger.

If the liberated person cannot be established as an independently existing entity even during life, and the conditions for renewed becoming have ended, what exactly does it mean to ask whether they exist after death?

Is the Buddha's refusal to answer an admission of ignorance?

Is it a deliberate rejection of the question?

Is it possible that the categories through which we've been attempting to understand death are simply incapable of describing what cessation means?

And what does all of this imply for the ordinary person who is terrified of dying?

**What actually happens to someone who is liberated and dies-and why does the Buddha refuse to tell us in the way we expect?**

That is Chapter VIII.

And now we're going to take the metaphysics of death apart without allowing either the eternal soul or the annihilationist's permanent nothingness to sneak through the back door.


**End of Chapter VII - Sermons 19-21 of 33.**
