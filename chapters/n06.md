# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter VI of X · Sermons 16-18 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, examined alongside Bhikkhu Analayo

## Can experience continue without someone owning it?

**The answer: Yes. And the extraordinary claim of these three sermons is that liberation can be complete while the body is still alive, the senses still function, and the world continues appearing.**

The liberated person can see a beautiful woman without needing to possess her.

Taste delicious food without becoming enslaved to its repetition.

Feel bodily pain without constructing an existential catastrophe around it.

Think without treating every thought as the pronouncement of an independent thinker.

And even use the word *I* without believing that an eternal metaphysical entity has been discovered behind the grammar.

But Nanananda is about to make a considerably more radical claim.

He argues that there is a distinctive realisation of cessation in which the ordinary perceptual structures of the world no longer find their footing.

A mode of concentration that cannot be adequately explained by ordinary sensory perception, the four absorptions, or even the exalted formless attainments.

And - most importantly - one that doesn't establish another permanent conscious self.

That creates two questions we must keep separate\:

**Can an arahant live and function without grasping?** Yes, according to the early Buddhist discourses.

**What exactly is the extraordinary concentration through which Nanananda describes the realisation of Nibbana?** That is much more controversial.

If we confuse the two, we'll either imagine enlightenment is a permanent sensory blackout or turn Nibbana into an everlasting experience of cosmic awareness.

Both would be fairly spectacular misunderstandings.

So let's follow Nanananda precisely.


# Sermon 16 - The meditation that doesn't meditate on anything

## 1\. You aren't suffering merely because things happen. You're suffering because you have invested yourself in them.

Chapter V ended with Bahiya's devastating instruction: seeing need not construct an independent seer; hearing need not produce an owner of hearing.

But an obvious objection remains.

Suppose somebody insults an arahant.

The ears still work.

The sound still arrives.

The meaning is understood.

Perhaps the words are profoundly offensive.

So why doesn't the arahant become psychologically entangled in the encounter?

Nanananda opens Sermon 16 by investigating exactly this distinction.

He returns to the dependence of ordinary experience upon contact and the manner in which the mind divides experience into a subject who grasps and objects that can be grasped.

He then introduces a discourse from the *Udana* concerning monks who are being insulted by rival ascetics.

The rival ascetics have become jealous.

The Buddha's community is attracting respect and support.

Their own prestige is suffering.

So naturally, like mature spiritual practitioners who have transcended worldly attachments, they begin abusing the Buddhist monks.

A magnificent display of religious attainment.

The monks report the situation to the Buddha.

And his response contains an extraordinary principle.

Pleasant and painful contacts become grounds for suffering through the supports of clinging.

When those supports have been relinquished, the same kind of bondage cannot occur.

Now here's the important distinction.

Nanananda is not saying that sound waves physically fail to reach the arahant's ears.

Nor that other people cease behaving cruelly.

He's investigating the process through which contact becomes psychologically appropriated.

**An event may affect an organism without becoming a possession around which an independent self must be defended.**

Think about the difference between these two things\:

Someone says something unpleasant.

And\:

*Someone has insulted ME, threatened MY identity, diminished MY existence, and now I must restore my rightful position in the universe.*

The first is an encounter.

The second is a blimming constitutional crisis.

And the second depends upon something additional: the significance which the mind has constructed around ownership.

### The insult that needs an owner

Imagine you've written an essay.

Someone criticises it.

Perhaps the criticism is accurate.

Perhaps it's completely stupid.

Either way, you recognise the words.

Now two things can happen.

You can investigate the criticism and decide whether anything needs changing.

Or you can experience the criticism as an attack upon the person who wrote the essay.

Suddenly the issue is no longer whether an argument is valid.

It is whether **you are valid**\.

And everything becomes more difficult.

Your heart races.

You begin defending positions you might otherwise have reconsidered.

You become hostile.

You imagine what the critic must think of you.

A perfectly ordinary disagreement has become an existential emergency.

Why?

Because the argument has become one of your possessions.

And through possessing it, you've become possessed by it.

Nanananda's thesis is that the liberated mind has ceased this kind of grasping at the most fundamental level.

But we should be exact.

Ordinary psychological equanimity is not identical to arahantship. A person can appear calm while suppressing intense resentment. An arahant, as understood in the tradition, has uprooted greed, hatred and delusion.

So we are discussing **freedom from the underlying cause**, not a permanent performance of composure.

And that brings us to Nanananda's next startling claim.

## 2\. The spiritual bank account you didn't realise you were maintaining

Nanananda calls the accumulated supports of identification *assets*\.

Not just money.

Not just land.

Not the valuables sitting inside your house.

The deeper assets are the things upon which your very identity has become dependent.

Your body.

Your memories.

Your accomplishments.

Your relationships.

Your knowledge.

Your status.

Your hopes for the future.

And even your image of yourself as somebody spiritually advanced.

All these can become psychological holdings.

And the mind maintains an extraordinary bookkeeping operation.

**Everything must be entered into the account of ME.**

A compliment comes in.

Credit.

An insult comes in.

Debit.

Someone admires you.

Your personal worth apparently appreciates.

Someone rejects you.

The market crashes.

You enter a profound meditation state.

Extraordinary spiritual asset acquired.

The state disappears.

A catastrophic loss has been reported to the Department of Enlightenment.

The whole blimming enterprise depends upon treating changing experiences as possessions of a continuing owner.

Nanananda argues that Nibbana involves the relinquishment of this entire structure of accumulation.

But here's where his argument becomes much better than a recommendation to give everything away.

He asks **how genuine relinquishment actually occurs**\.

And the answer is wisdom.

Not simply forcing yourself to surrender things.

Understanding their nature so completely that the compulsion to possess them loses its basis.

### The millionaire whose money became wallpaper

Imagine somebody with an enormous safe.

Inside are stacks of banknotes.

His entire life revolves around them.

He counts them.

Protects them.

Thinks about them.

Worries about theft.

Imagines all the extraordinary things he'll eventually purchase.

The notes aren't simply pieces of paper.

They represent power, security, possibility and identity.

Then one morning, the government announces that this particular currency has been completely invalidated.

The notes are worthless.

And suddenly something remarkable happens.

The man doesn't need to practise an elaborate meditation on renouncing each individual banknote.

He understands.

The value he had attributed to them is gone.

What previously seemed like an extraordinary treasure is now a collection of printed paper.

**The possession loses its psychological hold because the meaning supporting that hold has collapsed.**

Nanananda uses precisely this example to illustrate relinquishment through understanding.

Now the analogy isn't perfect.

A worthless currency can still be mourned.

An individual may remain deeply distressed about their ruined finances.

The illustration concerns the sudden loss of a previously accepted value, not a guarantee that human attachment disappears automatically whenever circumstances change.

But it makes one point beautifully.

**Understanding can release a grip that force alone struggles to loosen.**

And Nanananda gives an even better example.

## 3\. The man who nearly missed his train because of a dream

A man has been transferred to a new posting.

He needs to leave immediately.

His transport is arriving.

But he has all these possessions.

Furniture.

Clothes.

Boxes.

Valuables.

He is running around frantically trying to gather everything.

The vehicle is sounding its horn.

Time is running out.

He becomes desperate.

What can he carry?

What must he leave?

How can he possibly manage this catastrophe?

Then-

He wakes up.

It was a dream.

There is no transfer.

No waiting vehicle.

No urgent departure.

No possessions that must be gathered.

And the extraordinary feature is that he doesn't have to conduct a separate renunciation ceremony for every item in the dream.

He doesn't sit on the edge of the bed solemnly announcing\:

"I hereby relinquish the dream refrigerator."

"I relinquish the imaginary suitcase."

"I relinquish the metaphysical rights to the dream television."

He has understood that the entire situation was misconceived.

And the demands generated within it have lost their authority.

This is Nanananda's authentic dream analogy, which he places beside the banknotes and cinema in Sermon 16.

Now listen carefully.

He's not saying ordinary waking experience is physically identical to dreaming.

He isn't proving that external objects cease to exist when nobody thinks about them.

His point concerns the **delusive valuation and appropriation** of experience.

In a dream, something can become desperately important.

And the moment its status is understood, the entire network of urgency can collapse.

Likewise, insight into conditioned experience can undermine the assumptions through which the mind continually constructs possessions, identities and existential demands.

This explains why Nibbana isn't primarily an exercise in making yourself give things up.

It is the culmination of understanding that removes the basis of grasping.

### The difference between a temporary revelation and awakening

But here's the objection.

You can wake from a dream tonight and have another dream tomorrow.

You can understand that one particular possession is worthless and immediately become obsessed with another.

You can glimpse the constructed nature of your identity and, fifteen minutes later, become furious because someone has misunderstood you.

So what distinguishes full liberation from these temporary moments?

Nanananda's answer is decisive.

**In arahantship, the underlying defilements responsible for renewed grasping have been uprooted.**

It isn't merely that the present object has lost its attraction.

The fundamental conditions supporting the bondage have ceased.

The cinema lights don't just come on for five seconds before the audience becomes absorbed again.

The ignorance that made the whole performance compelling has been extinguished.

That's a much stronger claim.

And it immediately creates a problem.

If liberation is no longer constructed through grasping, what exactly does a liberated mind attend to?

Can it be concentrated without depending upon an ordinary object?

Now we reach the remarkable centrepiece of Sermon 16.


## 4\. The meditator who doesn't depend on earth, space, infinity-or anything else

Now consider how most meditation works.

You place attention upon something.

The breath.

A bodily sensation.

A visual object.

A mental image.

Perhaps an increasingly subtle experience of spaciousness.

And through sustained attention, the mind becomes collected.

That makes sense.

There's an object.

There's attention directed towards it.

Concentration develops through the relationship.

But in the *Sandha Sutta*, AN 11.9, the Buddha describes something astonishing.

A meditator does not meditate in dependence upon the four material elements.

Nor upon infinite space.

Nor upon infinite consciousness.

Nor upon nothingness.

Nor upon the state of neither-perception-nor-non-perception.

Nor upon this world or another world.

Nor upon the ordinary range of things seen, heard, sensed, known, attained or investigated.

**And yet the person meditates.**

Even the gods are bewildered. They cannot determine what this extraordinary meditator is relying upon.

Nanananda reads the passage as evidence for a special form of concentration associated with the liberation of an arahant. Analayo's comparative materials supply Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation and the corresponding Chinese *Samyukta-agama* passage, SA 926, which preserves the central paradox.

Now imagine the gods approaching the meditator.

"Excuse me, sir. Wonderful concentration. What is your meditation object?"

"Not earth."

"Right. Water?"

"No."

"Space?"

"No."

"Infinite consciousness?"

"No."

"Nothingness?"

"No."

"Well, WHAT THE HECK ARE YOU MEDITATING ON?"

And that's precisely the problem.

The gods assume that concentration must have a recognisable object corresponding to one of the familiar categories.

But the passage describes concentration which cannot be accounted for through those dependencies.

This is not simply someone who has stopped thinking.

Nor somebody sitting unconscious.

The text emphatically maintains that meditation occurs.

**What has been negated is the familiar dependence upon an object-not the occurrence of concentration itself.**

But now we must be careful.

Nanananda's identification of the passage with the fruition attainment of arahantship is an interpretation. The discourse establishes an extraordinary form of meditation without the usual dependencies; precisely how it relates to all the recognised stages of awakening requires further argument.

The Chinese parallel supports the unusual description, but doesn't by itself settle that identification.

And we also shouldn't turn the passage into a doctrine of an eternal consciousness that meditates without an object.

That would introduce a metaphysical subject which the discourse hasn't independently established.

The question is more precise.

Can the mind become liberated from the structures through which ordinary objects become supports for its activity?

Nanananda believes the answer is yes.

### The meditation that doesn't need an identity

Consider an ordinary meditator enjoying an experience of vastness.

The sense of bodily location becomes attenuated.

There is an impression of infinite space.

And the mind announces\:

"I am experiencing boundless space."

Then perhaps\:

"I am the boundless awareness in which everything appears."

Notice what's happened.

A refined meditative experience has become the foundation of an extraordinary metaphysical identity.

The experience may genuinely have been profound.

But the interpretation is additional.

And the conceit of ownership has survived.

Nanananda's interest is in a concentration which no longer depends upon constructing such an object or identity.

Even the exalted formless states cannot be treated as the ultimate destination.

**If you're still trying to become the owner of infinity, you're still playing the same game-just with a considerably more ambitious property portfolio.**

But this gives rise to a genuinely difficult question.

How can attention exist without attending to anything familiar?

Nanananda finds the next clue in a conversation involving Ananda.

## 5\. How can someone perceive without perceiving the usual objects?

Ananda asks the Buddha whether there can be concentration in which the meditator does not perceive the familiar material elements, formless states, this world or another world.

Yet perception remains.

The Buddha says there can.

Then, in a related discourse, Ananda asks whether attention can occur without being directed towards any of those familiar objects.

Again, the answer is yes.

And what explains this extraordinary attention?

**The mind attends to the peace of cessation-to the ending of craving and the relinquishment of everything previously grasped.**

Here is the exact connection with the opening insight of our series.

You set the mind towards Nibbana because the object of contemplation is the ending of the very processes that bind the mind.

It's not an attempt to acquire an increasingly impressive state.

It's an orientation towards release.

Nanananda brings together these discourses to argue that perception, attention and concentration can operate in an extraordinary manner associated with realising cessation. Analayo's accompanying notes compare the relevant renderings with Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations.

Now listen to the philosophical distinction.

Ordinarily, attention selects an object.

The object becomes important.

And importance can become the basis for craving.

The mind is organised around gaining, retaining or rejecting something.

But contemplation of cessation has a radically different direction.

It invites the mind to understand the ending of that very movement.

**The practice takes an activity which normally helps sustain acquisition and employs it to understand relinquishment.**

That's the apparent paradox.

And it isn't solved by inventing a mysterious object called Nothingness.

If you merely replace the breath with the thought of a cosmic void, you may have acquired another meditation object.

And if you identify with the void, you've simply given the old self an enormous empty palace.

Nanananda is arguing for something subtler.

The contemplation culminates in a realisation where the activities of grasping and fabrication have ceased.

But what about the difference between contemplating release and having actually attained it?

## 6\. The moment the examination results arrive

Nanananda distinguishes several stages of knowledge.

There's the determination to know what remains unknown.

There's knowledge penetrating the ending of the defilements.

And there's the certainty that liberation is complete.

He draws upon verses in the *Itivuttaka* and the *Ratana Sutta*, describing the exceptional concentration associated with the culmination of the path.

Then comes one of his memorable comparisons.

Imagine taking an examination.

Normally, you finish the exam.

Leave the hall.

Spend several weeks worrying about your answers.

Wait for the results.

Perhaps convince yourself you've failed.

Perhaps construct an entire identity around the possible failure.

A very productive period of unnecessary suffering.

But Nanananda compares the completion of the liberating path to an examination whose results are immediately known.

The knowledge of liberation is not an uncertain prediction that something wonderful might happen in the future.

It carries certainty about the ending of the defilements.

Nanananda connects this with the fruit of arahantship and the knowledge that one's release is unshakeable.

This is why the teaching is stronger than a method for temporarily reducing mental disturbance.

You can have a peaceful meditation.

Then become furious tomorrow.

You can experience spacious awareness.

Then become terrified when that experience disappears.

Those states don't establish complete liberation.

The traditional claim about arahantship is that the relevant defilements have been eradicated.

Their temporary absence is not mistaken for their complete destruction.

And that brings us to an exceptionally important distinction.

**A meditator may contemplate Nibbana while still training. An arahant's fruition attainment is described as a realisation of liberation already accomplished.**

Nanananda insists that we shouldn't imagine the arahant merely sitting and repeating a formula about peace.

The attainment is not simply an impressive performance of doctrinal recollection.

It is the realisation to which that recollection points.

The distinction is between describing release and being released.

A map of the exit is not the exit.

And memorising the map doesn't mean you've left the building.

### The empty gaze that isn't unconsciousness

Nanananda now makes a daring interpretation.

He returns to the difficult verse from the *Kalahavivada Sutta* examined earlier in the series.

It describes a condition which cannot be satisfactorily identified with ordinary perception, abnormal perception, unconsciousness or simply the destruction of perception.

Nanananda argues that the paradox points towards the extraordinary fruition concentration of an arahant.

He illustrates this with the cinema.

The film is running.

The audience is absorbed.

Then the lights come on.

The spectator looks towards the screen.

Yet the film no longer functions as the compelling drama it previously was.

The person hasn't become unconscious.

But neither are they experiencing the movie in the same way.

Nanananda uses this to illuminate the unusual relationship between perception and its apparent objects in the realisation of cessation.

Now this is a fascinating reading.

But it is also precisely where we need Analayo's scholarly restraint.

His lecture notes include an alternative analysis by Muni Bhikkhu which questions Nanananda's interpretation of the *Kalahavivada* verse as referring to final Nibbana. On that reading, the passage concerns an exceptionally subtle meditative perception that still needs to be transcended.

The accompanying Chinese evidence also suggests distinctions between ordinary perception, formless perception and other unusual perceptual conditions.

That is a substantial disagreement.

The same cryptic passage can be read as describing the culmination of liberation or a subtle state that is not yet liberation.

We shouldn't pretend the issue is settled merely because Nanananda's interpretation is philosophically elegant.

And we definitely shouldn't conclude that the passage proves the existence of an eternal witness behind perception.

The point worth preserving is more modest and defensible\:

**Liberating understanding cannot be identified merely by the presence of an extraordinary perceptual state.**

What matters is whether ignorance and grasping have ceased.

Even a state which seems to transcend ordinary perception is not necessarily final release.

And now Nanananda gives us a wonderfully uncompromising image of what freedom looks like.

## 7\. Mara has lost your forwarding address

The Buddha describes liberated beings as difficult to trace, like birds moving through the open sky.

A bird flies.

Movement occurs.

But the bird doesn't leave a permanent road behind it.

There isn't a great highway hanging in the atmosphere.

No luminous road signs.

No conveniently marked route for Mara to follow.

Nanananda interprets this as an image of the arahant's freedom from accumulated supports of becoming.

Mara, the personification of bondage and death, cannot track the liberated person through the usual structures of craving and appropriation.

Now here's the funny part.

Mara is desperately searching.

"Where's the attachment?"

"Where's the identity?"

"What does this monk want?"

"Which possession can I threaten?"

"Which spiritual attainment can I make him proud of?"

And the entire psychological intelligence service comes back with nothing useful.

The usual vulnerabilities have been uprooted.

But don't turn this into a supernatural claim that awakened people become physically untraceable.

Nanananda's argument concerns the absence of the grasping through which becoming is sustained.

The arahant's body remains conventionally identifiable.

Their movements can be observed.

They still eat, walk and speak.

And this is what makes the analogy so powerful.

**Freedom is not necessarily the disappearance of activity. It is the disappearance of the bondage that previously determined its existential significance.**

But we have now reached a very serious problem.

Nanananda has described a special concentration involving the cessation of ordinary perceptual supports.

Yet he also insists that the arahant remains alive and can function in the world.

So what happens when the arahant opens their eyes?

Does the ordinary world somehow reappear?

Does Nibbana temporarily disappear?

And if the world returns, hasn't the person fallen back into bondage?

That's the problem Sermon 17 now takes up.


# Sermon 17 - The extraordinary state where the world stops finding a footing

## 8\. The meditator who escaped the universe and accidentally built another one

Imagine somebody entering profound meditation.

Ordinary bodily perception fades.

The sense of a bounded physical world disappears.

An apparently infinite expanse of space opens.

Incredible.

Then they go deeper.

Even space is transcended.

An experience of boundless consciousness appears.

More extraordinary still.

Then nothingness.

Then a state so subtle that ordinary categories of perception scarcely seem adequate.

And the meditator announces\:

**"AT LAST. I HAVE FOUND THE ULTIMATE."**

Unfortunately, Nanananda has a rather inconvenient question.

Are these experiences still conditioned?

If so, why would their extraordinary subtlety establish that they are Nibbana?

This is how Sermon 17 begins.

Nanananda quotes an *Itivuttaka* passage contrasting beings who remain established in realms of form and formlessness with those who understand cessation and are liberated.

His argument is that one does not escape the machinery of becoming simply by exchanging a material object for an immaterial one.

Think about what the mind normally wants.

Something better.

Something purer.

Something more stable.

Something more encompassing.

Ordinary pleasure is disappointing.

So it seeks refined pleasure.

Refined pleasure is insufficient.

So it seeks tranquillity.

Tranquillity ends.

So it seeks something apparently limitless.

And when boundless consciousness appears, the mind may begin treating it as the final answer.

But the original strategy remains intact.

**Find a sufficiently magnificent state and make it the foundation of my security.**

The mind has upgraded from a suburban house to an infinite cosmic mansion.

But the mortgage is still craving.

### The textual problem that changes the meaning

Nanananda identifies a significant variant in the *Itivuttaka* verses.

One reading describes those who understand form and **do not settle** in formless states.

Another reading suggests they are **well established** there.

That is not a trivial difference.

One supports Nanananda's interpretation that liberation requires going beyond dependence upon both form and formlessness.

The other creates a different relationship between understanding form and abiding in a formless state.

Nanananda prefers the reading that denies settling in the formless. His choice is consistent with his argument, but the existence of the variant is an important qualification. Analayo's study materials preserve the alternative translation by John D. Ireland, allowing the disagreement to be examined rather than hidden.

Now no Pali deconstruction is needed to understand why this matters.

It concerns whether the ultimate goal is another exalted state of existence.

Or freedom from dependence upon such states altogether.

Nanananda's position is clear.

**You don't escape conditioned existence merely by discovering its most refined department.**

And this leads him back to the most radical definition of Nibbana.

The cessation of becoming.

## 9\. Sariputta watches a fire and sees something nobody else sees

Ananda approaches Sariputta with a question about extraordinary concentration.

Can somebody be in a state where the ordinary material and immaterial objects are not being perceived, and yet perception occurs?

Sariputta says yes.

In fact, he has experienced such concentration himself.

Then Ananda asks what the perception was.

Sariputta's answer concerns the cessation of becoming as Nibbana.

And he introduces a simple image.

A small fire made of twigs.

One flame arises.

Another disappears.

Another arises.

Another disappears.

The ordinary spectator sees a continuously burning fire.

Sariputta's explanation draws attention to the successive arising and ceasing of the flames.

Nanananda takes this as an illustration of insight into cessation.

Now imagine watching a candle.

You see a flame.

It looks like one continuous thing.

But the burning depends upon an ongoing process.

Fuel is consumed.

Heat is generated.

The process renews itself.

And you conventionally describe the whole thing as *a flame*\.

Useful.

But you can also recognise that the apparent continuity is sustained through successive conditioned events.

The name remains stable while the process changes.

Nanananda suggests that ordinary perception habitually emphasises the appearing, the continuing, the apparent presence of things.

Insight can understand their ceasing.

And this is a profound reversal of attention.

Ordinarily, the mind sees something pleasant arise and immediately begins thinking about how to retain it.

But insight recognises its conditioned character.

The arising is inseparable from the possibility of cessation.

And because the process is conditioned, its cessation can be understood.

### Why this isn't merely repeating a spiritual slogan

Sariputta's account involves an expressed perception concerning the cessation of becoming.

You might imagine him sitting there endlessly repeating\:

"Becoming ceases."

"Becoming ceases."

"Becoming ceases."

Until the words become sufficiently hypnotic.

But Nanananda rejects that reduction.

He argues that the repeated expression points towards an insight into the ceasing aspect of conditioned phenomena.

Just as the fire can be perceived as one apparently continuous flame or understood as a succession of arising and ceasing, so becoming can be examined through its conditions rather than merely accepted as a solid reality.

The contemplation isn't supposed to work because you've repeated a sentence enough times.

It's supposed to work because the sentence directs attention towards something that can be genuinely understood.

**The words point towards cessation. The liberating significance lies in understanding cessation.**

And that brings us to a question the mind finds almost impossible to answer.

If cessation is Nibbana, what actually ceases?

Does the physical world disappear?

Does perception cease?

Does the body become unconscious?

Or is the teaching describing something which cannot be captured by the usual opposition between experiencing and not experiencing?

Nanananda now turns to an extraordinary discourse about the six senses.


## 10\. The eye is there, the world is there, and yet the familiar experience is absent

The Buddha gives a short instruction.

The monks should understand a sphere in which the eye ceases and the perception of visible forms fades.

Likewise for hearing and sounds.

Smell and odours.

Taste and flavours.

Touch and tangible objects.

Even mind and mental objects.

Then the Buddha leaves.

The monks are baffled.

A familiar situation.

The Buddha has delivered something extraordinarily concise, then walked away before anybody has managed to turn it into a comfortable philosophical system.

So they approach Ananda.

He explains that the instruction concerns the cessation of the six sense bases.

The monks report his interpretation to the Buddha.

And the Buddha confirms it.

Nanananda regards this as direct canonical evidence for describing Nibbana through the cessation of the six sense spheres. The accompanying notes compare Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation and the Chinese parallel SA 211, which preserves the reference to their cessation.

Now here's the difficulty.

We established that an arahant can still see and hear.

But here Nibbana is being described through the cessation of the very structures that make seeing and hearing possible.

Is this a contradiction?

Not necessarily.

But we must distinguish **three different claims**\.

First, a living arahant can function through the ordinary senses without the defilements of greed, hatred and delusion.

Second, Nanananda argues that a distinctive liberating concentration involves the cessation of ordinary sense-sphere experience.

Third, at the final death of an arahant, the conditioned aggregates cease without renewed rebirth.

These are related claims.

They are not identical descriptions of the same everyday condition.

And this distinction is essential.

Otherwise somebody hears that Nibbana is the cessation of the six sense bases and concludes that awakened people must be permanently blind, deaf and unconscious.

Which would be a rather peculiar form of spiritual excellence.

Nanananda's argument is that the fruition concentration provides a distinctive realisation of cessation, while the living arahant can subsequently function through the senses without renewed bondage.

But the exact interpretation of the extraordinary attainment remains disputed.

The standard Theravada explanation distinguishes Nibbana as the object of supramundane consciousness from the ordinary operation of the sense faculties.

Nanananda gives much greater prominence to a special experience of cessation in which the familiar perceptual framework is no longer established.

**Both acknowledge living liberation. They differ over how the relevant passages should be understood.**

And Nanananda now attacks what he sees as the most serious consequence of interpreting Nibbana as a separate metaphysical destination.

## 11\. The place where there is no sun, moon, earth, water, fire or air

Now comes one of the most famous descriptions of Nibbana in the early Buddhist texts.

The Buddha speaks of a sphere where the four great elements are not found.

Nor the familiar formless attainments.

Nor this world or another.

Nor the sun or moon.

There is no coming or going, no remaining, no arising or passing away in the ordinary sense.

The description ends by identifying this with the ending of suffering.

This is *Udana* 8.1.

And Nanananda thinks the passage has been profoundly misunderstood.

Imagine a metaphysician hearing this.

"No earth?"

"No water?"

"No sun?"

"No moon?"

"NO COMING OR GOING?"

They immediately begin assembling a cosmic travel brochure.

**NIBBANA: THE ULTIMATE DESTINATION.**

Facilities include\:

- Absolutely no weather.
- No material elements.
- No celestial bodies.
- No arrivals or departures.
- Zero customer complaints.

An extraordinary place.

Although, admittedly, one with a rather uncertain tourism infrastructure.

Nanananda objects that such a reading has begun to transform cessation into another cosmic location.

The Buddha's description, he argues, concerns a realisation of Nibbana here and now, especially as disclosed in the extraordinary concentration we've been discussing.

The world of ordinary distinctions no longer establishes itself through the usual perceptual supports.

That, he thinks, explains why the passage negates so many familiar categories.

### Why the disagreement matters

The classical Theravada interpretation does not literally require Nibbana to be a geographical place somewhere in the cosmos.

It treats Nibbana as an unconditioned element, distinct from conditioned phenomena and not locatable through ordinary spatial categories.

Nanananda nevertheless objects that interpreting the passage as referring to an independently existing unconditioned element can encourage substantialising Nibbana.

He wants the language of cessation to retain its full force.

**Nibbana isn't another kind of becoming that happens to lack material furniture.**

That is the strength of his criticism.

But his conclusion is not the only coherent interpretation of the text.

The fact that the passage denies ordinary categories does not by itself establish that it refers specifically to arahant-fruition concentration rather than to Nibbana as the unconditioned.

Those are competing exegetical claims.

And one mustn't confuse this with a scientific statement about a dimension beyond the universe.

The early Buddhist issue concerns liberation, experience and the ending of suffering-not the discovery of a previously unknown physical region.

### The obvious objection

Fine.

Suppose Nanananda is right that the extraordinary attainment involves the cessation of ordinary perceptual supports.

But what about the person who comes out of that attainment?

Their body is still alive.

Their eyes still work.

The world still appears.

Have they come back from Nibbana?

And if so, wasn't the liberation temporary?

Nanananda's answer depends upon a crucial distinction between **a temporary meditative experience and the irreversible ending of the defilements**\.

The fruition attainment can be entered and left.

But the underlying liberation is not reversed when ordinary perception resumes.

That's the point he is trying to establish.

And now his discussion takes a particularly practical turn.


## 12\. The final enemy is not sensory experience. It's “I-making” and “mine-making.”

Nanananda brings in a discourse where the Buddha instructs Sariputta in the ending of personal appropriation.

The training concerns the body, external objects and the deeply ingrained tendencies through which these are treated as *I* and *mine*\.

It culminates in liberation of mind and liberation through wisdom.

The Chinese parallel, SA 982, confirms the central emphasis on eliminating the constructions of self and ownership in relation to internal and external experience. Analayo's notes also provide Bodhi's corresponding translation.

Now this is the philosophical key.

Ordinarily, you assume liberation requires escaping the world.

But the Buddha identifies a more precise problem.

The mind has constructed a relationship of independent ownership.

This body is mine.

These feelings are mine.

These thoughts are mine.

These experiences belong to me.

And the resulting identity becomes the basis of craving.

So the training is directed towards the ending of that appropriation.

Not merely the temporary absence of external objects.

Not simply an exotic state of consciousness.

**Freedom from the world as possession is not the same thing as physical removal from the world.**

And notice how this resolves the problem of ordinary activity.

You can use the body without imagining a permanent owner behind it.

You can recognise objects without treating their possession as existential security.

You can speak without making every statement an expression of personal conceit.

And you can even meditate without using the attainment to establish a superior spiritual identity.

But here's an extraordinary twist.

Nanananda now describes a concentration which becomes stable precisely because it is no longer being forcibly maintained.

## 13\. The spinning top that stays upright only because it cannot stop moving

Imagine a spinning top.

It's standing upright.

Remarkably stable.

Perfectly balanced.

And you might think its stability is an inherent property.

But what happens when it slows down?

It begins wobbling.

Eventually it falls.

The apparent stability depended upon continuous motion.

Nanananda uses this to distinguish ordinary concentration maintained through effort from the steadiness associated with liberation.

A meditator may achieve extraordinary tranquillity through sustained control.

Attention is continually corrected.

Distraction is restrained.

The mind is kept upon its object.

This can be immensely useful.

But the state remains dependent upon supporting activities.

When those conditions weaken, the concentration can deteriorate.

Now imagine another kind of steadiness.

Not a top balanced through continuous spinning.

But a stability associated with the ending of the disturbance itself.

Nanananda uses this to explain a discourse in which concentration is described as neither leaning forward nor backward, nor being maintained through forceful suppression.

Instead, freedom produces steadiness.

Steadiness produces contentment.

And contentment removes agitation.

**The mind is no longer stable because it's winning a fight. It's stable because the relevant fight has ended.**

That's an extraordinary distinction.

### Why calm isn't necessarily liberation

Suppose you're sitting in meditation.

The mind is quiet.

You're pleased.

Then a noise interrupts you.

Immediately\:

"FOR GOODNESS' SAKE."

"WHY CAN'T PEOPLE RESPECT MY PRACTICE?"

"THIS WAS SUPPOSED TO BE MY MOMENT OF TRANSCENDENT PEACE."

What happened?

Your tranquillity depended upon the environment remaining agreeable.

And the moment the environment changed, the dependence was exposed.

The state was genuinely pleasant.

It may even have involved valuable concentration.

But it wasn't necessarily the unshakeable freedom described by the Buddhist path.

Now consider the alternative.

An arahant need not maintain a psychological fortress against every possible disturbance.

The underlying greed, hatred and delusion have been uprooted.

That's why liberation isn't simply an exceptionally successful effort to suppress reactions.

It is freedom from the causes that would otherwise produce them.

### Analayo's important interpretive correction

Here the comparative notes preserve a particularly consequential disagreement.

The relevant discourse describes a concentration connected with final knowledge.

But the wording permits two interpretations.

Does final knowledge **produce** this concentration?

Or does the concentration **lead to** final knowledge?

Nanananda emphasises its association with fruition attainment.

Bhikkhu Bodhi argues that the context and related discourses support reading the concentration as something that has final knowledge as its fruit.

The Chinese parallel SA 557 also places the teaching within a discussion of signless concentration.

This matters enormously.

Because the direction of the relationship changes.

Under one reading, the concentration is especially characteristic of completed liberation.

Under another, it is an important meditative condition contributing to liberation.

Those aren't equivalent.

And we shouldn't simply merge them into one sweeping claim.

The more defensible common point is this\:

**The highest Buddhist understanding of concentration is inseparable from freedom from grasping and the cultivation or realisation of wisdom.**

It is not measured solely by how completely the mind can suppress ordinary perceptions.

But now Nanananda reaches his next paradox.

If the arahant has reached the farther shore, why can we still see them walking around on this one?

And this is where the whole discussion becomes delightfully absurd.


# Sermon 18 - The monk who crossed the river and somehow kept eating breakfast

## 14\. You cannot cross the river twice. But apparently you haven't crossed it once either.

Nanananda opens Sermon 18 with a wonderful paradox from the *Nalaka Sutta*\.

The teaching describes different paths of practice, then makes two apparently contradictory statements about reaching the farther shore.

One says the crossing is not made twice.

The other says it shouldn't be reckoned as a single crossing either.

Now what the heck is going on?

Suppose somebody tells you\:

"I crossed the river. But I didn't cross it twice."

Fine.

You crossed once.

Then they add\:

"Nor should you say I crossed once."

Mate.

Either you've crossed the river or you've been taking some extremely interesting mushrooms.

But Nanananda thinks this paradox exposes a fundamental misunderstanding.

We imagine the farther shore as a location.

The arahant travels there.

Then stays there.

But the living arahant can apparently return to ordinary sensory activity.

They walk.

Eat.

Speak.

Teach.

So did they come back?

Have they crossed the river and then returned to the original bank?

Does liberation repeatedly switch on and off?

Nanananda argues that the entire difficulty arises from treating the metaphor as though it described physical travel.

### The difference between crossing and possessing a destination

Imagine an ordinary journey.

You leave one town.

Travel.

Arrive somewhere else.

Now you possess a new location.

You can distinguish your departure point from your destination.

But if liberation consists in ending the grasping that makes conditioned experience into a possessive world, why imagine the result as another place which the liberated person now owns?

That would reproduce the same structure.

"I was over there."

"Now I'm over here."

"This is my liberated territory."

"Those unenlightened sods are still on the other bank."

Congratulations.

You've invented spiritual nationalism.

Nanananda proposes something subtler.

The language of crossing is useful for someone who remains bound.

It directs them towards the possibility of freedom.

But when the relevant bondage has ended, the contrast between the shores need no longer be appropriated as an identity.

The arahant isn't a permanent citizen of Nibbana Land.

Nor do they become spiritually unliberated whenever the ordinary senses function.

**The crossing describes liberation from a relationship of bondage, not the permanent relocation of an independent self.**

And this is the answer to our chapter's central question.

Experience can continue without the old appropriation.

But there's an important textual qualification.

### Analayo's alternative evidence

The accompanying comparative materials include Bhikkhu Bodhi's alternative translation of the *Nalaka Sutta* verse.

That translation can be understood as distinguishing different ways of approaching the goal, rather than asserting the exact paradox of neither crossing once nor twice.

In other words, Nanananda's argument depends in part upon a particular interpretation of a difficult verse.

The philosophical problem he raises remains important.

But his reading cannot be treated as the uncontested meaning of the canonical passage.

And that distinction matters because Nanananda is about to build an entire explanation of living liberation around the metaphor.

Fortunately, he has a considerably more straightforward canonical image.

One of the most beautiful in all of Buddhism.


## 15\. The lotus that lives in water without becoming wet

Picture a lotus leaf.



Water falls upon it.

The drop remains.

You can see it.

It moves across the surface.

But it does not soak into the leaf as it would into ordinary absorbent material.

The drop is present without the usual adhesion.

Nanananda draws on several early Buddhist passages comparing the liberated sage to a lotus untouched by the water or mud in which it grows.

This is not merely a decorative symbol of purity.

He uses it to explain the precise relationship between the arahant and ordinary experience.

The arahant lives in the world.

Experiences occur.

The senses function.

But the mind no longer becomes psychologically bound through greed, hatred and delusion.

**The water touches the leaf without soaking into it.**

Now think about the difference between these two things\:

A pleasant experience occurs.

And\:

*I must have more of this.*

Or\:

An unpleasant experience occurs.

And\:

*This must not be happening to me.*

The second involves an additional movement of appropriation.

The mind takes the occurrence and makes it a condition of its imagined owner's well-being.

Nanananda argues that the arahant is free from this entanglement.

### The Buddha can taste his breakfast

Now we reach one of the most useful canonical observations in the entire series.

A brahmin youth named Uttara closely observes the Buddha.

He wants to determine whether the extraordinary claims made about the Buddha's conduct are accurate.

Among his observations is something very simple.

The Buddha eats.

He tastes his food.

But he does not become attached to the taste.

Nanananda uses the passage from the *Brahmayu Sutta*, MN 91, to illustrate living liberation.

This is absolutely magnificent.

Because people imagine enlightenment must involve some impossible transformation of ordinary sensory life.

Perhaps the Buddha eats a mango.

And there's no taste.

Or the mango tastes like transcendental emptiness.

Or the Buddha's consciousness leaves his body and merges with the Infinite Fruit Absolute.

No.

The mango can taste like a mango.

The mouth works.

Taste occurs.

The Buddha recognises what he is eating.

**What doesn't arise is the compulsive appropriation of taste through craving.**

Now the difference becomes beautifully clear.

Pleasure is not the same thing as attachment to pleasure.

Pain is not identical to hatred of pain.

A perception is not the same thing as conceit about the person who perceives.

And thinking is not identical to the delusion of an independent thinker.

These distinctions make possible the doctrine of living liberation.

The arahant does not need to destroy the senses.

The defilements are what have ceased.

### The sensory world no longer overwhelms the mind

Nanananda then brings in a discourse from AN 9.26.

It describes a monk whose mind has been fully liberated.

Many sights may appear.

Sounds may be heard.

Smells, tastes, bodily sensations and mental objects may enter awareness.

Yet these experiences do not overwhelm the mind.

The monk recognises their passing away without becoming entangled.

This is not a description of someone who has become insensitive to the world.

It is a description of someone who is not psychologically conquered by what appears.

Now imagine someone receiving praise.

The words are understood.

The praise may be appropriate.

But the experience doesn't need to become a vast personal identity.

You don't need to spend the next six hours admiring the magnificent person who was praised.

Likewise, somebody criticises you.

The criticism is understood.

Perhaps there's something to learn.

Perhaps it's nonsense.

But the event doesn't need to become an existential crisis.

This is not a recommendation to be indifferent to injustice or incapable of defending yourself.

It is freedom from the additional compulsive identification that makes every encounter into an assault upon, or affirmation of, an imaginary permanent owner.

**The arahant can meet the world without needing the world to certify their existence.**

And now Nanananda takes the argument one step further.

If the farther shore is itself only a useful concept, perhaps even that must eventually be relinquished.


## 16\. The man who crossed the river and then tried to marry his raft

Imagine standing beside a flooded river.

You need to cross.

There's no bridge.

No ferry.

So you gather branches.

Twigs.

Leaves.

Grass.

You construct a raft.

Then you paddle across.

Wonderful.

The raft has served its purpose.

Now imagine arriving on the opposite bank and announcing\:

"This raft has saved my life."

"Therefore I must never abandon it."

"I shall carry it everywhere."

"It is the supreme raft."

"I shall organise my entire identity around being the possessor of this raft."

Then you strap the enormous thing to your back and spend the rest of your existence struggling under its weight.

A slightly inconvenient interpretation of successful river crossing.

The Buddha's raft simile from the *Alagaddupama Sutta*, MN 22, explains that the teaching is for crossing over, not for grasping.

Nanananda applies the same principle even to the concept of the farther shore.

This is extraordinary.

Because it means the teaching itself must not become a possession.

You can use Buddhist concepts.

Practise the path.

Develop insight.

Realise liberation.

But that does not require the perpetuation of an identity organised around possessing the teaching.

Even the distinction between ordinary existence and liberation must not become another basis for conceit.

### The final trap: becoming the owner of transcendence

Imagine an arahant who has realised the cessation of grasping.

Now suppose the mind announces\:

"Excellent."

"I have transcended the world."

"I am now established on the farther shore."

"This is MY enlightened state."

The whole machinery would have restarted.

There's an owner.

A possession.

An identity.

Something to defend.

Something to distinguish oneself from other people.

But if the relevant defilements have genuinely ceased, that appropriation does not occur.

Nanananda calls this **non-identification**\.

The arahant doesn't take even liberation as a new personal territory.

And the practical significance is enormous.

**The path aims at freedom from grasping, not the acquisition of the ultimate thing to grasp.**

That's why the raft must be put down.

Not because the raft was useless.

It was indispensable.

But its purpose was to enable crossing.

It wasn't meant to become a lifelong burden.

### The snake that leaves both shores behind

Nanananda now invokes the *Uraga Sutta*, whose verses repeatedly compare the liberated monk to a snake shedding its old skin.

The image is beautiful.

The snake grows.

Its old skin becomes inadequate.

Eventually it sheds it.

It doesn't carry the discarded skin around in a little suitcase.

It doesn't frame it.

It doesn't form an emotional attachment to being the extraordinary snake that has transcended its previous skin.

It leaves it.

Nanananda connects the simile with relinquishing both the near and farther shores-the distinctions through which the worldling continues constructing opposing standpoints.

The snake is not annihilated.

The old skin has served its function.

And the snake is no longer confined within it.

Likewise, the teaching is not attempting to destroy ordinary functioning.

It aims at the ending of the constructions through which experience becomes bondage.

And now the question becomes considerably more uncomfortable.

**If an arahant is already completely liberated while alive, what exactly happens when the arahant dies?**

Does Nibbana become greater?

Do they acquire a second portion of liberation?

Does something migrate into an unconditioned realm?

Or does the distinction between living liberation and final cessation concern something more precise?

Nanananda now tackles one of the most controversial doctrines in the history of Theravada Buddhism.


## 17\. Two Nibbanas? Has Buddhism invented a premium upgrade?

The *Nibbanadhatu Sutta*, Itivuttaka 44, describes two Nibbana elements.

One with a remainder.

One without a remainder.

Now imagine somebody hearing this.

"Ah. Two forms of enlightenment\!"

"Presumably the first is the standard package."

"And the second is the premium version unlocked after death."

"Does that include lifetime access to infinite consciousness?"

No.

We need to be much more precise.

The first description concerns a living arahant.

The defilements have been extinguished.

The task is complete.

But the five sense faculties remain intact.

The liberated person can still experience pleasant and unpleasant things.

The second concerns the complete cooling and cessation of the remaining experienced processes without renewed becoming.

Nanananda devotes the second half of Sermon 18 to examining this distinction.

### With a remainder

Imagine a fire which has stopped receiving fuel.

The flames have subsided.

But the physical process hasn't yet entirely exhausted itself.

There may still be embers.

Nanananda uses this image for the living arahant.

Greed, hatred and delusion have been destroyed.

The defilements no longer sustain renewed becoming.

But the organism remains alive.

The senses continue functioning.

The body is still vulnerable.

It requires food.

It can experience pain.

It will eventually die.

And yet the liberation is already complete.

This is the essential distinction.

**The remainder is not a leftover supply of greed, hatred and delusion waiting to be removed after death.**

The relevant defilements have already been eradicated.

What remains is the conditioned organism and its capacity for sensory experience.

This is why translations such as *Nibbana with residual clinging* can be misleading when read without explanation.

They may suggest the arahant still psychologically grasps at existence.

That would contradict the claim of completed liberation.

The traditional explanation ordinarily takes the remainder as the continuing aggregates or other conditioned supports, not an unremoved stock of the defilements that constitute bondage.

Nanananda himself connects the remainder with the body inherited from previous conditions, although his terminology occasionally becomes ambiguous.

And this allows us to answer the chapter's central question with greater precision.

A living arahant has experience.

But the experience doesn't create the same renewed bondage.

The fire no longer receives the fuel that would perpetuate it.

### Without a remainder

Eventually the body dies.

The sense faculties cease functioning.

The aggregates no longer continue in that organism.

And within the Buddhist account, there is no renewed rebirth because the causes responsible for renewed becoming have been eradicated.

The important point is that **death does not have to finish removing defilements that arahantship failed to remove**\.

The liberation has already been realised.

The distinction concerns the continuing conditioned life of the organism.

And here's where Nanananda challenges the classical commentarial interpretation.

He rejects the idea that the arahant's death involves arriving in an independently existing unconditioned destination.

He insists that Nibbana is the cessation of becoming, not a place where a liberated consciousness subsequently resides.

### Is there anything experienced after death?

This requires exceptionally careful language.

Within the early Buddhist teaching, final Nibbana is not described as the continuing private experience of an eternal individual.

But neither should we casually translate it into the annihilation of a substantial self.

Why?

Because the independently existing self whose destruction is being imagined was not established in the first place.

The Buddha repeatedly rejects attempts to force the liberated Tathagata into the ordinary categories of existence and non-existence after death.

Nanananda's emphasis is that renewed becoming ceases because its conditions have ended.

And the question of where the liberated person has gone is liable to reproduce the assumptions that the teaching questions.

So there is no reason to posit a permanent consciousness travelling into a new cosmic realm.

But there is also no basis for confidently describing what final Nibbana is like *for a surviving experiencer*\.

That presupposes the very subject whose independent existence has been under investigation.

**The ending of bondage does not need to create a new owner of the ending.**

And now comes the image that makes the entire distinction beautifully intelligible.


## 18\. The fire is out, but the embers remain

Nanananda draws upon a canonical fire simile found in AN 6.43 and in the verses of Udayi.

A great fire is burning.

Then the fuel supply stops.

The fire subsides.

Embers remain for a time.

Eventually they cool.

He uses this to illustrate the distinction between living liberation and the ending of the remaining conditioned life at death.

Now think about the difference between the two processes.

The fire has stopped receiving the fuel required to perpetuate it.

But the physical consequences of its previous burning can continue briefly.

Likewise, the arahant's organism continues to function because of conditions already established.

The body needs food.

The senses operate.

Pleasant and painful experiences remain possible.

But the underlying craving that sustains renewed becoming has been eradicated.

At death, the remaining conditioned organism ceases.

No further birth occurs.

**The end of craving and the end of the organism are not the same event.**

This distinction matters tremendously.

Because otherwise someone imagines that Nibbana must involve either the permanent suspension of consciousness or the immediate death of the body.

But the early Buddhist descriptions of living arahants make both conclusions untenable.

The Buddha himself lived and taught for decades after awakening.

The physical organism continued.

The liberation was already accomplished.

And this gives us one of the most precise answers to the entire series.

**Nibbana can be fully realised while the conditioned processes of bodily life continue.**

### Analayo's textual complication: were the embers actually still burning?

Now here's a particularly useful example of why comparative scholarship matters.

The fire verse has different manuscript readings.

One refers to embers.

Another refers to the calming of conditioned formations.

Nanananda prefers the embers reading because it fits his interpretation of living liberation.

But Analayo's notes preserve Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation, which also follows the embers reading while interpreting the imagery as the coals having gone out.

The materials include a related Chinese parallel in which the fire ceases because no fuel remains.

So the canonical imagery supports the relationship between fuel and extinguishing, but the precise nuance of **smouldering embers versus already extinguished coals** is not philologically uncontested.

That means Nanananda's analogy is illuminating without every detail being beyond dispute.

And his broader argument doesn't depend entirely upon the exact state of the embers.

The two Nibbana elements already distinguish the living arahant, whose sense faculties remain, from final cessation.

The crucial doctrinal fact is the ending of greed, hatred and delusion while the organism still lives.

We can preserve that without pretending a disputed fire verse supplies an unanswerable proof of every aspect of Nanananda's metaphysics.

### What Nanananda adds about extraordinary death

Near the end of Sermon 18, Nanananda mentions the traditional story of Dabba Mallaputta, who is said to have employed extraordinary meditative powers at his death, with his body consumed by fire.

This is a scriptural extraordinary-power narrative, not an independently established account of physical physiology or a practical model for approaching death.

Nanananda uses it to reinforce his claim that the liberated person has already relinquished identification with the body.

But we don't need to treat the miraculous detail as verified fact to understand the philosophical argument.

The decisive issue is the exhaustion of craving, not whether somebody can produce supernatural combustion.

And that brings us back to ordinary experience.

The arahant doesn't have to wait until death to become free from identification.

The body remains vulnerable.

Its final cessation is inevitable.

But the bondage created through grasping at it can already have ended.


## 19\. The final practical experiment: What would experience be without ownership?

Imagine sitting quietly with a cup of tea.

The warmth reaches your hands.

Aroma appears.

Taste arises.

Pleasure occurs.

Now watch how quickly the mind can begin constructing a personal world.

"This is lovely."

"I should buy more."

"I wonder where I can find this in India."

"Perhaps I should start importing tea."

"I could build a wonderful little brand."

"People would love it."

"This could become an entirely new direction for my life."

Within forty seconds, a cup of tea has become an international commercial enterprise.

The mind is an absolute flipping marvel.

And none of those ideas is necessarily wrong.

You might genuinely be able to build a successful tea business.

The problem isn't planning.

It's whether the mind begins treating its plans as the conditions of personal completion.

Now imagine another possibility.

The tea is tasted.

The pleasure is known.

The mind recognises its changing character.

There is no demand that the experience continue forever.

No requirement that it prove anything about who you are.

No need to turn it into a possession.

No psychological catastrophe when the cup is empty.

**The experience occurs without becoming a burden.**

That's the everyday analogy.

But don't confuse it with the full liberation of arahantship.

A moment of simple enjoyment doesn't prove that greed, hatred and delusion have been eradicated.

It merely helps illuminate the distinction Nanananda is making.

The arahant's freedom is supposed to be complete at the level of the underlying defilements.

Not simply a temporary capacity to remain relaxed while drinking tea.

### The three levels of our investigation

We've now encountered three different matters.

**Ordinary experience without immediate grasping.** This is something a practitioner may begin cultivating through attention, restraint and wisdom.

**The irreversible destruction of the defilements.** This is what arahantship claims to accomplish.

**The extraordinary fruition concentration discussed by Nanananda.** This is his interpretation of a group of difficult discourses concerning cessation, perception and concentration. Its precise relationship to other meditative states remains disputed.

These are related.

But they are not interchangeable.

And that is why we shouldn't say that every moment without obvious self-consciousness is Nibbana.

Nor that every state of sensory cessation is liberation.

Nor that liberation requires the permanent elimination of sensory consciousness.

The criterion is the ending of the causes of bondage.

And now we can state the central argument of these sermons without metaphysical confusion.

**The arahant can continue functioning because the senses are not themselves the fundamental problem. The problem is the ignorance and craving through which conditioned experience becomes appropriated and renewed becoming is sustained.**

When those causes cease, the person is liberated.

The organism can continue until its remaining conditions are exhausted.

And at death, there is no renewed becoming.

That is the core of the Buddhist claim.

Nanananda adds his distinctive interpretation of fruition concentration as a special realisation of cessation.

Analayo's comparative materials help us distinguish the canonical evidence from the more controversial conclusions drawn from it.

And the result is much sharper than simply saying\:

"Enlightenment means living in the present."

It means something far more radical.

**The mind's bondage to experience can cease without the immediate destruction of the living organism.**


# The final cut - The world doesn't have to disappear for you to be free of it

Three sermons.

One question.

And an answer that completely changes what liberation means.

**Sermon 16 asks whether the mind can attain a concentration that no longer depends upon the usual objects of perception.**

Nanananda argues that it can.

He connects the extraordinary descriptions of object-independent concentration with arahantship and the realisation of cessation.

But he also makes a more fundamental point.

Genuine relinquishment happens through wisdom.

The mind understands the nature of its possessions so profoundly that the compulsive need to grasp them can end.

The banknotes become worthless.

The dream ends.

The cinema lights come on.

The constructed world loses its hold.

**Sermon 17 asks how cessation is realised and why the mind cannot simply escape into a subtler form of existence.**

The practitioner might transcend ordinary bodily perception.

Enter the formless states.

Experience extraordinary tranquillity.

But none of those achievements automatically establishes liberation.

The relevant issue is the ending of ignorance and craving.

Nanananda investigates a special concentration associated with cessation, which he interprets as the fruition of arahantship.

And the comparative evidence reveals important ambiguities concerning whether certain passages describe the culmination of liberation or meditative conditions contributing to it.

**Sermon 18 asks how the liberated person can remain in the world after reaching the farther shore.**

And this is the decisive answer.

The arahant isn't required to disappear.

The senses can operate.

The person can walk.

Eat.

Teach.

Speak.

Recognise things.

But these activities no longer sustain the old bondage.

Like a lotus leaf touched by water without being soaked, the liberated mind encounters experience without the compulsive appropriation that previously held it captive.

Then, when the body dies, there is no renewed becoming.

Not because an eternal soul has finally travelled to a superior location.

Not because the body has been magically transformed into something immortal.

But because the conditions supporting the continuation of bondage have ended.

Now return to the question we began with.

**Can experience continue without someone owning it?**

The Buddhist answer challenges the assumption that experience requires an independently existing owner in the first place.

The living arahant continues functioning conventionally.

But the delusion of permanent independent ownership has ceased.

And the extraordinary implication is this\:

**You don't need to become the ultimate possessor of experience in order to be free. The compulsion to possess experience is itself what must end.**

That's the reversal.

You began looking for a consciousness that could own everything.

A permanent witness.

An unchanging experiencer.

Something invulnerable to the transformations of the world.

But Nanananda has been directing the investigation towards a different possibility.

Maybe freedom isn't the discovery of a sufficiently powerful owner.

Maybe it is the ending of the conditions through which ownership becomes bondage.

And when this becomes clear, the whole project of spiritual practice changes.

You're not trying to construct a more magnificent self.

You're not trying to acquire the perfect meditation state.

You're not trying to obtain permanent ownership of Nibbana.

You're cultivating the understanding through which the compulsive structure of acquisition can cease.

And then even the concept of the farther shore can be put down.

The raft has served its purpose.

The old skin is shed.

The water touches the lotus without binding it.

The fire has stopped consuming fuel.

The organism continues until its conditions are exhausted.

And the liberated mind no longer needs the universe to provide a permanent answer to the question\:

**"But what about ME?"**

Because the question no longer holds the mind captive.


## The question Chapter VII must answer

We've now established something extraordinary.

Nibbana is not simply the finest possible pleasure.

It isn't an endless state of sensory delight.

It doesn't require preserving a permanent experiencer.

And Nanananda has described an extraordinary realisation of cessation where even familiar perceptual structures no longer find a footing.

But this creates an enormous problem.

The Buddha repeatedly calls Nibbana the supreme happiness.

So what the heck does *happiness* mean when craving has ended?

How can something be blissful if it isn't a pleasant feeling?

Does the liberated person lose all ordinary enjoyment?

And why should anybody actually want the ending of the very processes through which pleasure occurs?

**If Nibbana isn't the greatest possible pleasurable experience, why is it described as the greatest possible happiness?**

That is Chapter VII.

And it takes us straight into one of the deepest puzzles of early Buddhist philosophy.

Perhaps happiness is not ultimately measured by how much pleasure consciousness manages to acquire.

Perhaps the most profound peace is precisely **the ending of the need to acquire it**\.
