# Nibbana - The Mind Stilled

Chapter II of X · Sermons 4-6 · Bhikkhu Katukurunde Nanananda, with Analayo's comparative commentary

## If Nibbana is release, why the heck don't I see it right now?

**The short answer:** Because you are not merely experiencing things. You are continually *taking a position* in relation to them.

You encounter something pleasant and lean towards it.

Something unpleasant and lean away.

Something confusing and demand an explanation.

Something uncertain and demand a guarantee.

Then, because the mind has spent its entire life organising experience around these movements, you mistake the activity for the basic condition of being conscious.

And when somebody suggests that the activity could cease, you imagine that consciousness itself must disappear.

That's the mistake Nanananda begins dismantling in Sermon 4.

**The difficulty isn't simply that Nibbana is hidden. It's that the mind keeps recreating precisely the conditions that prevent it from understanding cessation.**

But be precise. This doesn't mean an ordinary pause between thoughts is already full Nibbana. Nanananda is describing a much deeper uprooting of ignorance and craving.

The remarkable point is that this uprooting begins by understanding something immediately available: the way the mind depends, clings, and constructs its experience.


## 1. You've mistaken leaning for standing

Sermon 4 - Dependency, instability, and the impossible effort to preserve what changes

Imagine you are standing upright.

But suppose you aren't really standing. You're leaning heavily against a wall.

So long as the wall remains where you expect, everything seems perfectly stable.

Now somebody shifts the wall.

You fall over.

And immediately announce:

**"Something has disturbed my peace!"**

Well, yes.

But was the wall actually responsible for providing you with peace?

Or had you confused dependence with stability?

That is Nanananda's starting diagnosis.

### The hidden instability of dependence

He brings forward a striking instruction from the Buddha's discourses.

Someone who depends upon a support is liable to waver. Someone who no longer depends upon it is not shaken in that way.

From non-wavering follows calm; from calm, the ending of inclination; and eventually the ending of the whole movement of coming and going.

Nanananda reads this not merely as an instruction to become emotionally resilient, but as a remarkably compressed description of liberation itself.

Think carefully about what this means.

Your ordinary conception of peace is conditional:

*I will be peaceful when the circumstances stop disturbing me.*

So you organise your life to eliminate the disturbances.

You avoid difficult people.

You acquire money to protect yourself against uncertainty.

You develop an identity which you hope other people will respect.

You may even practise meditation to acquire a state that nobody can disturb.

None of these activities is automatically wrong. Money provides genuine security. Boundaries protect people. Concentration has real value.

The problem is the expectation that something dependent on conditions can guarantee unconditional peace.

Suppose you finally get everything arranged.

You have the perfect house.

The perfect routine.

The perfect relationship.

Your health is excellent.

The mind announces that it has finally achieved stability.

But what has actually happened?

**You have constructed an extraordinarily elaborate arrangement of things that must not change.**

Congratulations.

You've built a five-star prison with excellent interior design.

Every success introduces another possible loss.

And the more absolutely you identify your well-being with maintaining the arrangement, the more vulnerable you become to change.

The prison isn't the house or the relationship.

It's the demand that these things secure you permanently.

### The important distinction: pain versus bondage

We must avoid a common misunderstanding.

Buddhism does not claim an awakened person becomes impervious to pain.

An arahant still inhabits an aging body. Other people can act cruelly. The world is not transformed into an endless sequence of agreeable sensations.

The claim is more exact.

**Painful experience need not generate the additional bondage of craving, resistance and identification.**

There is a difference between bodily pain and the compulsive psychological insistence that its occurrence makes your existence unacceptable.

And another difference between intelligently responding to pain and being possessed by your resistance to it.

If the body hurts, seek treatment.

If someone abuses you, protect yourself.

The instruction is not passive submission.

It is freedom from the compulsive appropriation of experience.

And this leads to a strange conclusion.

The problem is not always that experience is disturbing.

Sometimes **the mind is disturbed because it has made an absolute demand upon experience**.

### Why every ordinary victory is temporary

Nanananda now makes a particularly cutting move.

Birth and aging are not two independent commodities that can be purchased separately.

You cannot demand the first while indefinitely rejecting the second.

Aging is implicit in being born.

Likewise, whatever arises through conditions is vulnerable to the alteration or disappearance of those conditions.

You can't insist on the arising while simultaneously demanding immunity from cessation.

Nanananda illustrates this using coupled animals pulling against each other, and then the absurd story of a miser desperately trying to separate rice cakes that have become joined together.

The man wants the cake without the inconvenient consequence.

The world refuses to cooperate.

He pulls harder.

The cake remains attached.

He becomes furious.

The cake remains attached.

Eventually the solution is to stop trying to obtain the impossible separation.

Now apply this to the mind.

"I want youth, but not aging."

"I want love, but never vulnerability."

"I want achievement, but no possibility of failure."

"I want existence, but none of the conditions inherent in existing."

These aren't merely unfortunate ambitions.

At their most absolute, they're internally contradictory.

**You want conditioned existence without the conditionality.**

And the more energetically you pursue that impossible arrangement, the more suffering you manufacture.

So what does the Buddha propose?

Not a cleverer means of pulling the cakes apart.

Letting go of the demand.

### But doesn't that mean giving up on life?

No.

It means giving up the *impossible bargain* you're trying to make with life.

You can love somebody without requiring that their existence guarantee permanent happiness.

You can enjoy beauty without insisting that beauty remain.

You can pursue knowledge without making your worth depend on knowing everything.

You can strive to relieve suffering without believing the universe must submit to your preferences.

The distinction is not between activity and inactivity.

It's between intelligent engagement and compulsive appropriation.

Nanananda makes this point through another memorable image: **Mara, the personification of bondage, is defeated when there is nothing left for him to seize.**

In a tug-of-war, the opponent pulls with all his strength.

You release the rope.

His entire strategy collapses.

Not because you pulled harder.

Because you've stopped participating in the mechanism that gave his pulling power over you.

This is an illustration of liberation, not a recommendation to abandon responsibility or practical action.

And it changes the way we understand spiritual progress.

Maybe awakening isn't about becoming the most powerful participant in the struggle.

Maybe it's about seeing through the necessity of struggling for possession in the first place.

### The first real answer

So why don't you see Nibbana right now?

Because the mind is accustomed to experiencing itself through dependence.

It doesn't simply see a condition arise.

It takes a position.

*For me.*

*Against me.*

*Mine.*

*Not mine.*

*Something I must keep.*

*Something I must get rid of.*

And this activity produces the very instability from which you're trying to escape.

But a deeper objection immediately arises.

**If every experience appears within this system of conditioned relations, how can we investigate it without adopting yet another position?**

Surely even trying to let go becomes a kind of grasping.

Nanananda knows.

And this is where his teaching becomes much more sophisticated than merely telling people to relax.


## 2. The razor that gets sharper when you stop strangling it

Sermon 5 - How to investigate experience without making another prison

Nanananda has a wonderfully practical answer.

Imagine you are sharpening a razor.

You hold the blade.

You press it against the sharpening stone.

But you're so desperately concerned about doing it correctly that your entire hand stiffens.

You grip the thing like an absolute lunatic.

The pressure becomes uneven.

The movement becomes clumsy.

The blade gets worse.

You've become so attached to sharpening the razor that you're preventing the razor from being sharpened.

Now release the unnecessary tension.

Hold it steadily but lightly.

Allow the movement to occur with precision.

Suddenly it works.

This is Nanananda's analogy for the distinction between **grasping at experience and examining experience wisely**.

And it has enormous consequences for meditation.

### The meditator's most embarrassing mistake

You're sitting.

You've been instructed to observe the mind.

A thought arises.

You notice it.

Excellent.

Then you think:

*I'm noticing the thought.*

Then:

*Was that genuine mindfulness?*

Then:

*I should be less identified with thinking.*

Then:

*Am I now identified with not being identified?*

Then:

*Perhaps I've attained a subtle state of nondual awareness.*

Then:

*What would Analayo say about this?*

Then:

*blast. I've been thinking for ten minutes.*

You've taken a practice designed to reveal the workings of attachment and turned it into a sophisticated attachment-production facility.

The entire thing is being run by the very mechanism you're attempting to understand.

But here's the subtlety.

The answer isn't to stop noticing thoughts.

It's to notice the additional *grasping* involved in your reaction to noticing thoughts.

**A thought can be known without being converted into a problem for the person who knows it.**

A feeling can arise without your immediately declaring what it means about your spiritual development.

Attention can be directed intelligently without the demand that every moment produce a particular result.

That distinction is the practical heart of Sermon 5.

### Why the Buddha gives you concepts and then tells you to abandon them

Nanananda now introduces the analogy of scaffolding.

You want to construct a building.

So you erect scaffolding.

Perfectly reasonable.

The scaffolding provides temporary support.

It enables the construction to proceed.

But imagine the building is completed and you refuse to dismantle the scaffolding.

Why?

Because you're terribly attached to the scaffolding.

You have spent years studying the scaffolding.

You know the precise dimensions of every tube.

You can recite the entire scaffolding manufacturer's catalogue.

And some poor sod comes along and says:

"The building is finished."

You reply:

"Yes, but according to the seventeenth-century commentarial tradition, this scaffolding is extremely important."

Mate.

**The scaffolding was never the building.**

This is Nanananda's explanation of the role of concepts in insight meditation.

The Buddha provides ways of examining body, feelings, mind, and mental phenomena.

They are useful distinctions.

They direct attention.

They help reveal arising and ceasing.

But they're not four permanent substances that you must now spend the rest of eternity worshipping.

They're instruments.

When knowledge has accomplished its task, the instrument need not be possessed as an identity.

And this exposes the difference between learning about Buddhism and understanding what Buddhist practice is intended to do.

You can become enormously learned.

You can memorise the discourses.

You can develop an extraordinarily sophisticated understanding of dependent arising.

You can win arguments against every other Buddhist school.

And still be completely enslaved to your own opinions.

Because every time somebody challenges your view, the mind announces:

*They are attacking ME.*

The conceptual scaffolding has become your personal fortress.

You are no longer using the teaching to examine attachment.

You are using attachment to defend the teaching.

An exquisite reversal.

### But don't you need effort to become liberated?

Absolutely.

This is why the razor is such a good analogy.

A relaxed grip isn't an absence of skill.

Nor is it a refusal to make an effort.

Sharpening the razor still requires practice, attentiveness, appropriate pressure and intelligent adjustment.

The difference is that **effort does not require psychological rigidity**.

You can practise diligently without treating every meditation session as an examination of your worth.

You can develop concentration without demanding that the mind become permanently quiet.

You can investigate craving without hating yourself whenever craving appears.

In fact, self-hatred is an extraordinarily inefficient way of overcoming hatred.

A remarkably common spiritual strategy, nevertheless.

"I SHALL DESTROY MY AVERSION BY BEING FURIOUS WITH MYSELF FOR HAVING AVERSION."

Brilliant.

The Buddha would presumably have some questions about that business model.

The relevant distinction is between cultivating conditions conducive to insight and clinging to the results of cultivation.

The path has a direction.

It does not require turning every experience into a possession.

### What do we actually observe?

Nanananda directs attention towards something easily overlooked.

The objects of mindfulness are not simply independent things waiting for a detached observer to catalogue them.

They arise and are known through conditions.

Consider feelings.

A painful feeling arises dependent on contact.

A pleasant feeling arises dependent on contact.

The feeling is not a permanent property belonging to an eternal owner.

It is an event in a conditioned process.

Likewise, what you attend to becomes the object of your present investigation.

That does not mean you create the entire physical universe merely by paying attention.

It means the world *as presently attended to, recognised and appropriated* is inseparable from the conditions of experience.

That's a very different claim.

And it becomes especially interesting when you examine your own body.

Ordinarily, you don't merely experience bodily sensations.

You experience **my body**.

My appearance.

My strength.

My health.

My decline.

My future.

A tremendous architecture of identification is built around a collection of changing processes.

And because you regard that architecture as yourself, changes within it become personal threats.

Someone insults your appearance.

A few words reach your ears.

Perception identifies their meaning.

Feelings arise.

Memories and expectations become involved.

And suddenly your entire identity is preparing for war.

All this from a moment of contact.

Astonishing efficiency.

But what if the process were understood without immediately converting it into an absolute statement about *me*?

That is where mindfulness becomes more than ordinary self-observation.

It begins undermining the conditions that produce identification.

### The arahant's strange freedom

Nanananda then confronts some apparently contradictory descriptions of liberated people.

An arahant is said to be beyond both taking up and rejecting.

What on earth does that mean?

Surely liberation involves rejecting craving.

Does the liberated person then spend eternity rejecting things?

No.

That's the whole point.

A person who has relinquished a burden doesn't need to continue performing the act of relinquishment forever.

Imagine you're holding a heavy suitcase.

You put it down.

The burden is gone.

Now someone tells you:

"Very good. You must continue putting it down."

How?

It's already on the floor.

You're no longer carrying it.

This is why the canonical descriptions can sound paradoxical.

The liberated person is not incessantly performing the psychological action of abandoning every possible object.

The compulsive need to acquire or reject has ended.

Nanananda illustrates this using a snake shedding its skin. The skin may remain visible, but the snake is no longer bound within it. Likewise, an arahant can continue using the senses and functioning in the world without the earlier bondage of appropriation.

Analayo's buddhistinquiry.org help here by setting Nanananda's translations beside other scholarly renderings, including Bhikkhu Bodhi's. The key distinction remains the same: **having completed an abandonment is not equivalent to endlessly trying to abandon something.**

And this answers our earlier problem of wanting Nibbana.

The desire to become free can function as part of the path.

But when liberation is complete, there is no need to preserve the identity of *someone who is continually trying to become liberated*.

Even the project of liberation has fulfilled its purpose.

So why don't we recognise this immediately?

Because another mechanism is operating beneath our deliberate intentions.

And Nanananda now turns towards something more disturbing.


## 3. The cinema in which you are both audience and accomplice

This is my favourite movement of Sermon 5.

Nanananda asks us to think about watching a film.

You enter the cinema.

You know perfectly well that you're about to watch actors performing fictional events.

You sit down.

The lights go out.

And twenty minutes later, you're furious because some fictional sod has betrayed another fictional person.

Your heart is racing.

You want revenge.

You may even cry when a character dies.

Now step outside the cinema for a moment.

What actually happened?

Light struck a screen.

Sound reached your ears.

You recognised faces and events.

Your mind connected successive scenes into a continuous story.

And you became emotionally invested in the world constructed through that process.

The film works precisely because the audience participates.

The audience doesn't have to consciously believe the actors are real people undergoing those events.

It simply enters imaginatively into the narrative.

Nanananda takes this familiar experience and makes a radical comparison.

**Ordinary experience also involves an enormous amount of construction that we habitually fail to recognise as construction.**

His central insight is that the spectator isn't merely receiving a finished show. The spectator contributes the conditions that turn sensory events into a meaningful drama.

### The audience brings its own glue

Take an ordinary relationship.

Someone sends you a message:

"Okay."

That's it.

Four letters.

You look at them.

And suddenly the cinematic machinery starts.

*Why did they just say okay?*

*Are they annoyed?*

*Have I done something?*

*They used to be more affectionate.*

*Something has changed.*

*Perhaps they're going to leave.*

Within thirty seconds, you've produced an entire psychological thriller.

You may even be preparing the heartbreaking final scene.

All from "Okay."

And here's the nasty part.

The imagined narrative produces genuine emotional consequences.

Your heart races.

Your stomach contracts.

You feel rejected.

Then you send an anxious reply.

That reply changes the interaction.

Your interpretation has begun influencing the situation it supposedly merely described.

This is not just a thought floating harmlessly in your head.

It's part of an ongoing process that conditions further action and experience.

But notice the distinction.

The message was actually received.

The emotional reaction actually occurred.

What is questionable is the additional interpretation that transforms incomplete information into a compelling story about yourself.

**The illusion isn't that nothing happened. The illusion is that the mind's constructed interpretation possesses the unquestionable reality you attribute to it.**

And that brings us to the really uncomfortable question.

### Who is making the film?

Ordinarily, you think:

"I am watching my experience."

Which makes it sound as though there's a stable observer receiving events from outside the process.

But the observer is already participating.

Your interests determine what you notice.

Your fears influence what appears threatening.

Your expectations organise ambiguous information.

Your memories provide the characters and storylines.

Your craving supplies the investment.

So the apparently passive observer is not entirely passive.

It is implicated in the production of the experienced narrative.

Nanananda uses the analogy of the cinema to explain how ignorance and craving allow an apparently continuous experiential world to be constructed. He particularly emphasises how the audience contributes to the impression of an independently real drama.

And now comes one of the most beautiful images in the series.

### Somebody opens the cinema doors

You're deeply absorbed in the movie.

The villain is approaching.

The hero is about to die.

The entire audience is transfixed.

Then somebody throws open the doors.

Sunlight floods the room.

The image loses its vividness.

You notice the screen.

You notice the projector.

You notice the people sitting beside you.

The compelling fictional world suddenly loses its hold.

Has the film been destroyed?

No.

Has the screen ceased to exist?

No.

Have you become unconscious?

Obviously not.

**You've recognised the conditions that made the illusion compelling.**

This is Nanananda's analogy for disenchantment.

The film loses its power because the supporting conditions have changed.

The experience that previously absorbed you is understood differently.

And that's exactly why the contemplation of Nibbana can be transformative.

You don't have to manufacture a competing fantasy of perfect spiritual peace.

You need to understand what is presently happening.

The process of fabrication can become less compelling when its nature is recognised.

### But don't I still enjoy the movie after understanding it?

Of course.

And this is where we should distinguish the analogy from a universal claim about artistic enjoyment.

You can knowingly enjoy fiction.

You can participate in stories without being deceived into believing they're literally occurring.

Likewise, understanding dependent experience does not logically require losing the ability to enjoy music, friendship or ordinary perception.

Nanananda's target is not imaginative activity as such.

It is the ignorance and craving through which conditioned experience becomes a basis for bondage.

A liberated person is not required to lose the capacity to recognise a story.

The issue is whether a story continues to generate delusive appropriation.

That is the crucial distinction.

**Knowing something is constructed changes your relationship to it. It need not eliminate the appearance.**

And this produces the next question.

If understanding the machinery weakens the illusion, why does the machinery keep rebuilding itself?

Why isn't one moment of clarity enough?

Nanananda's answer is that ignorance isn't merely an incorrect proposition that can be crossed out of a notebook.

It also operates through deeply established tendencies.


## 4. The mind is intoxicated by its own productions

Nanananda now discusses what Buddhist texts call the *influxes* or *taints*.

Forget the technical vocabulary.

Think of them as deeply established tendencies that keep influencing the mind, particularly around sensual attraction, becoming and ignorance.

They condition how experience is interpreted and what the mind becomes invested in.

Nanananda compares them to fermentation.

A small remainder of ferment can help set a fresh batch going.

Likewise, an underlying tendency can become active when the appropriate conditions appear.

Imagine you've spent years trying to impress people.

You've developed good manners.

You're apparently confident.

You meditate regularly.

You tell yourself that you've moved beyond external validation.

Then somebody you admire dismisses your work.

And suddenly:

*HOW DARE THEY.*

The old machinery comes roaring back.

Not because you've consciously decided to manufacture insecurity.

The tendency was never fully uprooted.

A condition appeared.

The process resumed.

So there's an important difference between **temporarily not experiencing craving** and **having eliminated the conditions that allow craving to reappear**.

You can be calm because nothing has recently provoked you.

You can be calm because you've temporarily suppressed a reaction.

Or you can possess a degree of wisdom that changes the way the reaction arises and is understood.

These are not equivalent achievements.

### Why deep calm isn't necessarily liberation

This is the reason that simply making the mind quiet cannot, by itself, establish Nibbana.

A person might attain extraordinary concentration.

Thought becomes subtle.

Bodily awareness recedes.

The usual sense of identity becomes attenuated.

Wonderful.

But the question remains:

**Has ignorance actually been uprooted?**

Or has its ordinary activity merely been suspended?

These are different things.

A sufficiently quiet mind may cease displaying an obvious disturbance without understanding the assumptions that support it.

And this creates an extraordinary spiritual trap.

The mind becomes so quiet that you assume you've discovered ultimate reality.

Then the conditions supporting the state change.

Ordinary experience returns.

And you begin craving the return of the extraordinary experience.

You've used meditation to manufacture yet another source of dissatisfaction.

What appeared to be liberation was a refined form of dependence.

That doesn't mean concentration is useless.

Far from it.

Concentration provides stability for the development of insight.

But Nanananda insists that wisdom, rather than tranquillity alone, is decisive.

We now have the second answer to the chapter's central question:

**You do not see Nibbana merely because the mind is quiet. You must understand the ignorance that makes even extraordinary states into objects of appropriation.**

And once that is understood, the question becomes more radical.

What exactly is the mind *constructing*?


## 5. The five things you call yourself are a magic trick

Sermon 6 - The production of experience, and the illusion of an independent owner

Nanananda now turns to one of the Buddha's most devastating collections of analogies.

The five aggregates - the principal categories into which ordinary personal experience is analysed - are compared to phenomena that appear more substantial than they are.

| **Body/form** | Foam - apparent solidity without the compact substance we imagine |
|--------|--------|
| **Feeling** | A bubble - arising and vanishing |
| **Perception** | A mirage - compelling appearance that invites mistaken interpretation |
| **Volitional formations** | A banana trunk - layers without a solid heartwood |
| **Consciousness** | A magic show - an appearance whose apparent nature deceives |

These come from the canonical *Phenapindupama Sutta*, SN 22.95, which Nanananda discusses in Sermon 6. Analayo's lecture notes also give Bhikkhu Bodhi's translation alongside the discussion.

Now pay attention to the philosophical precision.

The Buddha isn't saying a body is literally identical to foam.

Nor is he saying that consciousness doesn't occur.

The point is that when you investigate these processes, you don't discover the permanent independent essence that ordinary attachment imputes to them.

And these are the processes out of which you construct your notion of being a person.

So what happens when you investigate the person?

You discover changing phenomena.

But the mind keeps insisting that somewhere behind them there must be an independent owner.

### The owner is inferred from the activity

Consider a whirlpool again.

It has a recognisable shape.

It possesses a centre.

Things are drawn into it.

It exhibits a characteristic pattern of movement.

You can point to it.

You can discuss it.

You can predict aspects of its behaviour.

But none of that proves there's a separate whirlpool-substance hiding beneath the moving water.

The vortex is a dependent pattern.

Likewise, you can speak meaningfully of a person.

The person has memories.

Characteristics.

Responsibilities.

Relationships.

A continuous conventional identity.

But Nanananda argues that the existence of these organised processes doesn't establish an independent self behind them.

**Activity does not require an eternally self-existing actor.**

And yet we instinctively assume one.

There's thinking.

Therefore there must be a thinker-substance.

There's feeling.

Therefore there must be a feeling-owner.

There's consciousness.

Therefore there must be a permanent conscious subject.

But that inference is precisely what Buddhist analysis challenges.

The presence of a process does not automatically justify the additional metaphysical entity we imagine standing behind it.

That's the philosophical cut.

### The factory that produces its own manager

Picture an enormous factory.

Hundreds of interdependent processes operate within it.

Machines regulate other machines.

Information passes between departments.

Signals cause adjustments.

Outputs become inputs.

Everything is functioning through a network of conditions.

Now imagine somebody walks inside and asks:

"Where is the single permanent manager that independently performs every operation?"

They inspect the machinery.

They investigate the control systems.

They examine the relationships.

And whenever another process is discovered, they announce:

"That's all very interesting. But surely there must be a separate manager *behind* the process."

The argument has become circular.

They assume such a manager must exist and interpret every discovery as further evidence that the manager remains hidden.

This is not to deny ordinary agency or moral responsibility.

It's to question whether agency entails a metaphysically independent owner.

Nanananda's analogy of the body as a workshop is aimed at this mistaken assumption of a compact unit controlling the whole process.

And the same tendency occurs in meditation.

You notice a thought.

Then assume there's an independent observer who stands behind the thought.

You notice awareness.

Then imagine an ultimate witness standing behind awareness.

You become conscious of your sense of self.

Then postulate a deeper self which must be conscious of the first self.

An endless series of increasingly spiritual managers.

The chief executive officer of consciousness has apparently gone into hiding.

But why assume there has to be one?

### Wait. Doesn't Buddhism still teach consciousness?

Absolutely.

This is a crucial distinction.

Rejecting an independent permanent self is not equivalent to denying conscious experience.

The early Buddhist discourses analyse consciousness as dependently arisen.

For example, visual consciousness depends upon the eye and visible forms.

Contact depends on the relevant conjunction of conditions.

Feeling arises dependent upon contact.

These analyses examine how conscious experience occurs without treating consciousness as an independent, unconditioned soul.

But Nanananda wants to take the argument further.

He isn't simply describing how individual sensations arise.

He's asking how the entire apparent structure of a personal world is maintained.

And once again the cinema becomes useful.

### The mind puts the frames together

A film appears continuous.

Yet it consists of distinguishable frames displayed in succession.

The spectator integrates them into movement, narrative and emotional meaning.

Nanananda applies this analogy to our tendency to organise experience into an apparently continuous personal drama.

The present moment is interpreted through what has just happened.

What has just happened creates expectations of what will happen next.

Those expectations influence what the present moment means.

And craving helps bind the scenes into an ongoing story about becoming.

Suppose you wake in the morning feeling slightly uncomfortable.

Immediately the previous day enters.

Yesterday's unfinished work.

The argument you had.

The money you need.

The journey you're planning.

The person you want to become.

Suddenly the discomfort is not merely discomfort.

It's a chapter in the unfolding saga of *your life*.

And with the saga comes the protagonist.

The person who must succeed.

The person who must overcome the past.

The person who must become something in the future.

The construction is extraordinarily convincing because you have been participating in it for as long as you can remember.

But Nanananda wants you to examine its dependence on conditions.

The personal narrative is useful at a conventional level.

Yet its practical usefulness doesn't establish an independent self outside the very processes by which the narrative is produced.

### The dog who starts a fight with himself

And now Nanananda delivers an absolutely magnificent simile.

A dog crosses a plank above some water.

It looks down.

There is another dog looking up.

The dog becomes interested.

It wags its tail.

Perhaps the reflected dog is friendly.

Then it becomes suspicious.

It growls.

And the dog in the water growls back.

The sod.

An escalation begins.

And the extraordinary thing is that the dog believes its behaviour is responding to another animal.

But the supposed opponent is partly a product of the dog's own unrecognised participation in the situation.

Every growl generates another growl.

Every gesture meets its reflection.

The dog thinks:

"THIS THIEF KEEPS COPYING ME."

And so the cycle continues.

Nanananda uses this image to illustrate the deceptive relationship between the apparent object and the perceiving activity.

Now bring the analogy into human experience.

You feel insecure.

You interpret somebody's neutral expression as hostility.

You become defensive.

They notice your defensiveness.

Their behaviour changes.

You interpret their changed behaviour as proof of your original suspicion.

The whole system feeds itself.

You think you're discovering the world as it independently exists.

But your own reactions are helping maintain the situation you're interpreting.

This doesn't mean other people are literally projections of your mind, or that external threats aren't real.

The lesson is narrower and more important:

**You can become trapped by processes that your own unexamined participation helps perpetuate.**

And this takes us directly towards Nibbana.


## 6. Why recognising construction begins to undo it

Let's return to your original insight from Chapter I.

Setting the mind towards Nibbana is different from setting it towards an ordinary desirable object.

Why?

Because the understanding of Nibbana points towards the ending of grasping itself.

Now Sermons 4-6 have given that insight a mechanism.

The mind ordinarily takes positions in relation to experience.

Those positions become supports.

Supports become objects of attachment.

Attachment sustains the ongoing construction of a personal world.

Ignorance allows the whole process to be mistaken for something ultimately self-existing.

And because the process is not recognised as dependent, its products appear to demand further acquisition, resistance and identification.

The system renews itself.

So what changes when its conditioned nature is understood?

**The mind begins losing the conviction that it must continue doing what it has always done.**

It can encounter a feeling without automatically constructing an identity around it.

It can recognise an intention without taking the intention as proof of a permanent controller.

It can see a pleasant experience without demanding its repetition.

It can acknowledge painful experience without converting it into an absolute verdict on existence.

And it can even recognise the arising of craving as a conditioned event, rather than treating craving's demands as commands that must be obeyed.

This is not full liberation merely because it happens once.

It's a direction of practice.

And the aim is not to mechanically suppress every conditioned activity.

As Nanananda explains in Sermon 6, merely quieting bodily and mental activities does not necessarily remove ignorance. He distinguishes calming these activities from understanding their conditioned character through wisdom.

That difference is everything.

### The danger of trying to manufacture emptiness

Suppose you hear that the aggregates lack a permanent essence.

You decide:

"Right. I must now experience everything as unreal."

You sit down.

You stare at sensations.

You try to dissolve your body into emptiness.

You try to make your thoughts disappear.

You try to prevent identification.

And whenever ordinary perception returns, you become frustrated.

You have turned the teaching on non-attachment into a new attachment to an unusual state.

This is why the correct understanding of Nibbana matters so much.

**The target is not the destruction of ordinary appearance. It is the ending of ignorance and craving.**

Nanananda's language is sometimes exceptionally radical, particularly when he describes the experiential world as constructed or illusory.

Read carefully, those descriptions concern the delusion of independent substantiality and the dependent production of experience.

They shouldn't be simplified into the claim that nothing exists, nothing matters, or wisdom consists in perceiving a blank void.

Indeed, Sermon 4 invokes the Buddha's middle way between dogmatic existence and non-existence.

Things arise dependent on conditions.

Things cease dependent on conditions.

And understanding that process makes the extremes inadequate.

### One important qualification from Analayo

The textual comparisons in Analayo's lecture materials make an important contribution here.

They show that explanations of formations, perception, intention and the relationship between ignorance and its underlying tendencies are not expressed identically across every surviving early Buddhist recension.

For example, the published notes for Sermon 6 compare the Pali analysis of bodily, verbal and mental formations with a Tibetan parallel, while noting that a corresponding Chinese discourse does not preserve the same threefold definition.

Similarly, the notes for Sermon 5 identify differences between the Pali *Sammaditthi Sutta* and Chinese/Sanskrit parallels regarding the conditions for ignorance.

That matters because Nanananda is presenting an interpretive argument, not simply reporting that every Buddhist source contains an identical metaphysical system.

His insight into the construction of experience can be profound without requiring every one of his exegetical claims to be beyond dispute.

And that is precisely how we'll approach the rest of the series.

The philosophical argument should be tested, not merely admired.


## 7. The final trap: turning liberation into an eternal witness

Now we reach the end of Sermon 6.

And Nanananda opens a door which will lead directly into Chapter III.

If ordinary consciousness arises dependent upon conditions, what exactly happens when the conditions for delusive consciousness cease?

Does consciousness simply disappear?

Does some deeper consciousness remain?

Is there an unconditioned awareness that does not depend upon name-and-form?

Could Nibbana be identical to that awareness?

This is where the Buddhist discussion becomes extraordinarily delicate.

Nanananda introduces a passage from the *Kevaddha Sutta*, DN 11, concerning consciousness that is described as non-manifestative.

But observe how the passage is introduced.

A monk has been trying to discover where the four great elements cease.

He travels through various heavenly realms.

He questions increasingly exalted beings.

Even the highest gods cannot supply the answer.

Eventually he returns to the Buddha.

And the Buddha's first move is not to give him the geographical coordinates of an ultimate reality.

**He corrects the question.**

The monk had been asking where the elements cease.

The Buddha reformulates the enquiry in terms of where the elements do not find a footing.

Nanananda treats that distinction as philosophically decisive.

Think about it.

The original question imagines liberation as a location.

There must be somewhere the troublesome features of conditioned reality disappear.

Perhaps a transcendent realm.

Perhaps a pure consciousness dimension.

Perhaps the universe's secret basement.

You simply have to find it.

And the Buddha challenges the premise.

Perhaps the question isn't where experience goes.

Perhaps it concerns **the conditions under which the ordinary structures of experience are established at all**.

Nanananda will spend substantial time investigating that possibility in subsequent sermons.

But we mustn't rush his conclusion.

The passage has multiple competing interpretations in Buddhist scholarship, and its use of the word *consciousness* is genuinely difficult. It cannot simply be equated with an eternal Self, universal consciousness or the absolute awareness of Kashmir Saivism.

Nor should we confidently declare that Nanananda has proven the opposite before examining his argument.

That is exactly the problem he is preparing us to investigate.

And this time, rather than beginning with a declaration about what consciousness ultimately is, we'll follow the mechanism that allows an experienced world to appear.


## The final cut - Why don't I see Nibbana right now?

Let's put the entire chapter together.

You ask:

"If Nibbana is release from grasping, and grasping is something the mind is doing, why can't I just stop?"

Because grasping isn't merely an explicit thought that you can choose to dismiss.

It is supported by ignorance, habits of attention, feeling, intention, identification and deeply established tendencies.

You don't simply think *I am a self* and then consciously decide to cling to that proposition.

You experience the world through patterns that already make the self seem obvious.

And those patterns are reinforced whenever experience becomes something to possess, defend, resist or become.

So you cannot eliminate the problem merely by issuing another command from inside the very structure you're trying to understand.

"I COMMAND THE SELF TO STOP EXISTING."

Who exactly is issuing that command?

And what is the command attempting to achieve?

You've simply given the imaginary manager another assignment.

Instead, Nanananda directs us towards insight.

Understanding the conditionality of experience.

Recognising the support upon which the mind leans.

Seeing the difference between using concepts and being imprisoned by them.

Examining how intentions, perceptions, feelings and craving contribute to the construction of experience.

And understanding that the supposed owner of this entire process is itself something to investigate.

**You don't have to destroy experience. You have to stop being deceived about what experience requires and what it can provide.**

The process of release is not completed by acquiring the perfect object of awareness.

It's completed through the uprooting of the ignorance and craving that sustain bondage.

And now something extraordinary happens.

What initially sounded like a mysterious spiritual goal becomes a specific, intelligible task.

Not easy.

Not instantaneous.

But intelligible.

You are trying to understand a process so completely that you no longer compulsively reproduce the conditions of suffering.

That's why contemplating Nibbana matters.

It points the mind towards the ending of the very activity through which the mind remains bound.

But we've reached a much more dangerous question.

If the mind is participating in the construction of its experienced world, then what is consciousness actually doing?

Is there a world appearing *to* consciousness?

Or is the apparent separation between consciousness and its world itself part of what needs explaining?

And when the entire ordinary structure is understood, **is there anything we could meaningfully call consciousness that doesn't depend on that structure?**

That is the enquiry for Chapter III.

Not the usual spiritual fantasy of discovering an eternal witness and declaring victory.

We're going to investigate what consciousness is, how it acquires an object, and why Nanananda thinks the Buddha's account overturns some of our most basic assumptions about reality.
